This Week in Democracy: A Court Reshapes the Presidency, Even as It Holds a Few Lines
Amid record immigration arrests, a $2.2 billion presidential income disclosure, and a hijacked 250th anniversary, the Supreme Court ended a 90-year check on executive power
```markdown
This was a week in which the Supreme Court redrew the boundaries of presidential power — expanding it dramatically in one ruling, constraining it in the next — leaving behind a record that resists any single interpretation. It was also a week of record immigration arrests, a $2.2 billion disclosure of the president's personal income, and a congressional report accusing the administration of hijacking the nation's 250th anniversary for corrupt and ideological ends. Beneath the institutional drama, protest and dissent continued to assert a different account of the country's civic life.
The weekend set the tone. On Saturday, the Supreme Court paused lower-court rulings and allowed the administration to terminate Temporary Protected Status for roughly 350,000 Haitians and thousands of Syrians, exposing them to deportation despite State Department warnings that conditions in Haiti remain unsafe. DHS Secretary Mullin and adviser Stephen Miller defended the decision; Ohio's Republican governor publicly broke with the administration over it. At the same time, Trump escalated military strikes on Iranian sites and posted explicit threats that the Islamic Republic "will no longer exist" if diplomacy collapsed further — rhetoric that bypassed the ordinary process of congressional consultation on military action. Nationwide, the 50501 coalition, Women's March, and Next250 organized large demonstrations offering a fuller account of American history, in sharp contrast to a sparsely attended official National Mall fair that Fox News nonetheless described as a success. Speaker Mike Johnson, addressing a conservative conference, warned that a Democratic majority would turn House committees into investigative bodies targeting the president's family, Cabinet, and donors — while promising he personally runs a "protection program" for allies. The weekend also saw a Mississippi police response that deployed teargas against protesters seeking justice for a one-year-old shot and killed by an officer, with authorities withholding body-camera footage, and a mistrial in the federal arson case tied to the deadly 2025 Palisades fire, after a jury deadlocked 10-2 for acquittal.
By Monday, the Supreme Court delivered the week's most structurally significant ruling. In Trump v. Slaughter, a 6-3 majority overturned Humphrey's Executor, the 1935 precedent that had shielded independent agency heads from at-will presidential removal. The decision means the president may now fire leaders of the FTC, NLRB, and roughly two dozen other independent bodies without cause, eliminating a foundational separation between politically insulated regulators and the executive branch. Trump publicly celebrated the ruling as a major expansion of his authority. Yet the same day produced a narrower, more protective outcome: a 5-4 ruling kept Federal Reserve Governor Lisa Cook in her position, finding that Trump had failed to provide required notice before attempting to remove her over unproven mortgage-fraud allegations. The ruling preserved the Fed's independence for now, though it rested on procedural grounds rather than a broader principle, leaving that independence exposed to future challenge under the newly expanded removal doctrine. Separately, the Court held that geofence location-data warrants require Fourth Amendment protections and upheld Mississippi's mail-ballot postmark rule against a Republican National Committee challenge, protecting ballot-counting procedures used in more than a dozen states.
Away from the Court, Senator Bill Cassidy publicly accused Trump of treating Congress as an "appendage," describing a heated confrontation over a war-powers resolution on Iran in which the administration had failed to brief lawmakers. Trump also declined to commit to signing a bipartisan housing bill that had passed the House 358-32, calling it "a big yawn" unless Congress advanced a voter ID measure he favored, and toured a public D.C. golf course with Interior Secretary Doug Burgum to plan a luxury renovation later reported to include removing mature cherry tree groves. Separately, the administration invoked emergency authority to restart a California oil pipeline shuttered since a 2015 spill, prompting a lawsuit from the state's attorney general.
Tuesday brought another major and mixed judicial record. The Court narrowly upheld birthright citizenship, ruling that children born in the United States to undocumented or temporary-status parents remain citizens under the 14th Amendment and rejecting Trump's executive order attempting to redefine the constitutional phrase "subject to the jurisdiction." The margin was thin, and four justices signaled openness to revisiting the doctrine in the future, with Justice Kavanaugh's concurrence leaving room for legislative narrowing later. On the same day, a 6-3 majority upheld state bans on transgender athletes in girls' sports, allowing West Virginia and Idaho to exclude transgender girls from female school sports and rejecting Title IX challenges — a decision expected to embolden similar bans in roughly 25 other states. The Court also struck down limits on coordinated party campaign spending in a case brought by Vice President Vance and Republican officials, removing a safeguard at a moment when the Republican National Committee holds a substantial financial advantage over its Democratic counterpart.
Elsewhere, the Department of Health and Human Services restructured its civil rights office to elevate religious-conscience protections above other enforcement priorities, a move legal experts warned could allow providers to refuse care related to reproductive health, LGBTQ services, and vaccines. The Washington Post also revealed a $500 million no-bid contract for renovation work tied to the White House, the first thread in what would become a week-long story about presidential enrichment. Meanwhile, Speaker Johnson sent the House home without passing the defense authorization bill after a member blocked the floor rule pending unrelated voter ID provisions, and reporting surfaced that the White House had floated mass pardons — a "250 pardons for 250 years" initiative — as a symbolic centerpiece for Independence Day.
Wednesday deepened several of the week's central threads. The Court weakened the Voting Rights Act, giving southern legislatures greater latitude to redraw congressional districts previously protected against racial gerrymandering — a decision experts said would likely reduce Black congressional representation ahead of the midterms. The Justice Department, separately, refused to comply with a federal judge's order to produce Epstein-related documents by a July 2 deadline, citing its own interpretation of statutory redaction requirements — a direct instance of executive-branch resistance to judicial authority. Trump also announced acceptance of a $400 million Boeing 747-8 from Qatar; after bipartisan criticism, the Pentagon formally accepted the aircraft, with plans reported to eventually transfer it to Trump's presidential library.
Immigration enforcement escalated sharply, with roughly 10,000 arrests recorded over five days as officials pursued a directive of 2,000 arrests daily, even as a federal appeals court limited detention without bond hearings to 90 days. A federal judge separately blocked a USPS plan that would have denied mail-ballot delivery to states refusing to share voter rolls, the second judicial defeat for administration efforts to narrow mail voting. Florida's state education board voted to bar undocumented students from all 28 state-funded colleges and universities, and nearly 500,000 New Yorkers lost health coverage as federal Medicaid cuts and expired ACA subsidies took effect.
The day's most consequential emerging story, though, was the first wave of reporting on Trump's annual financial disclosure — a 927-page filing showing $2.2 billion in 2025 earnings, including $1.4 billion from cryptocurrency ventures and $636 million from a memecoin, alongside 22,000 individual stock trades. Additional reporting detailed $8.9 billion in federal funding flowing to mining companies linked to Trump and Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick, an undisclosed $50 million loan, and stock purchases made the day before a market-moving tariff announcement. An Air Force officer, Major Jason Watson, was arrested at the Capitol for standing in uniform with a sign reading "Impeach. Convict. Remove," and now faces an internal military investigation.
Thursday carried the citizenship ruling into enforcement. One day after the Court's decision, Acting Attorney General Todd Blanche directed prosecutors to pursue fraud cases targeting so-called "birth tourism," despite acknowledging the practice accounts for less than 1 percent of U.S. births — using selective prosecution to narrow, in practice, a right the Court had just upheld in principle. An appeals court separately ruled the administration need not restore historical materials on climate change, immigration, and slavery removed from national park sites, permitting long-term alteration of public historical memory. Further reporting connected Trump's sons to at least ten military-contractor investments that had received $3.7 billion in federal funding, even as the Senate rejected a crypto conflict-of-interest amendment along party lines. And a House Democratic subcommittee released a report titled "From Vanity to Insanity," alleging wire fraud, pay-to-play arrangements, and the diversion of nonpartisan 250th-anniversary funds into a shadow entity called Freedom 250 that promoted Christian nationalist messaging while harvesting voter data — a story with roots stretching back to the weekend's contested commemorations.
The week closed on Friday with an unusual legal collision in Louisiana, where a New Orleans grand jury indicted Republican Attorney General Liz Murrill on 16 counts related to alleged intimidation of city officials. The state supreme court quickly stayed the indictment after finding the trial judge had sealed the courtroom in violation of open-court law, a closure during which journalists were briefly detained. House Democrats formally released the full "From Vanity to Insanity" report the same day, alongside reporting on a $68 million taxpayer-funded contract awarded to an allied firm for Independence Day festivities. Legal experts, including Representative Jamie Raskin, outlined plans for expanded oversight of alleged administration corruption should Democrats retake the House majority in the midterms. New York City Mayor Zohran Mamdani delivered a widely covered Independence Day address framing American identity around inclusion and dissent, directly countering official anniversary messaging. And Trump, in a smaller coda, publicly criticized NATO allies over defense spending as "not reciprocal," despite recent allied increases nearing $1 trillion combined. Notably, the acute Iran conflict that dominated the weekend faded from direct coverage as the week progressed, surfacing only in secondary references to oil markets and economic messaging — a shift in attention rather than a resolution.
Taken as a whole, the week offers a genuinely mixed record of institutional behavior rather than a single trend line. The Supreme Court, in the space of two days, both dismantled a 90-year check on presidential removal power and refused to let the executive redefine constitutional citizenship by decree; it weakened voting-rights protections for minority communities while preserving mail-ballot access and digital privacy safeguards. Enforcement agencies moved swiftly to narrow the practical reach of rulings that didn't go the administration's way, even as courts imposed some procedural limits on detention. Financial disclosures and family business dealings drew bipartisan scrutiny without yet producing consequence, and a sitting attorney general's indictment collapsed amid procedural irregularities of its own. What connects these threads is not a single verdict on the health of American self-government, but a documented pattern: guardrails holding in some places, eroding in others, and the outcome of that contest left, for now, unresolved.
```
