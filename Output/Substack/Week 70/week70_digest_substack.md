# The Slush Fund Week

*A nearly $2 billion settlement for the president and his allies triggers a rare Republican revolt, as border deaths, detention abuses, and monument-building continue in the background*

![Header image](week70-digest-featured.jpg)

```markdown
This was the week a lawsuit the president filed against his own government quietly became a constitutional problem for his own party. It began on Monday with a routine-sounding announcement and ended on Friday with Senate Republicans burying their own immigration bill rather than let Democrats force a vote on it. In between, courts pushed back hard against prosecutorial abuse, Congress twice flinched from a war-powers vote it seemed poised to win, and the border and detention system kept producing casualties that no single week's news cycle could contain.

**The Weekend**

The weekend set a grim tone before the fund story even existed. Six migrants, including a 14-year-old, died of heat exposure in a sealed railcar near Laredo — a tragedy tied to a "prevention through deterrence" strategy that pushes border crossers into deadly terrain, compounded by a former Border Patrol agent's claim that agents are trained to sabotage water stations left by humanitarian groups. At the same time, ICE dropped its degree requirements and age caps while dangling $50,000 signing bonuses, raising alarms about who exactly would be given detention and deportation authority. In Colorado, the governor, under sustained pressure from Trump, commuted the sentence of Tina Peters, convicted of tampering with voting machines — a decision state election officials called a dangerous precedent. A Long Island Rail Road strike stranded 250,000 commuters, thousands rallied in Selma and Montgomery for voting rights following the Supreme Court's rollback of key protections, and reports surfaced of DHS agents intimidating journalists covering ICE operations.

**Monday**

By Monday, the week's defining story had begun, though it did not yet look like one. Trump and his sons dismissed, with prejudice, a $10 billion lawsuit they had filed against the IRS. In its place, the Justice Department announced a $1.776 billion fund — quickly dubbed the "Anti-Weaponization Fund" — drawn from the government's Judgment Fund and overseen by a five-member commission the president could remove at will. Reporters noted almost immediately that the fund's structure could allow it to compensate January 6 defendants. House Democrats filed an amicus brief calling it an unprecedented specter of corruption. On its first day, it was already more than a settlement.

Monday carried other weight too. A California state report on seven ICE detention facilities documented medical neglect linked to four deaths, contaminated food and water, overcrowding at 125 percent of capacity, and degrading strip-search practices — a systemic, state-verified record of abuse. A Brookings study estimated that more than 145,000 U.S. citizen children had lost all co-resident parents to immigration detention since January, with DHS's own figures judged a substantial undercount. A Third Circuit ruling narrowed judicial review for detained activist Mahmoud Khalil, blocking First Amendment retaliation claims from federal court until deportation proceedings concluded. In South Carolina, Republicans opened a special session to redraw the state's only Black-majority congressional district, acting on Trump's urging after the Supreme Court weakened Voting Rights Act protections. And on the National Mall, an eight-hour taxpayer-funded evangelical rally featuring top administration officials opened a broader "Freedom 250" anniversary program that had bypassed Congress's own bipartisan commission in favor of soliciting corporate donations for access to the president.

**Tuesday**

Tuesday deepened the fund story rather than letting it rest. Acting Attorney General Todd Blanche signed an order permanently barring the IRS from investigating or prosecuting Trump, his family, and affiliated businesses — halting, among other things, a live audit potentially worth more than $100 million. Senate Democrats immediately demanded preservation of related documents. Then came a signal from inside the administration itself: Brian Morrissey, the Treasury general counsel responsible for approving Judgment Fund disbursements, resigned as the fund was announced, a move widely read as an internal legal objection. Elsewhere, a federal judge barred ICE from making arrests at three lower Manhattan immigration courthouses except in exceptional circumstances, and Trump signed executive orders easing fintech regulation while directing financial regulators to treat immigration status as a risk factor in banking decisions. The New York Times sued over a Pentagon policy requiring journalist escorts, and Trump gave the press an extensive tour of his fortified White House ballroom project, describing drone-proofing and titanium fencing in terms more befitting a bunker than a banquet hall.

**Wednesday**

Wednesday made the fund's ambitions explicit — and, simultaneously, exposed their fragility. Blanche signed an amended agreement declaring the government would "forever discharge" Trump, his sons, and affiliated entities from all IRS claims and audits, a legal shield with no clear precedent for a sitting president. Almost in the same breath, Judge Kathleen Williams dismissed Trump's original IRS lawsuit and found that its withdrawal referenced no actual settlement of record at all, undercutting the very origin story the fund had been built on. Capitol Police officers Daniel Hodges and Harry Dunn, both injured defending the building on January 6, filed suit arguing the fund was illegal and could end up compensating the people who attacked them. Justice Ketanji Brown Jackson published an unusually candid public warning that the Supreme Court risked losing the public's confidence, citing the gutting of the Voting Rights Act and a pattern of emergency orders favoring Trump administration policies. And after the Senate parliamentarian blocked $1 billion in ballroom funding, Trump demanded Senate Republicans replace her with a loyalist and eliminate the filibuster outright, threatening political consequences for anyone who refused. The Senate, separately, advanced an Iran war-powers resolution by a 50–47 vote, with four Republicans joining Democrats to require congressional authorization for continued military operations. A federal judge also ordered the White House to comply with the Presidential Records Act, including preservation of Signal and WhatsApp communications, rejecting a separation-of-powers defense.

**Thursday**

Thursday brought the fund story to open crisis inside the Republican Party. Roughly two dozen GOP senators — including Mitch McConnell, Susan Collins, Lisa Murkowski, Thom Tillis, and Rand Paul — publicly and privately denounced the fund as "morally wrong," "stupid," and unprecedented, during a nearly two-hour, openly hostile meeting with Blanche. Representatives Fitzpatrick and Suozzi began drafting bipartisan legislation to kill the fund outright, and Speaker Johnson reportedly declined an Oval Office summons over the matter. The same day, the House quietly pulled its own Iran war-powers vote — one likely to pass with bipartisan support — adjourning early rather than let it reach the floor, a second retreat from a vote Congress seemed prepared to win.

Courts, meanwhile, delivered two forceful rebukes of prosecutorial conduct. Judge Waverly Crenshaw dismissed vindictive-prosecution charges against Kilmar Ábrego García, finding the Justice Department had reopened a closed case specifically to punish him after he successfully challenged his unlawful deportation — an act the judge called "an abuse of prosecuting power." Separately, federal prosecutors dropped all charges with prejudice against the "Broadview Six," Chicago ICE protesters, after a judge found prosecutors had improperly manipulated a grand jury, including removing jurors unwilling to indict. Elsewhere, a panel composed entirely of Trump appointees approved a 250-foot triumphal arch near Arlington National Cemetery over 600 letters of public opposition. Eighteen young Americans filed a constitutional challenge against February's rollback of vehicle emissions standards, and Trump dismissed $43.6 billion in Iran-conflict-related fuel costs to American households as "peanuts."

**Friday**

By Friday, the fund's political cost had become tangible and immediate. Senate Republican leadership quietly shelved a $72 billion immigration and border enforcement bill — already stripped of $1 billion in ballroom funding — for fear that bringing it to the floor would let Democrats force a public reckoning over the fund. The same day, the fund's real-world purpose came into sharper focus: Proud Boys leader Enrique Tarrio, Mike Lindell, Michael Cohen, and others announced they would formally apply for payouts ranging into the hundreds of millions of dollars, even as Trump publicly described the fund as an act of generosity. Federal courts in Maine and Wisconsin rejected Justice Department demands for sensitive state voter data, continuing a pattern of judicial pushback seen in at least eight states. At Delaney Hall, a New Jersey detention facility, roughly 300 to 400 detainees escalated a hunger and labor strike over medical neglect, spoiled food, denial of bond, and stalled case processing, citing violations of their Fourth, Fifth, and Sixth Amendment rights. Trump repeatedly promoted an online casino illegal in at least seventeen states shortly after its co-owner donated $1 million to his super PAC. And in Virginia, a judge dismissed charges against a school administrator who had ignored warnings that a six-year-old brought a gun to school, closing off criminal accountability despite an earlier $10 million civil verdict against her.

By week's end, a single thread ran through nearly everything: a fund conceived to close out one lawsuit had, within five days, become a permanent tax shield for a sitting president, a potential payday for people convicted of attacking the Capitol, and a source of open revolt within the president's own party — forceful enough to stall unrelated, urgent legislation on immigration enforcement. That its collapse into scandal took place alongside courts rejecting vindictive prosecutions, throwing out manipulated indictments, and blocking improper demands for voter data suggests an institutional system still capable of friction against concentrated executive power, even as Congress twice declined to test that power directly on questions of war. Meanwhile, the human costs of policy — migrant deaths in the desert, children separated from detained parents, detainees on hunger strike over medical neglect — persisted in the background, largely untouched by the week's institutional drama, a reminder that accountability fights in Washington do not automatically reach the people most affected by the policies in question.
```
