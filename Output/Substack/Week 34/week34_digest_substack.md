# War Footing: Troops, Threats, and an Assassination

*A week that opened with the president threatening a city and closed with a political assassination reshaping the national conversation*

![Header image](week34-digest-featured.jpg)

```markdown
This was a week that began with the language of war turned against American cities and ended with a political assassination that reordered the national conversation entirely. In between, courts split over the limits of executive power, the Supreme Court repeatedly stepped in to clear the administration's path, and a long-running dispute over a dead financier's documents kept eroding public trust in official statements. By Friday, the country was absorbing two story lines at once: an accelerating contest over the machinery of government, and a raw, unresolved argument over political violence.

The weekend set the tone. The president posted an AI-generated image of Chicago captioned with lines borrowed from a war film, then followed it with concrete steps — announcements of federal law enforcement deployments to Chicago and Baltimore, and talk of sending National Guard troops to New Orleans. Governors and mayors did not treat this as ordinary bluster; they called it what they saw it as, a threat of armed force against domestic political opponents' territory. The rhetoric came paired with action. Immigration and Customs Enforcement expanded a program called "Patriot 2.0" in Massachusetts and sent 230 agents into Chicago timed near Mexican Independence Day celebrations, forcing cancellations of cultural events. In Georgia, a raid on a Hyundai-LG battery plant led to 475 arrests, nearly 300 of them South Korean nationals, straining relations with a major foreign investor and ally. In Washington, Mayor Muriel Bowser, facing the end of a 30-day federal takeover, chose accommodation over confrontation, issuing an executive order for open-ended coordination with federal law enforcement rather than fighting to reclaim home rule. Courts, meanwhile, issued a mixed set of rulings — allowing the administration to claw back $16 billion in climate grants and letting a disputed Florida detention facility keep operating, while a separate court reinstated a Federal Trade Commission member Trump had fired without cause. The Epstein files controversy also resurfaced, with a hidden-camera recording capturing a Justice Department official discussing plans to redact Republican names from released files, and the White House warning lawmakers against supporting a discharge petition that would force fuller disclosure. Amid all this, more than a thousand current and former Department of Health and Human Services employees, joined by nine former CDC directors, called for Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr.'s resignation. And in both Chicago and Washington, thousands marched peacefully against the weekend's threats — an early sign that the projection of force was not going unanswered.

By Monday, the symbolism sharpened further: the Department of Defense was formally renamed the Department of War, a rebranding that fit neatly with a week already built around martial language turned toward domestic ends. That same day, the Supreme Court's conservative majority, in a 6-3 decision, lifted a lower court's restraining order and allowed federal immigration agents in Los Angeles to stop people based on race, language, or occupation — with Justice Kavanaugh's concurrence describing ethnicity as a "relevant factor." ICE resumed sweeps immediately. Monday also brought a direct hit to the president's credibility on the Epstein matter: the House Oversight Committee released a 2003 birthday book containing a letter matching one Trump had publicly called "fake and nonexistent." A federal appeals court separately ruled that Trump had exceeded his statutory authority in imposing sweeping tariffs, prompting the administration to seek expedited Supreme Court review while the tariffs stayed in effect. Another panel unanimously upheld the $83.3 million defamation verdict against Trump in the E. Jean Carroll case, rejecting his immunity claim. And in the Senate, Eric Schmitt delivered a speech built around European Christian ancestry and inherited "shame," a moment that reflected how exclusionary framings of American identity were moving from the margins toward the Republican mainstream.

Tuesday deepened two of the week's central threads. A coordinated legal campaign against the Voting Rights Act came into fuller view: the Eighth Circuit ruled that Sections 2 and 208 contain no private right of action, gutting enforcement across seven states, while Alabama, Mississippi, and Louisiana pursued parallel strategies to eliminate private enforcement nationwide, and the Republican National Committee pushed the same argument to the Fifth Circuit. The Supreme Court, for its part, scheduled reargument in *Louisiana v. Callais*, a case that could hollow out the Act's core remedy. The same day, the Court's shadow docket produced two more consequential, unexplained rulings — allowing Trump to fire the last Democratic FTC commissioner, discarding ninety years of precedent on independent-agency protections, and staying a lower court order that would have required the administration to spend $4.9 billion in already-appropriated foreign aid. Neither came with full briefing or a written opinion. Congressional Republicans, meanwhile, largely accepted the administration's unilateral funding cuts and military actions without prior notice, and Senator Mark Warner questioned whether oversight still functioned in any meaningful sense after the Pentagon canceled a classified briefing he had scheduled. The Epstein letter controversy also escalated: the Wall Street Journal published the document in full, and the White House press secretary falsely attributed a signature analysis to a conservative outlet that had not produced it, even as an actual forensic examiner said the signature was consistent with Trump's. Separately, three senior FBI officials, including former acting director Brian Driscoll, sued Director Kash Patel, alleging he fired them for perceived political disloyalty, and Director of National Intelligence Tulsi Gabbard ordered the NSA to recall an accurate classified report on an envoy's contacts with Venezuela's government.

Then came Wednesday, and the week's second, unrelated axis of crisis. Conservative activist Charlie Kirk was shot and killed while speaking at Utah Valley University, setting off an immediate manhunt and a wave of shock that displaced nearly everything else in the news cycle. Within hours, and before the shooter's identity or motive was known, the president publicly blamed "the radical left" and pledged federal action against organizations he said funded political violence — a framing that made no mention of recent violence against Democratic officials or his own history of inflammatory rhetoric. The same day, federal troops and agents formally took control of the District of Columbia's police department despite crime rates at thirty-year lows, prompting the formation of a local activist group called Free DC and setting up further weekend protests. Less than an hour after the Kirk shooting, a separate school shooting at Evergreen High School in Colorado left two students wounded before the shooter died by suicide, compounding an already grim day. The White House also formally dismissed the Epstein birthday letter as a hoax, even though it had been bound into Epstein's own birthday book at the time, with Republican allies either denying the signature or downplaying its significance. And overseas, Russian drones breached Polish airspace; Poland shot down nineteen of them and invoked NATO's Article 4 consultation process, while Trump's response amounted to a brief social media post with no substantive commitment to collective defense.

Thursday was consumed by the disorder surrounding the investigation into Kirk's death. Director Patel announced that a suspect had been apprehended, then reversed the claim roughly two hours later, creating confusion about the case's actual status some thirty-six hours after the shooting. The lawsuit against Patel by former FBI officials proceeded in parallel, alleging systematic politicization of the bureau under instructions traced to the White House. Meanwhile, a retaliatory campaign against Kirk's critics gathered force: Representative Clay Higgins said he wanted to revoke licenses and impose bans on people who had "belittled" Kirk's death, MAGA-aligned influencers organized efforts to identify and target critics for employer retaliation, and multiple firings followed nationwide, including MSNBC's dismissal of contributor Matthew Dowd for remarks connecting political rhetoric to violence. Some commentators went further, using the unresolved investigation to call for authoritarian measures and a "purge" of the Democratic Party, invoking the Reichstag fire as a historical parallel — all before any facts about the shooter had been confirmed. The day closed with politicized commemorations: a proposal for a Capitol statue honoring Kirk, a posthumous Presidential Medal of Freedom announced by Trump during a September 11 memorial ceremony, and a political and religious address from Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth to uniformed troops about Kirk's death.

By Friday, the suspect had been identified and arrested. Tyler Robinson, 22, was taken into custody after his own father alerted authorities; his background, by the available record, was that of a young man from a Republican, Mormon family. That fact did not stop a fast-moving current of false claims. Representative Nancy Mace asserted, without support, that Robinson was transgender. Steve Bannon labeled him a "left-wing Antifa-aligned radical." Other conservative commentators blamed "woke" college professors. None of these characterizations matched what was publicly known about Robinson, and none were meaningfully corrected once contradicted. Against that backdrop, Utah Governor Spencer Cox offered a notable counterpoint, calling for a rejection of political violence and a rebuilding of civil dialogue, drawing comparisons to the upheavals of the 1960s — a markedly different posture from the president's immediate assignment of blame two days earlier. The retaliation campaign against private citizens continued as well, with school employees in at least six states placed on leave or investigated over social media posts made within a day of the shooting. Elsewhere, the week's institutional threads kept moving: Missouri Republicans approved a mid-decade redistricting map eliminating a Kansas City-area Democratic seat, an appeals court allowed the administration to cut Medicaid funding to Planned Parenthood, threatening the closure of up to 200 clinics serving more than a million low-income patients, and the Supreme Court's shadow docket issued yet another unexplained order extending profiling authority in immigration enforcement. Trump also suggested the Russian drone incursion into Poland "could have been a mistake," a claim Polish officials publicly disputed. And the National Guard deployment once aimed at Chicago was redirected to Memphis, even though that city's mayor said he had asked for funding and additional police, not troops.

Taken as a whole, the week recorded two distinct but simultaneous strains on American public life. One was structural: a presidency testing the boundaries of federal power over cities, agencies, courts, and voting rights, aided at key moments by a Supreme Court willing to issue consequential rulings without explanation. The other was human and immediate: a political assassination that, before facts could settle, was folded into pre-existing narratives, retaliatory campaigns, and calls for extraordinary measures. Between them stood a scattering of institutional actors — a governor urging restraint, judges pushing back on executive claims, health officials demanding accountability, citizens marching in the street — whose responses did not resolve the week's tensions but did mark, clearly, that they were being contested rather than accepted.
```
