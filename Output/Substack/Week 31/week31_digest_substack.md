# A Capital Under Occupation, a Map War Erupts

*Troops filled Washington's streets while Texas and California turned redistricting into open partisan warfare*

![Header image](week31-digest-featured.jpg)

```markdown
The week of August 16 to 22 opened with troops in the streets of the nation's capital and closed with those same troops ordered to carry weapons — a bookend that captured a week in which federal power expanded steadily across multiple fronts: the policing of Washington, the redrawing of congressional maps, and the treatment of officials who produced facts the administration found inconvenient. Between those poles, courts intervened repeatedly, if not always successfully, to slow the pace of change.

The weekend began with President Trump federalizing the Metropolitan Police Department and ordering roughly 800 National Guard troops and federal agents into Washington, D.C., citing what he called an "out of control" crime wave. Government data told a different story: violent crime in the city was at a 30-year low. Republican governors in West Virginia, South Carolina, and Ohio nonetheless pledged additional troops within days, and the administration began exploring a longer-term arrangement with Congress to keep federal control in place. The same weekend brought a wave of other major actions. Trump issued pardons covering everyone convicted or awaiting trial for the January 6 Capitol attack, after previously describing the day as one of "love," while appointees moved to purge prosecutors and target FBI agents who had worked those cases. In Florida, the state Supreme Court, led by a DeSantis appointee, upheld a redraw of northern districts that diluted Black voters' influence, effectively nullifying a 2010 constitutional amendment meant to prevent exactly that outcome. Sensitive State Department documents from the Trump-Putin summit turned up on a public hotel printer in Alaska, which the White House falsely described as a lunch menu. And in Texas, House Democrats fled the state to deny Republicans the quorum needed to pass a mid-decade congressional map expected to hand the GOP five new seats — the opening move in what would become the week's second major storyline. Trump also floated eliminating mail-in voting by executive order, an idea that surfaced immediately after his meeting with Vladimir Putin, who had publicly questioned the legitimacy of mail ballots.

By Monday, the mail-in voting proposal had hardened into a formal plan, with Trump saying his lawyers were drafting an order to eliminate mail ballots and certain voting machines before the 2026 midterms, despite the Constitution's clear allocation of election administration to the states. The day's other major development concerned data rather than voting: Trump fired Bureau of Labor Statistics Commissioner Erika McEntarfer, accusing her without evidence of issuing a "rigged" jobs report, and nominated Heritage Foundation economist E.J. Antoni to replace her — a choice later complicated by revelations that Antoni had promoted election-denial theories and had been present at the Capitol on January 6. The Justice Department, under Attorney General Pam Bondi, impaneled a grand jury to investigate unfounded "treason" claims against former President Obama, based on assertions from Trump and Director of National Intelligence Tulsi Gabbard. Missouri Attorney General Andrew Bailey, known for actions like investigating Media Matters, was installed in a newly created co-deputy director role at the FBI. Courts offered an early counterweight: a federal judge heard an ACLU suit over conditions at the "Alligator Alcatraz" immigration detention facility in the Everglades, and a separate order temporarily halted its expansion. Meanwhile, in Washington, federal investigators opened a probe into whether the city's own police department had manipulated crime statistics — the same statistics being cited to justify the occupation. In Texas, Democratic lawmakers ended their two-week walkout and returned to the Capitol, while in California, Governor Newsom filed a federal records request after border patrol agents appeared at his own redistricting announcement.

Tuesday centered almost entirely on what happened next in Texas. Returning Democratic lawmakers were required by Speaker Dustin Burrows to sign "permission slips" accepting 24-hour police monitoring as a condition of reentering the chamber. Representative Nicole Collier refused and was effectively confined inside the House chamber overnight; she later sued over her detention and faced threats of felony charges over phone calls she made from a bathroom. The measures drew comparisons to Jim Crow-era controls on Black political participation. The same day, federal prosecutors formally opened a criminal inquiry into the DC police data question first raised on Monday, following the earlier suspension of a police commander accused of falsifying statistics. The Congressional Budget Office added a separate note of consequence, projecting that the reconciliation bill adding $3.4 trillion to the deficit would trigger up to $491 billion in Medicare cuts and nearly $1 trillion in Medicaid cuts by 2034. Trump also began publicly criticizing the Smithsonian's exhibits on slavery, directing lawyers to review the museum's content in terms similar to earlier actions against universities. Separately, DNI Gabbard revoked security clearances for 37 current and former intelligence officials accused of politicization and leaking.

The Texas standoff reached its resolution on Wednesday. In a gesture of protest, Democratic members tore up the police-monitoring agreements they had been forced to sign, then watched Republicans pass the redistricting map anyway — a map Democrats argue dilutes the votes of Black and Latino Texans in violation of the Voting Rights Act. The day also brought a federal appeals court ruling allowing the administration to proceed, at least temporarily, with ending Temporary Protected Status for more than 60,000 immigrants from Nicaragua, Honduras, and Nepal. A federal judge separately found that Alina Habba, a former Trump lawyer, was not lawfully serving as acting U.S. attorney in New Jersey, though the ruling was stayed pending appeal. Scrutiny of the Washington deployment intensified as reports put its cost above $1 million per day, even as more Republican governors sent troops from states with higher crime rates than the capital's — reinforcing the sense that the operation functioned as a display of power rather than a response to data. More than 750 current and former HHS employees sent a letter accusing Health Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr. of fueling distrust in vaccines and public health guidance, citing a fatal shooting at CDC headquarters in Atlanta as evidence of the costs of that rhetoric. In North Carolina, bills advanced to criminalize unauthorized camping and to require full Social Security numbers for voter registration.

Thursday brought the redistricting fight to its second front. Governor Newsom signed legislation setting up a November special election to redraw California's congressional map, aiming to offset the Texas map by adding up to five Democratic seats — confirmation that what began as a Texas dispute had become a genuine national contest over how congressional lines get drawn outside the normal once-a-decade process. Immigration enforcement expanded sharply the same day: the State Department and U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services announced ongoing social-media vetting of all 55 million U.S. visa holders, part of a broader effort to screen for "anti-American" activity. A State Department press officer was fired after objecting to language regarding forced relocation of Palestinians. Journalist Mario Guevara, held by ICE despite a judge's order permitting his release on bond, sued along with the ACLU, arguing his detention was retaliation for his reporting on immigration raids. The administration also withdrew more than $12 million in federal health grants from California, citing the promotion of "gender ideology," and the White House followed up its earlier criticism of the Smithsonian with a formal published attack on the museum's exhibits covering race, slavery, trans identity, and immigration.

Friday delivered the week's clearest judicial pushback alongside further escalation of the Washington occupation. A federal judge in Miami ordered the "Alligator Alcatraz" detention facility closed within 60 days, citing severe environmental harm and barring further intake, in a case brought by environmental groups and a Native American tribe. Kilmar Ábrego García, wrongfully deported to El Salvador earlier in the year in what the administration called an "administrative error," was released from criminal custody to await trial with his family in Maryland. At the same time, the administration canceled collective bargaining agreements covering roughly 400,000 federal workers across agencies including the VA, EPA, and USDA, following a favorable appeals court ruling and citing national security grounds from a March executive order. In Washington, Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth ordered National Guard troops patrolling the capital to carry service weapons, with Trump suggesting the posture could extend to Chicago and New York. Trump also signed a directive authorizing military action against Latin American drug cartels, newly designated as foreign terrorist organizations, following the deployment of U.S. destroyers near Venezuela. The Justice Department released redacted transcripts of interviews with Ghislaine Maxwell touching on Jeffrey Epstein's associations, part of an ongoing, incremental disclosure to the House Oversight Committee. FEMA, meanwhile, began requiring an email address for disaster aid applications, a change critics said could delay assistance for elderly and disabled survivors with limited internet access.

Taken as a whole, the week showed executive power extending simultaneously along several axes — over a city's police force, over the shape of congressional districts, over the officials who produce inconvenient statistics, and over who may enter, remain in, or be believed within the country. The occupation of Washington rested on a claim about crime that the government's own investigators were, at the same time, working to verify or disprove — a contradiction that persisted from the first weekend through the final Friday without resolution. The redistricting fight, born in a Texas legislative walkout, ended the week as a two-state confrontation with national implications for how House seats will be contested in 2026. And where the executive branch pushed hardest, the judiciary offered the week's most consistent counterweight: ordering a detention facility closed, freeing a wrongfully deported man, and finding an unlawfully installed prosecutor unlawfully installed. Whether those rulings hold, and whether the deployment of armed troops in American cities becomes a template rather than an exception, are questions the following weeks will begin to answer.
```
