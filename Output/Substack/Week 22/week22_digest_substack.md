# A Parade, a Purge, and a Political Assassination

*Mass protests, a lawmaker's killing, immigration raids, and a widening standoff between courts and the executive branch defined a volatile week*

![Header image](week22-digest-featured.jpg)

```markdown
The week of June 14th began with a jarring contrast and ended in an unsettled one. On Saturday, President Trump presided over a $40 million military parade in Washington, staged to mark both his 79th birthday and the Army's 250th anniversary. Attendance was thin, and the event drew criticism for its disorganization. That same weekend, more than five million people turned out for over 2,100 "No Kings" protests across the country — one of the largest coordinated demonstrations in American history, organized around objections to what participants described as an unchecked consolidation of presidential power. The split-screen — a state spectacle drawing modest crowds against a nationwide wave of dissent — set the tone for a week in which public sentiment and official display seemed to run in opposite directions.

The same weekend brought a far graver development. In Minnesota, a gunman posing as a police officer shot and killed State Representative Melissa Hortman and her husband, while wounding State Senator John Hoffman and his wife in a related attack. Governor Tim Walz called it a politically motivated assassination. The FBI opened a manhunt for the suspect, Vance Boelter, who was apprehended and later charged federally. The killing of a sitting legislator, and the wounding of another, marked one of the most serious acts of political violence against elected officials in recent American history, and it cast a shadow over the rest of the week, including a bout of misinformation that falsely tied the shooter to left-wing politics before facts emerged showing his views leaned in the opposite direction.

The weekend's unrest did not stay peaceful everywhere. In Los Angeles, police used tear gas and rubber bullets to disperse a protest that turned confrontational near a federal building. In Salt Lake City, a peacekeeper at a similar demonstration fatally shot a bystander while confronting an armed man. Against this backdrop, President Trump ordered thousands of National Guard troops and hundreds of Marines into Los Angeles without the consent of Governor Gavin Newsom — the first such unilateral domestic deployment since 1965. California challenged the move in court immediately, opening a legal fight that would run through much of the week.

Immigration enforcement lurched unpredictably over the same days. ICE briefly paused workplace raids in agriculture and hospitality after Trump acknowledged the economic damage they were causing, even as the administration simultaneously announced intensified deportation efforts aimed at Democratic-led cities and withdrew a list of "noncompliant" sanctuary jurisdictions. Elsewhere, an executive order shaped by adviser Stephen Miller sought to end birthright citizenship for children of undocumented immigrants, a direct challenge to the Fourteenth Amendment that remained pending before the Supreme Court. In a smaller but telling episode, the EPA dropped an enforcement case against the private prison operator Geo Group over toxic disinfectant use at a detention facility — a decision that followed substantial company donations to Trump and Republican leaders. The weekend closed with symbolic rollbacks as well: Trump removed a bust of Martin Luther King Jr. from the Oval Office without explanation, and Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth ordered the renaming of a Navy ship that had honored LGBTQ rights pioneer Harvey Milk — early signs of a pattern that would recur later in the week around Juneteenth.

By Monday, the courts were pushing back. Judge William Young ruled that the administration's termination of hundreds of NIH research grants — many tied to DEI and LGBTQ health research — was unlawful and discriminatory, ordering the funds restored. Meanwhile, ICE raids continued in Los Angeles, sweeping through a Santa Fe Springs swap meet, a Home Depot parking lot in Montebello, and other sites, detaining day laborers and, in some cases, U.S. citizens, and prompting accusations of racial profiling. In Georgia, Spanish-language journalist Mario Guevara was arrested while covering a "No Kings" protest and, despite holding valid work authorization, was transferred into ICE custody — a move that blurred the line between immigration enforcement and press suppression. The American Bar Association filed suit the same day, alleging that executive orders targeting law firms were designed to intimidate lawyers away from challenging the administration in court. In a quieter but still notable set of developments, Trump fired an NRC commissioner without cause, the GAO found the administration had illegally frozen funding for libraries, archives, and museums, and the Trump family launched a new commercial venture, Trump Mobile, renewing questions about the mixing of the presidency with personal profit.

Tuesday's most striking moment came in New York, where NYC Comptroller and mayoral candidate Brad Lander was arrested by ICE and FBI agents while trying to accompany an immigrant out of a courthouse. Agents accused him of assaulting law enforcement, a claim disputed by video evidence; he was released hours later without charges. The arrest of a sitting elected official by federal agents raised alarm about the use of immigration enforcement as a tool of political intimidation. That same day, ICE raids on farms and hotels resumed after only four days of pause, under pressure from immigration hardliners pushing for 3,000 daily arrests — another turn in a week defined by enforcement whiplash. Congressional Democrats, including Senator Richard Blumenthal and Representative Jamie Raskin, opened inquiries into Trump's cryptocurrency dealings, including a dinner event for top purchasers of his memecoin, while Senator Jeff Merkley introduced legislation aimed at curbing crypto-related corruption. Elsewhere, a federal judge expanded protections allowing transgender and intersex citizens to update gender markers on passports, and congressional leaders began reviewing security arrangements for lawmakers in the wake of the Minnesota killing.

Wednesday brought two major Supreme Court developments in entirely separate domains. In *United States v. Skrmetti*, the Court's conservative majority upheld Tennessee's ban on gender-affirming care for minors, ruling that it did not constitute sex-based discrimination — a decision with implications for similar laws nationwide. Separately, a federal judge held Florida Attorney General James Uthmeier in contempt for continuing to enforce a state immigration law despite a court order blocking it, a direct rebuke of official defiance of judicial authority. The National Guard dispute in Los Angeles took another turn as well: a federal judge ordered control of the troops returned to Governor Newsom, only for an appellate panel to stay that ruling and leave federal control in place, with Hegseth suggesting he might not comply with an adverse decision regardless. The same day, corporate America's retreat from Juneteenth recognition became visible, as Amazon and Verizon withdrew financial support for celebrations and West Virginia's governor ended state backing for the holiday — developments that traced directly back to the MLK bust removal days earlier. Federal courts also blocked the administration from barring Harvard from admitting international students, part of a broader wave of rulings that day pausing or narrowing executive actions on education and environmental policy, even as the State Department announced it would begin reviewing the social media accounts of student visa applicants for signs of hostility toward the United States.

Thursday deepened several of the week's central conflicts. New DHS guidance required lawmakers to give 72 hours' notice before visiting immigration detention facilities, a rule that directly contradicted federal law; Representatives Dan Goldman and Jerry Nadler were subsequently denied entry to an ICE facility in Manhattan. The National Guard dispute reached a further stage when the Ninth Circuit Court of Appeals overturned the lower court's order and allowed Trump to retain control of the deployed troops, finding sufficient evidence of violent protester conduct to justify the federalization — a ruling that, unlike Wednesday's stay, represented a more definitive step in the administration's favor. The administration also formally rescinded long-standing guidance that had shielded schools and churches from immigration arrests, forcing community organizations to strip these locations of their status as safe spaces. Trump renewed calls for a special prosecutor to investigate the 2020 election as fraudulent while separately dismissing his own intelligence community's assessment of Iran's nuclear timeline. Smaller acts of resistance surfaced too: the Los Angeles Dodgers denied ICE agents access to stadium parking lots, and an Australian writer was denied entry to the United States and deported after being questioned about his views on the Israel-Palestine conflict and his coverage of student protests.

The week closed on Friday with developments spanning media, energy, and environmental policy. The administration terminated 639 employees at Voice of America and its parent agency, part of a broader effort that had eliminated 1,400 positions since March and left nearly dismantled an organization that once reached 360 million people weekly. The Supreme Court, in a 7-2 ruling, allowed fossil fuel companies and Republican-led states to proceed with a challenge to California's federal waiver for stricter vehicle emissions standards, reopening a long-settled area of environmental law. Using emergency executive authority, the administration ordered a Michigan coal plant to remain open past its scheduled closure and delayed another plant's planned shutdown, overriding the state's clean energy law. Congress, meanwhile, used joint resolutions to reverse an Office of the Comptroller of the Currency rule on bank mergers and an EPA rule on pollution source classification, rolling back regulatory protections through ordinary legislative means.

Taken as a whole, the week traced several threads that would not resolve neatly by Friday. Political violence entered the machinery of elected governance directly, from the killing of a state legislator to the forceful, if brief, detention of a sitting city comptroller. Immigration enforcement swung between retreat and escalation, driven less by settled policy than by shifting economic and political pressure, while reaching further into spaces — courthouses, schools, churches, congressional oversight visits — previously treated as off-limits. The courts remained an active check on executive power, blocking grant terminations and expanding rights protections, even as appellate rulings and a major Supreme Court decision showed that judicial outcomes were far from uniformly resistant to the administration's aims. And alongside these confrontations, a quieter erosion continued: the paring back of Juneteenth recognition, the removal of civil-rights symbols, and the near-dismantling of an independent international broadcaster suggested that institutional memory and public voice were being narrowed even as the more visible battles over courts, troops, and protest dominated the week's attention.
```
