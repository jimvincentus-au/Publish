# The Week the Government Said No to the Courts

*A defied Supreme Court order, a firebombed governor's mansion, a university that said no, and a president who floated deporting citizens*

![Header image](week13-digest-featured.jpg)

```markdown
This was the week the machinery of American government tested, in public, whether a president could simply say no to the courts — and whether anyone could stop him. Over seven days, a wrongly deported Maryland man became the fulcrum for a constitutional confrontation that widened by the day, a state governor's family survived a firebombing of their home, one of the nation's oldest universities refused to bend to federal pressure and paid for it within hours, and by Friday the FBI was leading a sitting judge out of her own courthouse in handcuffs.

The week began badly and got worse before Monday even arrived. Overnight between Saturday and Sunday, a man named Cody Balmer scaled a fence around the Pennsylvania governor's residence, broke in with a hammer, and firebombed the building hours after Governor Josh Shapiro's family had finished their Passover Seder. State troopers evacuated Shapiro, his wife, and their four children before the fire spread further. Balmer was arrested and later told investigators he hated Shapiro over his position on Israel; he was charged with attempted murder, terrorism, and arson, and held without bail. It was, by any plain reading, a political attack on an elected official's family in their home — the kind of event that in past eras would have drawn immediate, unified condemnation from the nation's leadership. That weekend also brought fresh economic turbulence: Trump paused most tariffs for 90 days while keeping a 145 percent tariff on China, a reversal that came only after a near-collapse in the government debt market and a sharp slide in the dollar — the kind of capital flight more often associated with unstable economies than with the United States. Tens of thousands turned out for rallies in Los Angeles and Boise against the administration, and in Washington, a Social Security Administration IT executive was physically dragged from his office after objecting to a policy targeting immigrants' bank access.

By Monday, the week's central storyline sharpened into focus. In a staged Oval Office meeting with El Salvador's president, Nayib Bukele, Trump signaled that his administration would not facilitate the return of Kilmar Abrego Garcia, a Maryland man the government had already admitted was wrongly deported — this despite a unanimous Supreme Court order directing the administration to facilitate his return. A hot microphone caught Trump telling Bukele he wanted to go after "home-growns" next, and the two discussed building more prison capacity to hold American citizens abroad. Asked directly, Attorney General Pam Bondi and Press Secretary Karoline Leavitt both declined to rule out that the administration could lawfully deport U.S. citizens. The administration then filed a court declaration containing a claim from senior aide Stephen Miller that the Supreme Court had ruled 9-0 in the government's favor — a description of the ruling that did not match its actual text. Asked about the Shapiro firebombing nearly a day after it happened, Trump said he had not heard about it, then dismissed the attacker as "probably just a whack job."

Monday also delivered the week's second defining confrontation. Harvard University's legal team formally rejected federal demands to dictate its governance, admissions, and hiring practices in exchange for continued funding, arguing plainly that no government should tell a private university what it may teach. The retaliation was immediate: within hours, the administration froze $2.2 billion in grants and a $60 million contract. This was not passive resistance — Harvard also mounted a public defense of its position, making clear it intended an active fight rather than a quiet standoff. Elsewhere that day, ICE detained Mohsen Mahdawi, a Columbia University student and lawful permanent resident, during his own naturalization interview in Vermont, in what his lawyers said was retaliation for pro-Palestinian campus activism; a federal judge blocked his deportation that same day. The White House, meanwhile, again denied Associated Press journalists access to the Oval Office despite a standing court order requiring it, and issued an official statement branding NPR and PBS purveyors of "radical, woke propaganda."

Tuesday brought the judiciary's first sharp answer. U.S. District Judge Paula Xinis declared that "nothing has been done" to comply with the Supreme Court's order in the Abrego Garcia case, rejected the administration's argument that the Bukele meeting satisfied that order, and directed depositions and sworn testimony from up to six officials. Trump, who had briefly signaled he might respect the Court's order, reversed himself again, insisting his administration lacked the power to bring Abrego Garcia back — even as he separately said he would "love" to send American citizens to El Salvador's mega-prison, with Leavitt calling the idea "a legal question the president is looking into." The same day, the IRS began preparing to revoke Harvard's tax-exempt status, an action legal experts noted appeared to violate a 1998 law barring the president from directing IRS enforcement; the Education Department confirmed a separate $2.3 billion funding freeze. Civil society pushed back on multiple fronts: the American Association of University Professors sued over the Harvard freeze, the NAACP sued over anti-DEI school policies, labor groups sued over canceled child-labor programs, and Protect Democracy sued over the removal of a federal spending database. A whistleblower also revealed that DOGE staff had accessed National Labor Relations Board data in a manner resembling unauthorized intrusion, including a login attempt traced to a Russian IP address, while the group scrubbed identifying information from its own website. Amid all this, Trump's media company launched investment products explicitly built to profit from his own tariff decisions, and a federal judge in Boston blocked the administration from abruptly ending legal status for roughly 500,000 migrants from four countries. Barbara Lee won Oakland's mayoral race by ranked-choice vote, and North Carolina's contested judicial election dispute deepened as a losing candidate pressed a challenge to roughly 260 ballots built on flawed residency data.

Wednesday opened a second, legally distinct front in the standoff between courts and the executive. Chief U.S. District Judge James Boasberg, in a separate case concerning deportation flights conducted under the Alien Enemies Act, issued a 46-page opinion finding probable cause that administration officials had committed criminal contempt by failing to recall those flights despite his earlier order — and warned he would appoint an independent prosecutor if the administration would not prosecute itself. This was not the Abrego Garcia case; it involved different facts, a different judge, and a different legal theory, but it landed in the same week and compounded the same underlying message — that the executive branch was treating judicial orders as optional. California's governor and attorney general sued separately that day, arguing Trump's unilateral tariffs exceeded authority reserved to Congress. The Justice Department sued Maine over its transgender-athlete policy, following earlier threats to withhold the state's entire $250 million K-12 budget — threats a federal judge had already partially blocked. North Carolina's election board, after investigative reporting found dozens of challenged voters had documented histories of living in the state, committed to restoring wrongly invalidated ballots. An immigration judge denied bond to Rümeysa Öztürk, a Turkish doctoral student detained over a campus op-ed about Gaza, ruling her a "danger to the community" even though the government itself had argued only flight risk — a case distinct in its facts from both Abrego Garcia and Mahdawi, but part of the same widening pattern of immigration enforcement reaching political speech. The State Department closed its office monitoring foreign disinformation, and police used stun guns on protesters at a Marjorie Taylor Greene town hall in Georgia. Senator Chris Van Hollen traveled to El Salvador to see his constituent Abrego Garcia and was told a meeting might not be possible, even as officials confirmed the U.S. was paying El Salvador to hold him.

Thursday's ruling tied the week's judicial threads together. A three-judge panel of the Fourth Circuit Court of Appeals — including J. Harvie Wilkinson, a Reagan appointee — unanimously rejected the administration's appeal in the Abrego Garcia case, writing bluntly that the government was "asserting a right to stash away residents of this country in foreign prisons without the semblance of due process." Coming a day after Boasberg's contempt finding, the ruling made clear that judicial resistance to the administration's posture was not confined to one court or one party's appointees. The same day, Trump escalated a different institutional fight, publicly attacking Federal Reserve Chair Jerome Powell as "always too late and wrong" and declaring that "Powell's termination cannot come fast enough" after Powell warned that tariffs would hit the economy harder than expected; Powell publicly rejected the pressure and reaffirmed the Fed's independence. DOGE staff terminated roughly 1,500 of the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau's 1,700 employees despite concerns the move violated a standing court order. The administration extended the federal hiring freeze through mid-July and signed orders deregulating commercial fishing and opening marine monuments to review. Markets fell sharply on new chip export limits and Powell's warning, manufacturing orders hit their lowest level since April 2020, and China diverted purchases of oil, soybeans, aircraft, and beef away from American suppliers — hard evidence that the tariff turmoil begun over the weekend was inflicting real damage on the sectors it was meant to protect. The FCC chair threatened Comcast over its coverage of the Abrego Garcia case, a U.S.-born citizen remained jailed on an ICE detainer even after a judge authenticated his birth certificate, and a gunman killed at least two people at Florida State University, prompting Trump to say only that "the gun doesn't do the shooting, the people do."

The week closed with its most direct assault yet on judicial independence. FBI agents arrested Milwaukee County Circuit Judge Hannah Dugan, alleging she had helped a defendant evade a federal immigration arrest team inside her own courtroom; FBI Director Kash Patel posted a photograph of her in handcuffs captioned "No one is above the law." This was a separate matter from the Abrego Garcia and Boasberg proceedings — a different court, a different defendant, a different theory of liability — but arriving on the same Friday as those other threads, it read as the culmination of a week in which the executive branch had moved from resisting judicial orders to directly confronting the judges who issue them. That same day, Trump signed an executive order requiring documentary proof of citizenship to vote, and the House passed the SAVE Act imposing similar federal requirements, while legislatures in seven states advanced parallel restrictions. A federal judge blocked a policy that would have allowed rapid deportation of migrants to third countries without a chance to show they faced persecution. Judge Amy Berman Jackson blocked the CFPB layoffs a day after they had proceeded. The Office of Personnel Management moved to strip civil service protections from roughly 50,000 federal workers, and the Justice Department's Civil Rights Division was redirected away from its traditional protective mission toward pursuing claims of discrimination against white applicants and unauthorized voting. Trump instructed advisors to study whether he had legal authority to remove Powell. The IRS's acting commissioner was ousted days after his appointment, Pentagon aides were fired amid a leak investigation tied to disputed surveillance claims, and the official dismantling USAID was in turn fired by the secretary of state. By week's end, Van Hollen finally secured his meeting with Abrego Garcia in El Salvador and relayed a message to his wife back home.

Taken as a whole, the week did not produce a single dramatic rupture so much as an accumulation of tests, each probing whether existing checks on executive power still held. Courts at every level — a district judge, a chief judge finding probable cause for contempt, a conservative-majority appellate panel, another district judge blocking a deportation policy hours after it was announced — answered with unusual unanimity that they did not intend to yield. Universities, states, unions, and advocacy groups filed suit rather than comply. The Federal Reserve chair, in his own domain, said the same. Whether those institutional answers translate into enforceable consequences, or whether verbal defiance from the executive branch hardens into settled practice, is the question the week leaves behind — one no single ruling will resolve, but that will be answered by whether the pattern set this week continues, or breaks.
```
