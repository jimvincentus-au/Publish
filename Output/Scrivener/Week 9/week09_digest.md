The Week the Courts and the White House Went to War
Deportation flights defied a judge's order, a Cabinet department was ordered closed, and the Chief Justice broke his silence to defend judicial independence
```markdown
This was the week the tension between the executive branch and the judiciary stopped being theoretical. Over seven days, the administration deported hundreds of people under a rarely invoked wartime statute even after a federal judge ordered the flights turned back, the President called for that judge's impeachment, and the Chief Justice of the United States felt compelled to respond in public. By Friday, a Cabinet department had been ordered closed, a law firm had been coerced into concessions, and an agency head was threatening to shut down Social Security rather than comply with a court's data-access order. Few weeks of the year offer as clear a picture of how power moves when it meets resistance.
**The Weekend**
It began over the weekend of March 15 and 16, when the administration invoked the Alien Enemies Act of 1798 to deport roughly 250 Venezuelan and Salvadoran men to a mega-prison in El Salvador, bypassing individual hearings entirely. The government's evidence of gang membership included tattoos that turned out to carry no gang significance at all — in one case, a religious symbol inked on a gay makeup artist with no criminal record. Judge James Boasberg ordered the flights halted and directed that any planes already in the air be turned around. The administration argued the aircraft had already left U.S. airspace and were therefore beyond the court's reach, and the flights continued anyway. Whether that argument was a genuine legal position or a pretext for ignoring a judicial order became the defining question of the week.
Among those swept into the operation was Kilmar Ábrego García, a Maryland man who held a 2019 legal order specifically protecting him from deportation to El Salvador. He was sent to the same notorious prison, known as CECOT, as the others. The government first called this an "administrative error," then reversed course, labeling him a gang member without offering evidence and refusing to facilitate his return — a refusal that held even after courts, and eventually the Supreme Court, ordered the government to bring him back. The case would recur throughout the week as a stark example of an error the government declined to undo, even when instructed to by the nation's highest court.
The weekend carried other weight as well. Reporting revealed that Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth had shared detailed strike plans for a Yemen operation — targets, weapons systems, timing — over the commercial messaging app Signal, in a chat that inadvertently included a journalist. A second Signal chat, involving his wife and brother, contained similarly sensitive material. Separately, an executive order eliminated the US Agency for Global Media, the parent organization of Voice of America, Radio Free Europe/Radio Liberty, and Radio Free Asia, placing more than 1,300 VOA employees on leave and severing contracts with major wire services. Columbia University lost $400 million in federal funding over its handling of campus protests, the University of Maine had funding frozen over transgender athlete policy, and cybersecurity support for state and local election security was cut — three separate signals that federal money would now be wielded as leverage over institutional behavior.
**Monday**
The confrontation over the deportation flights sharpened into something more personal. Trump called Judge Boasberg a "Radical Left Lunatic" and demanded his impeachment over the restraining order. Chief Justice John Roberts, who rarely comments on political disputes, issued a public statement asserting that impeachment is not an appropriate response to disagreement with a judicial ruling. It was an extraordinary moment: the head of the judicial branch publicly defending a lower-court judge against the head of the executive branch.
The same day brought a cluster of other developments. Immigration agents detained Georgetown researcher Badar Khan Suri over alleged Hamas ties and pro-Palestinian social media activity, and separately detained longtime immigrant rights advocate Jeanette Vizguerra outside a Denver store. A Brown University professor was deported despite a judge's order requiring 48 hours' notice before removal. In Texas, a midwife became the first person criminally charged under the state's near-total abortion ban, facing felony counts that could carry decades in prison. And employees from Elon Musk's Department of Government Efficiency gained access to the US Institute of Peace after initially being denied entry, replacing leadership and canceling contracts at the independent, congressionally created institution — even as a separate federal judge ruled that DOGE was likely subject to Freedom of Information Act requests.
**Tuesday**
Tuesday brought a string of judicial setbacks for the administration. A federal judge blocked the transgender military service ban, calling it "unabashedly demeaning." Separate rulings protected USAID from further dismantlement by DOGE and temporarily blocked a $20 billion EPA climate grant clawback. Yet the same day, Rep. Brandon Gill formally introduced articles of impeachment against Judge Boasberg, asserting without support that he was "guilty of high crimes and misdemeanors" — translating Trump's rhetoric into actual legislative action. Radio Free Europe/Radio Liberty and the US Institute of Peace both filed lawsuits challenging the funding cuts and takeover attempts against them, testing whether courts would protect institutions built to operate independently of the White House. In a smaller but telling moment, Trump praised Judge Aileen Cannon, who had ruled in his favor in a classified documents case, calling her a model judge — a contrast that made his attacks on Boasberg look less like a matter of judicial philosophy than a matter of outcomes.
**Wednesday**
Wednesday shifted the week's center of gravity toward the machinery of federal government itself. Trump signed an executive order directing the Education Secretary to begin closing the Department of Education, an action critics immediately said required congressional approval that had never been sought. New York's attorney general announced a legal challenge, warning the move would disproportionately harm low-income and disabled students who rely on federal education protections. The same day, the Justice Department withdrew eleven pieces of guidance on Americans with Disabilities Act compliance, the EEOC and DOJ issued joint guidance recasting workplace diversity programs as potential civil rights violations, and the Department of Education found Maine in violation of Title IX over its policy allowing transgender athletes to compete.
In North Dakota, a jury ordered Greenpeace to pay Energy Transfer more than $660 million over its role in Dakota Access pipeline protests — a verdict civil liberties groups warned could chill protest activity nationwide, given its scale. A judge did bar the administration from deporting Badar Khan Suri while his case proceeded, but the Social Security Administration quietly froze a program that automatically issued Social Security numbers to work-authorized noncitizens — a change made without public announcement that risked overwhelming field offices and delaying benefits for millions.
**Thursday**
Thursday made the Department of Education order formal. Trump signed Executive Order 14242, declaring the agency had spent "$3 trillion with virtually nothing to show for it," and barred federal education funds from supporting diversity or "gender ideology" programs. The same day brought a cluster of additional executive orders: consolidating federal procurement under the General Services Administration, directing agencies to share unclassified data across government in the name of fighting "waste, fraud, and abuse," and accelerating mineral production on federal lands by bypassing standard environmental review. Each, in its own way, concentrated authority that had previously been distributed or reviewed.
The day's most consequential judicial check came from Judge Ellen Lipton Hollander, who issued a temporary restraining order blocking DOGE from accessing Social Security Administration data, calling the effort a "fishing expedition" undertaken without any articulated legal basis, and ordering the deletion of data DOGE had already obtained. Separately, ICE arrested Mahmoud Khalil, a green card holder and former Columbia graduate student, under a rarely used provision permitting deportation of noncitizens deemed to pose "adverse foreign policy consequences" — an arrest that quickly became a flashpoint in the broader campaign against campus protest. In a smaller moment that nonetheless underscored unresolved tensions around outside influence in government, Trump acknowledged that Elon Musk should not be briefed on war plans involving China, given Musk's business interests there.
**Friday**
By Friday, several threads converged. The Department of Homeland Security announced the revocation of temporary legal status for more than 530,000 immigrants from Cuba, Haiti, Nicaragua, and Venezuela, ending a Biden-era parole program effective April 24 and exposing all of them to potential deportation.
Acting Social Security Commissioner Leland Dudek responded to Judge Hollander's data-access ruling by threatening to shut down the agency's operations entirely, claiming — inaccurately, according to the judge herself — that the order required halting all of the agency's IT systems. Judge Hollander issued unusual public clarification letters insisting that benefit payments were not at risk and that Dudek's reading of her order was mistaken, an exchange that left open the question of whether the threat reflected confusion or resistance.
Trump also issued a memo directing the Attorney General to identify "partisan" lawsuits against the federal government and recommend sanctions against the attorneys and firms bringing them, along with a retroactive review of litigation conduct reaching back to 2017 — a move that followed the administration's earlier pressure campaign against the law firm Paul Weiss, which had been coerced into concessions after facing an executive order targeting its business. And the $1.7 trillion federal student loan portfolio, covering 42 million borrowers, was transferred from the Department of Education to the Small Business Administration, an agency that simultaneously announced a 43 percent reduction in its own workforce. The week's final act came quietly: federal funding supporting legal representation for 26,000 unaccompanied immigrant children was cut, with a White House memo describing immigration legal advocacy itself as "fraud."
Taken as a whole, the week did not turn on any single event so much as on a pattern repeated across immigration enforcement, agency restructuring, and the treatment of dissenting institutions: courts issued orders, and the administration either tested their limits, argued around them, or in some cases appeared simply to disregard them. Some judicial checks held — the transgender military ban stayed blocked, USAID retained court protection, and DOGE's access to Social Security data was constrained, at least for the moment. Others produced open confrontation, as with the deportation flights and the Social Security shutdown threat, where compliance itself became contested territory. The Chief Justice's willingness to publicly rebuke a sitting president over an attack on judicial independence was itself a measure of how far the week's events had traveled from ordinary political disagreement. What remained unresolved by Friday evening was not whether the courts could rule, but whether their rulings would be obeyed — a question that history suggests matters as much as any single policy decided along the way.
```
