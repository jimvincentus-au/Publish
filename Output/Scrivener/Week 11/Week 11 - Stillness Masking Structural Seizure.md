Week 11: Stillness Masking Structural Seizure
A near-flat clock reading concealed the week's true weight, as executive overreach on trade and tenure met real but reactive resistance.
The Wisconsin snow had barely melted when the week began, and it ended with the ground shaking under something larger. This was the week the clock's arithmetic, so often measured in single minutes, recorded almost no movement at all, even as consequential action reached some of the highest levels of the term. The stillness of the reading and the weight of the events did not match, and that mismatch is itself a lesson. Some weeks erode democracy through a single decisive blow. This one did it through accumulation, through five or six fronts opening at once, each testing a different wall.
At the close of the previous period, the Democracy Clock read 7:33 p.m. It closed this week at 7:33 p.m. as well, a net movement of two-tenths of a minute, essentially a holding pattern. But a holding pattern is not stillness. It is the balance point between severe deterioration on several fronts and real, sometimes bipartisan, resistance on others. The week saw a president openly test the constitutional limit on his own tenure, seize taxing power through a manufactured emergency, and preside over the largest two-day market collapse in history. It also saw a Senate majority vote against him, courts order remedies for admitted wrongful deportations, and voters reject a billionaire's attempt to buy a state supreme court seat. The clock's near-flat reading reflects that tension, not an absence of danger.
The week's most structurally significant act was economic. President Trump declared a national emergency over trade deficits and used it to impose sweeping tariffs on nearly every trading partner, a power the Constitution assigns to Congress. The number behind the tariffs came from a formula that divided a country's trade deficit by its imports, a method economists across the spectrum mocked as incoherent. Whether this reflected genuine confusion or a deliberate excuse to claim emergency power, the effect was the same: a president used a manufactured crisis to bypass the legislature on a matter of profound economic consequence.
The market did not wait to render judgment. Global stocks lost roughly $6.6 trillion in value over two days, the sharpest such decline in modern history. Companies responded fast. Stellantis and Whirlpool cut jobs within days, citing tariff costs directly. Cleveland-Cliffs idled two Minnesota plants and laid off more than six hundred steelworkers as auto orders collapsed. China retaliated with its own tariffs, export restrictions on rare earth minerals, and a blacklist of American defense contractors, widening a trade conflict that reached supply chains far beyond consumer goods.
Congress did not stay silent. A bipartisan Senate coalition, including Mitch McConnell, Rand Paul, Susan Collins, and Lisa Murkowski, voted 51 to 48 to rescind the Canada-specific tariffs. Senators Grassley and Cantwell introduced the Trade Review Act to require congressional approval of future presidential tariffs. The House declined to take up either measure. A small-business owner filed the first lawsuit challenging the tariffs as an unlawful bypass of statutory trade procedure. These were real checks, but partial ones. The tariffs stood. The precedent of a president seizing trade power remained intact, softened only by embarrassment, not by reversal. Fittingly, fossil fuel products were quietly exempted from the tariff package after the industry had given heavily to the president's campaign, a small irony sitting atop a much larger wound.
If the tariffs tested the boundary of economic power, the president's own words tested something deeper. Trump stated repeatedly, across multiple venues, that he was "not joking" about seeking a third term. He claimed unspecified "methods" existed to get around the 22nd Amendment. This was not a stray comment. Representative Andy Ogles introduced a formal resolution to amend the Constitution and permit a third term, moving the idea from rhetoric into legislative process. Attorney General Pam Bondi called the prospect "a heavy lift," a rare moment of public daylight between the president and his own chief law-enforcement officer. That daylight offered no real check. It showed only that even within the administration, some recognized how far this had gone.
The week's clearest test of due process centered on Kilmar Abrego Garcia, a legal resident deported to a Salvadoran prison. The administration admitted the removal was an "administrative error" but refused to arrange his return. Vice President Vance falsely claimed on social media that Abrego Garcia was a "convicted" MS-13 member, a claim without basis. Judge Paula Xinis ordered the government to bring him back, calling the deportation "wholly lawless." The White House response was not compliance but attack. Stephen Miller branded the judge a "Marxist," and House Republicans displayed "Wanted" posters of federal judges outside congressional offices. A government conceding its own error, then refusing the court-ordered remedy while smearing the judge who issued it, marks a specific and serious breach between the executive and the judiciary.
That breach widened elsewhere. Trump and House leaders coordinated attacks on the judiciary broadly, including calls to impeach Judge Boasberg and proposals to curb nationwide injunctions. In Wisconsin, federal agents arrested a sitting county judge, Hannah Dugan, over alleged interference with immigration enforcement, before formal charges were filed. Courts still found room to push back. A judge blocked termination of Temporary Protected Status for Venezuelan nationals, finding the government's reasoning discriminatory, and another halted the firing of CIA and ODNI employees connected to diversity programs. The judiciary was under open assault. It had not yet been silenced.
Immigration enforcement more broadly showed the same disregard for process. ICE circulated an internal "validation guide" that assigned gang-membership points for tattoos and clothing style, a system with no reliable evidentiary basis, used to justify removals under the Alien Enemies Act. Three U.S. citizen children, one battling stage 4 cancer, were deported to Honduras along with family members, according to a lawsuit filed by their attorneys. A Guatemalan worker with a valid permit was detained under inhumane conditions and lost his identifying documents in the process. A court did block cuts to legal aid for unaccompanied migrant children, a narrow preservation of representation amid a widening pattern of arbitrary removal.
Speech itself came under direct pressure. Secretary of State Rubio publicly defended revoking visas from students engaged in pro-Palestinian activism, and roughly three hundred such visas were revoked over the course of the week. The administration attempted to deport Mahmoud Khalil, a permanent resident, over his campus activism, and a court intervened to stop the detention of another Columbia student, Yunseo Chung, who alleged she was being targeted for her speech. USCIS announced it would monitor immigrants' social media for "antisemitic activity" tied to pro-Palestinian expression, extending surveillance into protected political speech. An attorney representing one of the student protesters was himself detained at a Detroit airport. Columbia alumni tore up their diplomas in protest, and international "Tesla Takedown" demonstrations spread to 253 cities. Resistance to these patterns was neither quiet nor confined to courtrooms.
Universities and law firms, the traditional guardians of independent thought and independent counsel, absorbed direct coercion this week. Columbia, having already lost $400 million in federal funding, placed an academic department under external receivership and restricted campus protest, a striking surrender of institutional self-governance. Major law firms, facing the threat of punitive executive orders, accepted settlements involving pro bono commitments and hiring restrictions favorable to the administration. Twenty-one state attorneys general organized to warn firms against capitulating, a genuine act of institutional solidarity, and former President Obama publicly urged universities, law firms, and citizens to resist federal pressure even at financial cost. The resistance was real. So was the coercion it was resisting.
Retaliation against disfavored officials and states followed a similar shape. The FHFA director referred New York Attorney General Letitia James, who had successfully sued Trump, for criminal investigation. An interim U.S. attorney opened an investigation into New Jersey Governor Phil Murphy over a lawful immigration policy disagreement. Maine lost federal education funding and, briefly, its vital-records contracts for newborn registration, both apparently in retaliation for Governor Mills' refusal to comply with federal demands. Public backlash forced a partial reversal of the Social Security cancellations. In a related case, a federal judge dismissed corruption charges against New York City Mayor Eric Adams with prejudice, noting the arrangement "smacks of a bargain" tied to his cooperation on immigration enforcement. Law, in each of these cases, worked less as a limit and more as a lever.
The press faced its own coordinated pressure. The FCC opened investigations into NBC, NPR, and PBS. Trump filed lawsuits against CBS News and the Des Moines Register over coverage and polling he disliked. The Associated Press remained barred from the Oval Office and Air Force One over a naming dispute. An executive order slashed federal funding for NPR and PBS, calling them purveyors of "radical woke propaganda," a formal defunding action that moved well past rhetorical complaint. Together, these steps described a single campaign, conducted through regulation, litigation, and appropriations, aimed at outlets seen as adversarial.
Elon Musk's intervention in the Wisconsin Supreme Court race deserves its own account, because it ended differently than most stories this week. Musk spent more than $20 million and personally handed cash payments to voters and petition-signers, an unprecedented and, under state law, legally dubious tactic in a judicial election. Despite this spending, voters elected the candidate Musk opposed. He responded by amplifying an unsubstantiated claim that the election had been "stolen." The defeat did not erase the danger of what he attempted, but it did show that money, even at this scale, does not automatically buy an outcome, and that fact is worth recording plainly.
Truthfulness suffered its own quiet collapse this week, spread across smaller moments that together formed a pattern. Trump amplified a fabricated video claiming Warren Buffett endorsed his tariff policy; Berkshire Hathaway denied it within hours. Press Secretary Leavitt made false claims about the criminal history of a wrongfully deported man. Economic officials gave contradictory statements about whether tariffs would raise consumer prices. A Navy secretary misstated the date of Pearl Harbor. The president himself mischaracterized the Declaration of Independence as being about "unity and love" rather than the right to resist tyranny. None of these lies, on its own, reshaped policy. Together, they described an administration for which correcting the record had become optional.
Stewardship of public institutions absorbed real damage. Roughly ten thousand employees were cut from HHS agencies including the CDC, FDA, and NIH, with officials later admitting a fifth of those cuts were made in error. The EPA terminated $14 billion in climate grants and moved to eliminate over a thousand scientist positions. Federal grants supporting libraries, museums, archives, and National History Day were terminated nationwide, and four hundred volunteer scientists were dismissed from the congressionally mandated national climate assessment. These were not symbolic cuts. They reduced the government's working capacity to track disease, protect the environment, and preserve public memory, damage that will outlast the week in which it occurred.
Congress, for its part, showed flickers of function beneath the dysfunction. Senator Cory Booker delivered a twenty-five-hour floor speech, the longest in Senate history, condemning the administration's direction. House Republican leadership attempted to block a proxy-voting accommodation for new parents and was defeated by a bipartisan revolt that stalled the chamber's business for the week. Neither event altered policy outcomes, but both showed that legislative dissent, though often symbolic, had not been extinguished.
The week closed, fittingly, with a scene that captured its contradictions. As markets reeled from the tariff shock, the president held a $1 million-per-person fundraising dinner at Mar-a-Lago for his super PAC, and skipped the dignified transfer ceremony for four fallen service members to attend a golf tournament instead. Public duty and private interest, so often blurred this term, were here placed side by side without disguise.
Nothing this week was hidden. The tariffs were announced from the Rose Garden. The third-term talk was said to television cameras. The judge's ruling was public record, as was the White House's attack on her. What made the week significant was not concealment but volume: the sheer number of fronts on which restraint gave way at once, met by a resistance that was real but reactive, capable of slowing specific actions without altering the larger direction. The clock did not move because nothing happened. It barely moved because too much did, in too many directions, for the ledger to settle cleanly in either column.
