# The Data Purge, the Redistricting War, and the Price of Loyalty

*A week in which economic statistics, university funding, and legislative quorums all became battlegrounds for executive power*

![Header image](week29-digest-featured.jpg)

```markdown
The first week of August opened with a jolt to the government's own numbers and closed, six days later, with a president formally moving to change how the country counts its people. In between, congressional maps became battlegrounds in two states, three universities settled with the federal government under financial duress while a fourth sued to resist, and immigration enforcement kept expanding even as the FBI's own crime data undercut the rhetoric used to justify it. Taken together, the week showed a recurring instinct: when official facts or institutional independence proved inconvenient, the response was to challenge, restructure, or bypass them.

**The Weekend**

It began with the Bureau of Labor Statistics reporting just 73,000 new jobs for July, along with sharp downward revisions to prior months. President Trump responded by firing BLS Commissioner Erika McEntarfer, asserting without evidence that the data had been manipulated to make him look bad. Economists warned that removing a nonpartisan statistician over unwelcome numbers could compromise confidence in government economic reporting for years to come. Trump paired the firing with public pressure on Federal Reserve Chair Jerome Powell to resign over interest rate policy — a demand he has no legal authority to enforce, but one that tested the norm of central bank independence nonetheless.

The same weekend, a separate confrontation was building in Texas. Roughly thirty House Democrats, a number that would grow past fifty, left the state for Illinois and elsewhere to deny Republicans the quorum needed to pass a new congressional map that could deliver the GOP five additional U.S. House seats. Governor Greg Abbott and Attorney General Ken Paxton threatened arrest, expulsion, and daily fines, casting the walkout as an abdication of duty tied to unrelated flood-relief legislation. It was the opening move in what would become the week's longest-running story.

Other threads emerged over the same two days. The Senate confirmed Jeanine Pirro, a former Fox host who had promoted 2020 election fraud claims, as the top federal prosecutor in Washington, D.C., on a party-line vote. The Office of Special Counsel confirmed it had opened a Hatch Act inquiry into former special counsel Jack Smith, based on a Republican senator's allegations. Trump announced a $200 million, donor-funded White House ballroom exempt from historic preservation review. A newly unclassified annex to the Durham report contradicted DNI Tulsi Gabbard's claims about the origins of 2016-era emails, confirming Russian hacking rather than Clinton campaign coordination. Trump also announced he had repositioned nuclear submarines in response to a Russian official's rhetoric and directed the Pentagon to prepare potential action against Latin American drug cartels. Meanwhile, courts advanced access to Epstein-related and immigration detention records, including a federal appeals court order to review sealed documents in the Giuffre v. Maxwell case.

**Monday**

The Justice Department escalated its own use of investigative power, announcing a new "strike force" to examine unsubstantiated claims that Obama administration officials conspired against Trump in 2016. Critics said the move effectively turned federal law enforcement toward relitigating a past election. The administration also expanded 287(g)-style programs allowing local police departments to enforce federal immigration law nationwide, a step that raised concerns about profiling and eroded trust between immigrant communities and local officers. Elsewhere, the administration moved to block abortion access at VA hospitals even in cases of rape or incest, and delayed a Medicare rule on "skin substitute" products after a company likely to benefit had donated $5 million to a pro-Trump PAC. A DHS directive tying disaster aid to a state's stance on Israel drew immediate criticism and was revised, then reversed, by day's end. DHS Secretary Kristi Noem also floated new detention centers near airport runways in several states, modeled on Florida's Everglades facility.

**Tuesday**

The spotlight shifted to the press. Trump filed a $10 billion lawsuit against Dow Jones over a Wall Street Journal report describing his past contribution to an Epstein birthday album, and separately asked a federal judge to compel Rupert Murdoch's deposition, citing Murdoch's age as grounds for urgency. The same day, a coalition of nonprofits, tribes, and local governments sued the EPA over the cancellation of a $3 billion congressionally mandated climate justice grant program, arguing the executive branch could not unilaterally nullify funding Congress had approved. The House Oversight Committee issued subpoenas to Bill and Hillary Clinton along with several former attorneys general and FBI directors, seeking depositions related to the Epstein case through October — a separate and distinct thread from Trump's litigation against the Journal. Elsewhere, the House Ethics Committee acknowledged it could not determine whether the wife of Representative Mike Kelly had traded on nonpublic information, citing her refusal to cooperate, and more than 40 protesters were arrested outside the Trump International Hotel in New York while demonstrating for expanded humanitarian aid to Gaza.

**Wednesday**

A striking contradiction came into public view. The FBI's annual crime report showed violent crime had fallen 4.5 percent nationally in 2024, reaching its lowest level since the 1960s — directly undercutting political claims of a crime wave under the prior administration. Yet the same day, ICE launched a sweeping recruitment campaign aimed at social media and college campuses, expanded detention capacity by 1,000 beds in Indiana, briefly offered and then canceled cash bonuses for rapid deportations, and began recruiting directly from local police departments. Trump also directed the Commerce Department to begin work on a new census that would exclude undocumented immigrants, a plan that could reshape congressional apportionment and federal funding despite the constitutional requirement to count all persons.

Separately, HHS Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr. announced the cancellation of roughly $500 million in mRNA vaccine research contracts, alarming researchers who see the technology as promising well beyond infectious disease. In a smaller but unsettling episode, sections of Article I of the Constitution — including habeas corpus provisions — briefly disappeared from the Library of Congress's Constitution Annotated website, attributed to a coding error and restored by day's end.

Courts pushed back in narrower ways: a federal judge halted new construction at the Everglades detention facility on environmental grounds, even as Border Patrol agents conducted an unmarked-truck raid outside a Los Angeles Home Depot, arresting 16 people despite a standing court order limiting such sweeps. A report from Senator Jon Ossoff's office documented 510 credible allegations of abuse at immigration detention facilities since January, including deaths in custody, while accusing DHS of obstructing oversight.

**Thursday**

The week's most crowded day was anchored by mounting financial pressure on universities. Brown reached a settlement adopting federal definitions of sex and granting the administration access to admissions data in exchange for restored funding. Columbia agreed to pay more than $220 million and accept an independent monitor following antisemitism allegations. UCLA remained under a funding freeze exceeding $300 million despite settling a separate lawsuit over pro-Palestinian campus protests. Harvard, by contrast, sued the administration over $2.6 billion in funding cuts and the revocation of its ability to host international students, notching early court victories while negotiations continued — the sharpest institutional resistance of the week to what critics described as coercive ideological conditions on federal funding.

The redistricting fight also escalated sharply. The Texas House voted to authorize civil arrest warrants for the absent Democratic lawmakers, while Vice President JD Vance traveled to Indiana to press Republican legislators there to redraw their own congressional map, prompting protests at the statehouse. What had begun as a single-state standoff was now a coordinated, multi-state effort to redraw districts mid-decade for partisan advantage.

The same day, Trump signed a cluster of executive orders touching homelessness policy, university admissions data, retirement investing, and banking — opening 401(k) plans to cryptocurrency and private equity, directing crackdowns on public camping, requiring universities to report race-neutral admissions data, and addressing alleged "debanking" of conservatives, alongside a separate order tightening political review of federal grantmaking. Trump also threatened to federalize Washington, D.C.'s government after an alleged assault on a former DOGE staffer, despite the FBI's own data showing violent crime in the city had fallen sharply.

Elsewhere that day, the administration awarded a $231.8 million contract to build a 5,000-bed detention facility at Fort Bliss, a planned Epstein messaging meeting among top officials was canceled after details leaked, a bomb threat forced the evacuation of Texas lawmakers sheltering in Illinois, constituents confronted Nebraska Representative Mike Flood with chants of "Vote him out" over immigration policy, commentators noted that major outlets had given limited coverage to the FBI's crime-decline report, the Army announced plans to restore a Confederate memorial at Arlington National Cemetery, and the Air Force denied previously approved early retirement to transgender service members with 15 to 18 years of service.

**Friday**

The week closed with formal confirmation of the census plan: Trump stated directly that he had directed the Commerce Department to proceed with excluding undocumented immigrants from the count, a move critics said conflicted with the constitutional mandate to count all persons regardless of status. The legal group Democracy Forward sued the DOJ and FBI for records related to the Epstein investigation, including any communications between Epstein and Trump, after FOIA requests went unanswered — continuing the separate subpoena and disclosure track from earlier in the week.

A new round of tariffs on more than 60 countries took effect, expected to raise consumer prices even as broader economic indicators showed slowing growth. The administration approved the Paramount-Skydance media merger shortly after CBS canceled Stephen Colbert's Late Show following his on-air criticism of the network's settlement with Trump, a sequence that drew scrutiny over the timing.

Protests continued on multiple fronts: demonstrators were arrested outside a New York immigration court over an alleged covert ICE detention site, crowds gathered again at the Indiana Statehouse against redistricting, and the Secret Service faced questions after asking the Army Corps of Engineers to raise river levels for a Vance family boating trip. HHS confirmed its "coordinated wind-down" of the mRNA vaccine contracts announced earlier in the week, and leaked drafts of a State Department human rights report showed softened language on El Salvador, Israel, and Russia despite documented abuses in each.

**Looking back**

By the week's end, the through-lines were unmistakable. A jobs report led to the removal of the official who produced it. A congressional map fight in Texas spread to Indiana within days. Universities negotiated under financial duress or sued to resist it. Immigration enforcement expanded on the strength of public-safety arguments that the government's own crime statistics did not support. And a lawsuit against a newspaper sat alongside congressional subpoenas and public records suits, each seeking a different kind of accounting for the same unresolved case. None of these developments occurred in isolation; each tested, in its own way, whether independent data, courts, universities, the press, and elected bodies could hold their footing under sustained pressure from the executive branch. The record of the week suggests that pressure was constant, resistance was real, and the outcomes remained unsettled.
```
