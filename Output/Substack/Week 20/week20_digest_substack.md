# This Week in Democracy: Tariffs, Travel Bans, and a Public Feud Over Power

*A Supreme Court that keeps saying yes to the executive, a budget bill that reshapes the safety net, and a president-billionaire falling-out that exposes how personal government has become*

![Header image](week20-digest-featured.jpg)

```markdown
This was a week that moved on several tracks at once, each reinforcing the others. The Supreme Court repeatedly sided with executive power even as lower courts pushed back on it. The president doubled tariffs and signed a sweeping new travel ban. The House narrowly passed a budget bill that will touch millions of Americans' health coverage and food assistance. Federal disaster agencies entered hurricane season stripped of staff. And a public feud between the president and the world's richest man exposed just how thoroughly private wealth and government power had become entangled.

The weekend set the tone. Trump announced a doubling of steel and aluminum tariffs to 50 percent, timed to a Pennsylvania rally celebrating a Nippon Steel investment deal, and formalized the increase by executive order days later. The move added fresh uncertainty to trade relationships, including a recently negotiated tariff reduction with the United Kingdom. At nearly the same time, the U.S. Court of International Trade ruled that Trump's broader "Liberation Day" tariffs were illegal, finding that only Congress has the constitutional authority to levy tariffs — but the Federal Circuit paused that ruling on appeal, letting the tariffs stand while the case proceeded. The dispute went straight to the question of who actually controls American trade policy, and it remained unresolved by week's end.

The Supreme Court also acted early, allowing the administration to end parole protections for roughly 500,000 people from Cuba, Haiti, Nicaragua, and Venezuela, and separately ending Temporary Protected Status for Venezuelans. Both rulings stripped legal status from enormous populations while their court challenges were still pending — part of a pattern in which the Court allowed policy to take effect before the underlying legal questions were settled. That same weekend, federal officers entered Representative Jerry Nadler's Manhattan office and handcuffed a staff member, falsely accusing him of harboring a migrant. Nadler and other Democrats called it intimidation, and it would not be an isolated incident. Meanwhile, the House passed a budget package cutting Medicaid and SNAP while adding work requirements and increasing border and detention funding by $160 billion — the opening move in what would become the week's central legislative story.

By Monday, courts were pushing back forcefully in several directions. Judges blocked the Department of Homeland Security from ending TSA officers' collective bargaining rights, halted the deportation of a detained student pending review, ordered continued treatment access for a plaintiff class, and reaffirmed limits on retaliation against the law firm Jenner & Block. Even as the Supreme Court moved to expand executive latitude, lower courts were drawing lines around it. That same day, a man threw an incendiary device into a rally supporting Israeli hostages in Boulder, Colorado, injuring eight people; the FBI treated it as terrorism and a hate crime, and the administration moved almost immediately to fold the attack into its argument for tougher deportation policy. Monday also brought unsettling news on disaster preparedness: staffing cuts and a hiring freeze had left more than a dozen Gulf Coast National Weather Service offices understaffed, and the new acting head of FEMA reportedly did not know the United States has a hurricane season — the first of several alarming disclosures that week about the state of federal disaster infrastructure. Enforcement actions continued as well, with ICE agents raiding a San Diego restaurant using flash-bang grenades to disperse protesters, and the arrest of international student Rümeysa Öztürk over a campus op-ed about Gaza.

Tuesday's dominant story was the House's passage of Trump's "One Big Beautiful Bill," which combined major cuts to healthcare and social programs with a provision preempting state and local regulation of artificial intelligence for a full decade — a sweeping removal of state authority over a fast-moving technology, with almost no offsetting federal oversight built in. The same day, the administration explicitly connected the Boulder attack, committed by a man who had overstayed his visa, to calls from Trump and Stephen Miller for stricter deportation policy and a revival of travel-ban style restrictions. Elsewhere, nineteen corporations — including Anheuser-Busch, Toyota, Booz Allen Hamilton, and Deloitte — withdrew Pride sponsorships, leaving funding gaps in cities including New York and Washington. Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth ordered the Navy to strip Harvey Milk's name from a ship during Pride month, part of a broader campaign to rename vessels honoring historical figures in favor of what he called warrior-culture branding. And two developments raised concerns about the integrity of federal science: an executive order reportedly granting political appointees new authority to override research findings, and a proposed 43 percent cut to National Institutes of Health funding that would threaten cancer and biomedical research nationwide.

Wednesday brought the first open break between Trump and Elon Musk. After Musk publicly denounced the spending bill, Trump suggested Musk's government contracts and subsidies could be cancelled; SpaceX responded by announcing it would begin decommissioning the Dragon spacecraft used to resupply the International Space Station. The exchange revealed, in stark terms, how deeply a private billionaire's business empire had become woven into core federal operations — including the country's access to space. The same day, courts continued reinforcing due-process protections: a federal judge ruled that more than 100 migrants sent to a Salvadoran prison must be allowed to challenge their deportations, another ordered the return of a wrongly deported Guatemalan asylum seeker, and a third temporarily blocked deportation of the Boulder suspect's family. The administration also threatened to withdraw billions in federal funding from California's high-speed rail project, giving the state 30 days to demonstrate compliance, while further FEMA cuts emerged — nearly $4 billion in cancelled infrastructure resilience grants and a proposal to eliminate the agency outright, compounding the staffing concerns raised days earlier. A House Democrat called for subpoenaing Musk over alleged drug use during his time inside the administration, an early sign that the rift was becoming a matter of formal oversight rather than private grievance.

The week's final stretch, spanning Thursday into Friday, carried the heaviest concentration of consequential developments. Trump signed a proclamation restricting entry from 19 countries effective June 9, reviving and expanding first-term travel-ban policy and drawing warnings about family separation and discriminatory impact — a direct continuation of the rhetoric that had followed the Boulder attack earlier in the week. Separately, Trump signed an order suspending entry of foreign nationals seeking to study at Harvard, escalating his conflict with the university; a federal judge blocked the policy almost immediately, finding it would cause irreparable harm. The administration also opened an investigation, led by Attorney General Pam Bondi, into whether aides had effectively run the government during Biden's presidency due to alleged cognitive decline, while the House Oversight Committee sought testimony from former Biden aides and the Justice Department opened a separate inquiry into Biden's pardons — an unusually direct use of executive and prosecutorial power against a predecessor's legitimacy.

The reconciliation bill's passage in the House, by a single vote, 215-214, was among the week's most consequential developments. It extended the 2017 tax cuts, funded a major expansion of immigration enforcement, and cut Medicaid and food assistance, even as the Congressional Budget Office projected it would add trillions to the deficit and cause millions to lose health coverage — directly contradicting the administration's public claims. Passed on the thinnest possible margin, the bill now heads to the Senate as the week's largest structural reshaping of the federal safety net.

The Trump-Musk rupture reached its most explosive point on Friday. Trump publicly criticized Musk and again raised the possibility of cancelling his government contracts; Musk responded by claiming Trump appeared in files related to Jeffrey Epstein, a claim that drew immediate national attention before the dispute began to cool. Whatever its ultimate resolution, the episode left a lasting record of how entangled personal loyalty, private fortune, and federal contracting authority had become at the center of government.

The Supreme Court closed the week with a cluster of major rulings issued in a single sitting: it struck down Mexico's lawsuit against American gunmakers, expanded a religious tax exemption for Catholic Charities, eased the path for reverse-discrimination claims in Ames v. Ohio, and allowed the Department of Government Efficiency to continue accessing Social Security records while litigation proceeds. Each ruling shifts legal terrain that will outlast this particular week — on corporate liability, religious exemption, discrimination law, and executive access to Americans' private data.

Enforcement actions also escalated on the ground. David Huerta, president of the Service Employees International Union, was arrested and hospitalized during an immigration raid on a Los Angeles garment factory; the Department of Homeland Security maintained he had assaulted an officer, despite video evidence showing officers using force against him. It was the most prominent instance yet of enforcement activity touching a national labor leader rather than an individual immigrant, following the same week's incidents at Nadler's office and the San Diego restaurant raid. Separately, a federal appeals court allowed the administration to continue barring the Associated Press from the Oval Office, Air Force One, and Mar-a-Lago while litigation proceeds, narrowing press access to the presidency even as it preserved some access to East Room events. And the administration announced it would no longer enforce federal guidance requiring emergency rooms to perform necessary abortions under emergency care law, narrowing federal protection for reproductive care nationwide.

Taken as a whole, the week showed two branches of government pulling in different directions on the same underlying question: how much latitude the executive should have to act unilaterally, and how quickly. The Supreme Court extended that latitude on immigration status, government data access, and workplace removals; district and appellate courts kept insisting that due process, contractual rights, and institutional independence still bound executive action, even under pressure. Congress, for its part, used its narrowest possible majority to pass a bill that will reallocate resources across the entire country for years. And the public dispute between a president and a businessman whose companies now perform core government functions offered an unusually plain illustration of how personal, financial, and political power had come to occupy the same space — a condition future historians will likely find easier to name than to reverse.
```
