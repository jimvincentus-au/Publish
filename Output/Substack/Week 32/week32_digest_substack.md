# A Government Turns Inward: Troops, Firings, and the Machinery of Control

*In one week, the administration moved against a Fed governor, a CDC director, city governments, protest rights, and labor protections — while courts and dissenters pushed back at nearly every turn.*

![Header image](week32-digest-featured.jpg)

```markdown
The week of August 23 to 29 opened with confrontation and closed with a coordinated rollback of labor protections, but its throughline was a government testing the limits of executive power on nearly every front — military, financial, judicial, and administrative — while courts, juries, and state officials pushed back at almost every turn.

**The Weekend**

The weekend set the tone. The administration ordered a stop-work halt on the Revolution Wind project off New England, an offshore wind installation more than 80 percent complete, part of a broader retreat from clean energy that drew criticism from state officials and environmental groups. Director of National Intelligence Tulsi Gabbard announced a 40 percent cut to her office's foreign-influence monitoring unit, reducing a key safeguard against election interference. President Trump demanded Colorado release Tina Peters, the election official convicted of equipment breaches, threatening unspecified "harsh measures" if she remained jailed — a direct presidential challenge to a state criminal conviction.

The Justice Department opened investigations into former FBI Director James Comey and former CIA Director John Brennan over 2016 Russia-era statements, while FBI agents raided John Bolton's home and office in an early-morning search tied to classified-information handling. Attorney General Pam Bondi extended this pattern, opening probes into Senator Adam Schiff, New York Attorney General Letitia James, and Federal Reserve Governor Lisa Cook, based on mortgage-fraud allegations pushed by a Trump loyalist. Trump then demanded Cook's resignation outright; she refused, calling it bullying and vowing to fight.

Separately, following a call with Vladimir Putin in which Trump repeated discredited claims about the 2020 election, he announced that lawyers were drafting an executive order to eliminate mail-in voting nationwide — a method used by nearly a third of American voters. Trump also called on the FCC to revoke the broadcast licenses of ABC and NBC over critical coverage. Meanwhile, reports confirmed Pentagon planning for a National Guard deployment to Chicago without any request from Illinois officials, and Trump threatened troops for Baltimore after reading Governor Wes Moore's invitation as a provocation. In Washington, Defense Secretary Hegseth announced that Guard troops would carry weapons, and Trump signaled interest in extending the deployment beyond its legal 30-day limit.

Amid all this, the Justice Department gave Congress a limited tranche of Epstein-related documents — 33,000 pages that Democrats said were 97 percent already public — while Ghislaine Maxwell's transfer to a minimum-security facility drew separate criticism. A wave of younger Democratic primary challengers, including a populist Senate bid by Maine oysterman Graham Platner against Susan Collins, showed competitive politics continuing beneath the turbulence.

**Monday**

By Monday, the pace of executive action became the defining feature of the week. Trump signed twin executive orders ending cashless bail nationwide, directing the Attorney General to identify noncompliant jurisdictions and threatening to cut federal funding within 30 days, with specific measures aimed at Washington, D.C. He also signed an order directing prosecution of flag burners, explicitly contradicting the Supreme Court's 1989 *Texas v. Johnson* ruling, and extending immigration consequences to non-citizens who burned flags. Trump then declared a crime emergency in Washington, D.C., despite Justice Department data showing crime at a 30-year low, mandating additional federal prosecutors, specialized Guard units, and housing inspections in the capital.

That same day, he moved to fire Lisa Cook "for cause," based on unproven allegations; Cook, appointed by President Biden and never charged with any crime, said Trump lacked legal authority and vowed to contest the removal in court.

Courts pushed back forcefully on other fronts. In Utah, Judge Dianna Gibson struck down the state's Republican-drawn congressional maps as an unlawful gerrymander violating a 2018 voter initiative, ordering new maps by September 24. The administration responded by signaling its own lawsuit against California to block its redistricting move, framing it as a countermeasure to Texas's map changes — turning redistricting into a national legal battleground on multiple fronts simultaneously. A federal judge halted the deportation of Kilmar Abrego Garcia to Uganda after his lawyers alleged the Justice Department had threatened deportation unless he pleaded guilty to smuggling charges, offering Costa Rica as an alternative. And in a sharp rebuke of executive overreach, a federal judge dismissed the administration's lawsuit against all 15 Maryland district judges, citing absolute judicial immunity and criticizing the suit as unprecedented.

Governors Newsom, Pritzker, and Moore publicly pushed back against federal pressure — Newsom filing a FOIA request over agents at a Japanese American museum, Pritzker rejecting the Chicago deployment as a manufactured crisis, and Moore mocking the administration's rhetoric on Baltimore. Separately, more than 180 FEMA employees signed a letter warning Congress of Katrina-level disaster-response risk due to mismanagement and staffing losses; roughly 30 were later placed on administrative leave.

**Tuesday**

On Tuesday, the National Guard question sharpened further. Trump signed an order directing Defense Secretary Hegseth to create specialized Guard units trained for domestic "public order" enforcement, widely read as preparation for deployment in Democratic-led cities. The NAACP filed suit against Texas's newly redrawn congressional maps, alleging the lines diluted Black voting power in violation of the Voting Rights Act. A North Carolina court issued a ruling addressing allegations that certain election procedures had disenfranchised voters.

Governor Pritzker escalated his opposition to a potential Chicago deployment, calling it unprecedented overreach and signaling readiness to sue, while criticizing the White House for a lack of communication. By day's end, Trump indicated he might act unilaterally, federalizing Guard troops for Chicago without local consent — extending a pattern already seen in Los Angeles and Washington.

**Wednesday**

Wednesday brought a new institutional crisis into view. CDC Director Susan Monarez was removed amid a dispute with Health and Human Services Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr. over vaccine policy; her legal team argued that only the president, not the secretary, held authority to remove a Senate-confirmed official. Her ouster triggered a wave of senior resignations that would deepen over the following two days.

Elsewhere, the administration sent letters to 46 states and Washington, D.C., threatening to cut federal funding unless transgender references were removed from sex-education curricula within 60 days. In Florida, Judge Kathleen Williams ordered the "Alligator Alcatraz" immigration detention facility in the Everglades dismantled within 60 days over environmental and human-rights concerns; the state began compliance despite an appeal. Judge Paula Xinis extended protection for Kilmar Abrego Garcia, barring his deportation until at least October and ordering administration officials to testify about efforts to remove him again.

A D.C. grand jury declined to indict Sean Charles Dunn, who had thrown a sandwich at a Customs and Border Protection agent during a protest against troop deployment — a symbolic rebuke of the federal presence in the capital. In Georgia, a judge fined the Fulton County Commission $10,000 a day for refusing to appoint two Republican nominees tied to election denialism to the county election board. And in special elections, Democrats posted notable results: Debra Shigley forced a runoff in a traditionally Republican Georgia Senate district, and Catelin Drey flipped an Iowa Senate seat in territory Trump had carried by more than eleven points.

**Thursday**

The CDC crisis reached its full scale on Thursday, when the White House confirmed Monarez's firing despite her legal objections, and four senior officials — Debra Houry, Demetre Daskalakis, Daniel Jernigan, and Jennifer Layden — resigned in protest, citing political interference and the manipulation of scientific data. It was one of the most significant single-agency leadership collapses of the year.

The same day, Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy announced a federal takeover of Union Station under a "beautification" initiative, extending the administration's direct control over another civic landmark in the capital. Trump signed a further executive order narrowing federal labor-management rules, excluding additional agency units from collective bargaining on national-security grounds.

In Washington state, ICE agents arrested two firefighters mid-response to the Bear Gulch wildfire, requiring crew members to show identification — an incident that drew broad condemnation for disrupting emergency operations. Separately, ICE transferred an 18-year-old detainee, Benjamin Guerrero-Cruz, between facilities in California and Arizona without notifying his family, nearly deporting him to Louisiana before a California congresswoman introduced legislation requiring 24-hour notice in such cases. A whistleblower alleged that a former senior DOGE official had copied sensitive Social Security data belonging to more than 300 million Americans onto an unsecured cloud server.

Reporting also showed National Guard troops in Washington spending their time on tasks like mulching and trash pickup rather than addressing crime, which remained at a 30-year low — underscoring the political rather than operational character of the deployment. The week's news also included a $1.1 billion cut to public broadcasting funding, which threatened emergency-alert capacity in rural communities.

**Friday**

Friday closed the week with a coordinated rollback of labor protections that touched several vulnerable worker populations in a single day. The administration proposed ending federal minimum wage and overtime protections for 3.7 million childcare and home care workers, rescinded minimum wage requirements for disabled workers, moved to strip collective bargaining rights from roughly one million federal employees, and cut the minimum wage for federal contractors from $17.75 to $13.30 an hour. Taken together, these actions represented one of the most significant economic policy shifts of the week, arriving alongside — rather than separate from — the period's institutional confrontations.

The same day, Trump signed an order ending the de minimis tariff exemption for packages under $800, a change expected to add up to $13 billion in costs and shipping delays, prompting postal suspensions from nearly 30 countries. He also revoked former Vice President Kamala Harris's extended Secret Service protection, which President Biden had authorized before leaving office, drawing criticism from California officials over her continued safety. The Pentagon announced the restoration of a portrait of Robert E. Lee at West Point, reversing a 2020 law that had mandated removal of Confederate tributes from military installations. A proposed rule would bar VA doctors from performing abortions for veterans even in cases of rape or incest, narrowing services established under the previous administration.

In Florida, a court denied a stay motion in the "Alligator Alcatraz" case, confirming that closure would proceed despite state resistance. And organizers announced plans for a September 6 march in Washington demanding withdrawal of federal troops, alongside a Raleigh die-in protest and a nationwide "Workers Over Billionaires" campaign — civic responses aimed squarely at the week's dominant themes.

Viewed as a whole, the week showed a government reaching simultaneously into monetary policy, public health leadership, municipal governance, labor law, and voting procedure, often bypassing legislative process through executive order and funding threats. But it was not a week of unchecked expansion. Federal judges blocked a deportation, dismissed a suit against an entire state judiciary, ordered a gerrymandered map redrawn, and shut down a controversial detention facility. A grand jury declined to criminalize protest. State officials threatened litigation rather than compliance. Career officials at the CDC resigned rather than execute directives they viewed as scientifically indefensible. The week's tension was not between calm and crisis, but between an expanding executive and the institutions — judicial, professional, and civic — built to resist it, each testing, in real time, how far the other would go.
```
