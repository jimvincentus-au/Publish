Week 72: The Clock Holds, Barely
Force, money, and quiet rule changes met real judicial and congressional pushback, leaving the week's balance of power almost exactly where it began.
Week 72 opens on a familiar arrangement: force applied at the edges, money moved at the center, courts holding, in places, where politics will not. The period from May 30 to June 5, 2026, adds no new mechanism to the machinery of power; it confirms the ones already running. The Democracy Clock, built to measure the distance between a government of laws and a government of men, barely moves this week. But barely is not nothing. It is the sound of a system settling into a posture, rather than lurching toward one.
At the close of the previous period, the clock stood at 7:42 p.m. This week it moved to 7:41 p.m., a shift of two-tenths of a minute. The movement was small because the week's forces mostly canceled each other out. Executive pressure on oversight, civil service protections, protest rights, and voting access pushed the hour later. Judicial rulings, a Kennedy Center order that stuck, and a rare bipartisan retreat on a contested slush fund pulled it back. The result was a week that neither collapsed nor recovered. It held, uneasily, in place.
The week's clearest throughline was force used against people who were watching. At Delaney Hall in Newark, New Jersey State Police pepper-sprayed protesters and members of Congress alike, including a sitting senator. A photojournalist was targeted directly by a flash-bang grenade; he said he had never experienced that before. Governor Sherrill then imposed a half-mile "free speech zone" around the facility, and Newark's mayor added an indefinite curfew. Within a day, riot police cleared dozens of protesters from the streets in minutes.
Read in isolation, the sequence looks like a local law-and-order story. Read as a whole, it is something else: an entire toolkit of suppression, aimed at people trying to see what their government does to detainees. Curfews control movement. Zoning controls visibility. Chemical agents control bodies. Together they pushed a basic democratic function, the right to observe and object, further out of reach. Some concessions followed: family visitation was partly restored, and the state later added funding for immigration legal aid. But the concessions came only after force had already done its work.
Behind the protest line, the conditions being protested worsened on their own terms. ICE quietly ended a rule requiring it to report detainee deaths occurring within 30 days of release, closing a window that once caught deaths tied to custody but occurring just outside it. A detainee at Winn Correctional Center died despite no criminal record. Children in a Texas facility were reportedly fed food containing worms. At "Alligator Alcatraz" in Florida, detainees were denied food and water until they signed documents in a language many could not read. None of this required a new law; it required only the quiet removal of a reporting requirement. The world simply stopped needing to know.
A civil service order, signed this week, cut deeper than either of these episodes. Trump stripped job protections from roughly eight to ten thousand senior federal employees, turning a professional, tenured workforce into an at-will one. Paired with an Office of Management and Budget proposal letting political appointees vet and kill federal research grants, the order marks a shift from occasional loyalty tests to permanent ones. Career officials who once served across administrations now serve at the pleasure of this one. Grant reviewers who once answered to peer scientists may soon answer to political appointees instead. MIT had already reported a twenty percent decline in federally funded research. The order gives that decline a mechanism, not just a cause.
Money moved in familiar directions this week, toward people close to the president rather than away from them. The Pentagon awarded Dell a $9.7 billion contract shortly after Trump personally bought and publicly promoted Dell stock. A separate defense-funding decision benefited a drone company where Donald Trump Jr. serves as adviser and shareholder, with a White House aide intervening directly in the loan process. By week's end, reporting surfaced that more than $50 billion in federal contracts had gone to fourteen corporate donors of the president's ballroom project. None of these arrangements were secret. They were simply left undisturbed.
Trump himself was fined $200 for failing to disclose tens of millions of dollars in stock trades. The number is so small next to the underlying conduct that it works less as a penalty than as a confirmation: the rules exist, and they do not bind him in any way that costs him anything. Financial accountability, for elites closest to power, remained a formality rather than a constraint.
Against this backdrop, the week's most contested storyline concerned the so-called Anti-Weaponization Fund, a $1.776 billion pool intended to compensate Trump allies, potentially including January 6 defendants. Republican senators privately pressed to kill it. Two Capitol Police officers who had defended the building on January 6 sued to block it. Mike Pence, breaking publicly with the administration, called it "deeply offensive." Under this pressure, the administration announced it would drop the fund. But a Senate amendment to permanently bar it failed narrowly, 49 to 50. By week's end, a reconciliation bill had funded ICE and Border Patrol through the end of the president's term with no restrictions on the fund's revival. The fund is not dead. It is merely unannounced.
Courts, this week, did real work. A federal panel struck down Alabama's congressional map as intentionally discriminatory. Judge Christopher Cooper ruled the Kennedy Center could not be renamed without an act of Congress and blocked its closure. The institution complied within days, stripping Trump's name from its signage even as he attacked the judge on social media. The D.C. Circuit found the Pentagon's transgender service ban likely unconstitutional. The Supreme Court itself struck down an executive order denying birthright citizenship. These were not small rulings. They were the clearest evidence this week that judicial independence, however strained, still produces outcomes the executive cannot simply overturn by saying so.
Then, the same week, in a separate case, the same Supreme Court allowed Alabama's discriminatory map to stand for the midterms after all. The 6-3 ruling overturned a unanimous lower-court finding of discriminatory intent against Black voters. The contradiction is not really a contradiction; it is a court whose institutional check operates unevenly, protecting some constitutional guarantees while permitting the quiet erosion of others. Voting rights lost ground this week even as citizenship rights held.
Congress showed a similar split personality. The House passed a war powers resolution directing Trump to end hostilities with Iran or seek authorization, and advanced Ukraine aid over leadership's objection. Both were genuine assertions of legislative independence. Senate Republicans stripped a billion dollars in ballroom security funding from a reconciliation bill, a small but real check on vanity spending. Yet the same Senate passed a seventy-billion-dollar immigration enforcement package funding ICE through the end of the term, with no guardrails on the very fund it had just claimed to abandon. Congress can assert itself. It has not yet learned to sustain the assertion.
Beneath the week's headline fights ran a quieter economic story. Inflation climbed to its highest level in nearly three years. Treasury Secretary Bessent and economic adviser Kevin Hassett dismissed the concern as partisan framing rather than lived hardship, even as the administration quietly cut tariffs on farm and construction equipment, an implicit admission that tariffs had been raising prices all along. Rural voters in Texas, meanwhile, signaled new willingness to vote Democratic over the strain that data centers were placing on local farmland and water. The gap between what officials said and what households felt did not close this week. It widened.
Elsewhere, the machinery of information control turned in smaller, stranger ways. The Pentagon reclassified its own press office as a restricted facility, barring journalists from a space that had long been a basic point of access. The USDA deleted a public notice about a second screwworm detection in Texas. The White House declined to say whether Trump still uses a hair-loss medication, citing no obligation to disclose. None of these acts, alone, would register as significant. Together, they describe a government growing more comfortable withholding ordinary information for no stated reason at all.
The president's own public conduct added to a pattern of unresolved questions rather than dispelling them. He made contradictory statements about Iran's military capabilities within the same interview. He disappeared from public view for six days amid active war. A late-night medical memo claimed an "AI-enhanced" assessment of his cardiac age. None of this crystallized into formal action. It simply accumulated, the way small oddities do, until they form a shape no single event can explain.
Two appointments this week deserve to be read together. Bill Pulte, a mega-donor with no intelligence background and a documented history of using regulatory power against political opponents, was installed as acting Director of National Intelligence, explicitly tasked with investigating "rigged elections." Elsewhere in national security, a convicted January 6 rioter was placed in a sensitive counterterrorism role. Loyalty, in both cases, substituted for qualification. The intelligence community, built to operate at a remove from partisan grievance, found itself instead absorbing it directly.
By week's end, DOJ had sent a federal prosecutor to observe California's routine ballot processing, after Trump posted unsubstantiated claims that Democrats were stealing gubernatorial and mayoral primary races. Ron DeSantis amplified the claims. No evidence supported them. But federal investigative weight followed anyway, lending institutional legitimacy to allegations that independent journalists and election officials had already shown to be baseless. The pattern is not new. Its persistence is the story.
What the week leaves behind is not a single dramatic turn but a ledger of small, specific costs: a curfew here, a stripped protection there, a fund quietly kept alive under a different name. The courts held some lines and let others go. Congress asserted itself on war powers and let enforcement funding pass unchecked in the same span of days. None of it resolved. Little of it was reversed. The week's stillness on the clock face reflects not calm, but a balance struck between forces still very much in motion, each testing how far it can go before something gives.
