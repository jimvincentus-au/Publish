Week 70: The Fund That Would Not Yield
A settlement built a $1.776 billion executive fund and permanent tax immunity, testing whether resistance can catch harm before it hardens into law.
Week 70 opened on a settlement, and settlements are usually quiet things. This one was not. In the space of six days, the machinery of federal law enforcement became an instrument for shielding a sitting president from the very laws it exists to enforce. Congress, the courts, and even members of the president's own party found themselves scrambling to respond to something that had already taken effect before they could act. The Democracy Clock has tracked such moments before. This week it registered one more, not through drama but through the sheer mechanical fact of what had been built, and who now controlled it.
At the close of the previous period the clock stood at 7:42 p.m. It remains there now, unmoved by measure though not by weight. The lack of movement should not be mistaken for calm. It reflects an unusual week in which severe institutional damage and unusually forceful institutional resistance arrived together, in nearly matched proportion. The fund that gives this week its name advanced regardless of the resistance it drew. But the resistance was real, cross-partisan, and in several cases immediate. The clock's stillness marks a rare balance, not an absence of consequence.
The central event was a settlement. Trump had sued the IRS for ten billion dollars. In exchange for dropping the suit, the Department of Justice created a fund of $1.776 billion, drawn from the Judgment Fund, without congressional appropriation. Todd Blanche, the acting deputy attorney general, then signed an order permanently barring the IRS from auditing or prosecuting Trump, his family, or affiliated companies. No statute authorized this. No precedent supported it. A former IRS commissioner called the arrangement without parallel in the agency's history. Commissioners the president could remove at will would run the fund. No rule required disclosure of recipients or amounts.
The consequences arrived quickly. A federal judge, Kathleen Williams, later found there was no valid settlement of record at all. The legal basis for the entire arrangement did not exist in the form DOJ claimed. Treasury's general counsel, Brian Morrissey, resigned. Career IRS lawyers objected internally, noting the case's statute of limitations had already expired. None of this reversed the immunity order or unwound the fund. The structure took hold before anyone with authority to stop it could act. This is the shape the week kept returning to: harm formalized quickly, resistance arriving after the fact.
What followed was almost as revealing as the fund itself. Individuals convicted in the January 6 assault on the Capitol began applying for payouts, with requests ranging from two million to four hundred million dollars. Vice President Vance would not rule out compensating people convicted of assaulting police officers. Two of those officers, Daniel Hodges and Harry Dunn, sued to block the fund from reaching their attackers. Ninety-three House Democrats filed a brief warning of corruption without parallel in American history. The image was stark: a fund built from public money, meant to compensate victims of government overreach, now positioned to reward the people who stormed the Capitol.
The backlash inside the president's own party was, by the standards of this term, extraordinary. Roughly two dozen Republican senators confronted Blanche directly in a closed meeting. They called the fund morally wrong. They warned it would cost seats. Mitch McConnell, Thom Tillis, and others spoke against it publicly. Representative Brian Fitzpatrick, joined by Democrat Tom Suozzi, drafted legislation to kill the fund and signaled a discharge petition to force a floor vote over leadership's objection. House Speaker Mike Johnson reportedly declined to attend an Oval Office meeting the president had called to address the controversy. None of this stopped the fund. But it marked the first sustained, public, cross-partisan revolt of the term, and it mattered.
The president's use of executive influence to punish independent judgment inside his own party continued in parallel. Senator Bill Cassidy, who had voted to convict Trump at his second impeachment trial, was defeated in Louisiana after direct presidential intervention. Representative Thomas Massie, who had pushed for release of Epstein-related files and opposed increased military spending, lost his Kentucky primary after a record-spending campaign personally directed by Trump. Lauren Boebert was threatened with withdrawal of endorsement. Senator Thom Tillis was told publicly not to run again after criticizing administration policy. In Texas, the president backed Ken Paxton over incumbent Senator John Cornyn, citing loyalty rather than any policy disagreement. Each of these was a separate contest. Together they described a single method: reward loyalty, punish independent judgment, and let the primary process do the work discipline once required argument to achieve.
Congress's handling of the ongoing military action against Iran showed a similar asymmetry between chambers. The Senate advanced a bipartisan war-powers resolution, with four Republicans joining Democrats to reassert constitutional authority over military engagement. The House did not follow suit. Republican leadership canceled a scheduled vote on its own war-powers resolution after concluding it lacked the votes to defeat it, meaning the measure would likely have passed. Rather than allow a vote it might lose, leadership avoided the vote altogether. The president, meanwhile, dismissed the war's mounting economic toll, estimated at $43.6 billion in added household fuel costs, as "peanuts." His own Agriculture Secretary had already acknowledged rising fertilizer costs tied to the conflict, and Republican Budget Chair Jason Smith conceded inflation had climbed since Trump took office.
Voting rights sustained a distinct but related set of blows. The Supreme Court vacated and remanded several Section 2 cases, including challenges from the NAACP and the Turtle Mountain Band, under a new precedent that narrows private enforcement of the Voting Rights Act. Justice Jackson dissented. Within days, South Carolina Republicans moved to dismantle Representative James Clyburn's district, the state's only majority-Black congressional seat, citing the Court's new framework as cover. Grassroots mobilization followed swiftly. Thousands gathered in Selma and Montgomery, joined by eighteen members of Congress, to protest the rollback. Federal courts in Maine and Wisconsin also rejected DOJ demands for sensitive voter data, part of a pattern of rejection now spanning at least eight states. The doctrine moved one way. The resistance moved the other. Both were real.
A smaller but sharply pointed pair of events touched the legitimacy of elections more directly. Colorado Governor Jared Polis commuted the sentence of Tina Peters, a former county clerk convicted of tampering with voting machines, after repeated requests from Trump himself. Colorado's top election official condemned the move as a dangerous precedent. Almost simultaneously, Blanche told Fox News the Department of Justice had "a ton of evidence" the 2020 election was rigged and was actively investigating further, a claim with no standing in any court that has reviewed the 2020 results. Taken together, these were small gestures with a large implication. A legal system willing to reward those who broke faith with election integrity, while formally reopening a settled question, hollows out the very idea of a trustworthy vote.
Along the southern border, enforcement continued to produce documented death and neglect. Six migrants, including a fourteen-year-old, died of hyperthermia after being transported in a sealed railcar. Border Patrol agents were reported to have vandalized water stations left in the desert, under a long-standing "prevention through deterrence" strategy that knowingly channels migrants into lethal terrain. ICE simultaneously eliminated degree and age requirements for new hires while offering fifty-thousand-dollar signing bonuses, loosening the vetting standards for an agency already under scrutiny. California's Department of Justice released findings of systemic abuse across seven detention facilities: deaths, denied medical care, spoiled food, contaminated water. Detainees at one New Jersey facility began a hunger strike over these same conditions. None of this was new in kind. Its accumulation this week was its own form of evidence.
Against this record, courts provided one of the week's clearer counterweights. A judge dismissed the criminal case against Kilmar Ábrego García, finding the Justice Department had engaged in vindictive prosecution against a man who had successfully challenged his own unlawful deportation. Another judge, April Perry, found serious prosecutorial misconduct in the grand jury proceedings against Chicago protesters and signaled possible sanctions against the U.S. Attorney's Office. A third judge barred ICE arrests at three Manhattan immigration courthouses except in exceptional circumstances, preserving access to due process for people appearing in court. These were not minor rulings. They showed that judicial resistance to executive overreach remained functional in specific, documented instances, even as the Supreme Court moved the other way on voting rights.
The week's glorification projects proceeded largely without interruption. Trump announced a National Garden of American Heroes, drawing comparison to his own twenty-two-foot gold statue. His new organization, Freedom 250, bypassed the bipartisan congressional commission established for the nation's 250th anniversary and began soliciting corporate donations in exchange for access. An eight-hour taxpayer-funded evangelical worship event on the National Mall promoted an explicitly Christian-nation narrative, featuring the president, the vice president, and cabinet officials. The Commission of Fine Arts, composed entirely of presidential appointees, approved a 250-foot triumphal arch near Arlington National Cemetery over more than six hundred letters of public opposition. None of these required new legal authority. They required only appointees willing to point existing authority in a new direction.
One clash over funding showed the limits of even this. The Senate parliamentarian, a nonpartisan officer, ruled that a billion dollars in ballroom security funding could not ride through the reconciliation process, which requires only a simple majority, and would instead need sixty votes. Trump responded by demanding the Senate replace her with a loyalist and eliminate the filibuster altogether. The parliamentarian's ruling held. The demand did not succeed. But the sequence, an adverse ruling followed by a call to remove the referee, extended a pattern visible elsewhere in the week: pressure aimed not at the substance of a decision, but at the independence of the person who made it.
A final cluster tied money more directly to policy. Trump promoted an unregulated online casino on his social media platform shortly after its co-owner donated one million dollars to his super PAC. Reynolds American gave five million dollars to the same PAC roughly a week before a favorable federal vape policy emerged. Vance, asked why the president would be exempt from a proposed congressional ban on stock trading, offered sarcasm rather than an answer. None of these events, individually, proved intent. Together, they described a governing style in which donation and outcome moved close enough in time that the line between the two grew hard to find.
Two matters remain open by the record itself. Representative Fitzpatrick's discharge petition to eliminate the settlement fund has not yet come to a vote. The Senate's war-powers resolution on Iran, having passed with bipartisan support, awaits House action that leadership has so far declined to schedule.
The week did not move the clock, but it tested the instruments that might have. A fund without precedent in scale or design was created, exploited, and defended by allies who stood to benefit from it, all inside a single week. At the same time, judges dismissed vindictive prosecutions, a Treasury official resigned in protest, and members of the president's own party confronted his appointees in a closed room and called the arrangement morally wrong. Neither force cancelled the other. Both left a record. What Week 70 shows is not a system that has stopped resisting, nor one that has stopped consolidating. It shows both processes running at once, each real, each documented, and neither yet resolved.
