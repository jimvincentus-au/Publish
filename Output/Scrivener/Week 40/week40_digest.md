This Week in Democracy: Millions March, a Wing Comes Down, and the President Sends Himself a Bill
Record protests met a White House that mocked them, demolished part of itself, and tried to charge the Justice Department $230 million for investigating its own occupant
```markdown
This was a week that opened with the largest single day of protest in years and closed with the president announcing, without apparent hesitation, that he intended to kill suspected drug traffickers on his own authority. In between, a wing of the White House came down without permits, a sitting president asked his own Justice Department to pay him $230 million, and immigration enforcement produced both a death and a shooting. Courts intervened repeatedly. They did not always prevail.
**The Weekend**
The week began on Saturday, October 18, when organizers of the "No Kings" demonstrations reported nearly 7 million participants at more than 2,700 events across all fifty states and overseas. The rallies, concentrated in cities like Washington, Chicago, Boston, and Los Angeles but reaching well beyond them into Republican-leaning areas, were reported as largely peaceful. By scale alone, it stood as one of the largest single exercises of assembly rights in decades, and its geographic breadth suggested the concerns driving it were not confined to traditionally liberal areas.
The White House's response came quickly and took an unusual form. Rather than acknowledge the demonstrations through ordinary political channels, the president's and the White House's official accounts posted AI-generated videos depicting Trump as a crowned monarch dumping waste on protesters and cities, along with images showing Democratic leaders bowing to him or rendered in racially stereotyped garb. House Speaker Mike Johnson had, before the marches even took place, labeled them a "Hate America rally" and predicted they would expose "antifa types" and "Marxists." When Monday came, Johnson and other House Republicans held a press conference reiterating that framing, describing the demonstrations as a celebration of Republican deaths and a promotion of extremism. The sequence—preemptive delegitimization followed by mockery from official government accounts—meant that one of the largest peaceful protests in modern American history was met by its own government with contempt rather than engagement.
The weekend carried other threads as well. Trump commuted the seven-year fraud and identity-theft sentence of former Representative George Santos after less than three months served, following lobbying from Republican allies—a clemency decision distinct in kind from what would follow later in the week. Separately, Trump told Fox News he could invoke the Insurrection Act, calling it "unquestioned power," even as his administration sought Supreme Court permission to deploy the National Guard to Illinois after lower courts had already blocked the move. And in New York, Christopher Moynihan, a January 6 rioter who had received a pardon, was arrested for threatening to kill House Minority Leader Hakeem Jeffries ahead of a planned public appearance—a reminder that blanket clemency for participants in the Capitol riot carried consequences beyond the original cases.
**Monday**
By Monday, the week's defining institutional story began to take shape at the White House itself. Demolition crews started tearing down the East Wing to make way for a ballroom then priced at $250 to $300 million, without submitting plans to the National Capital Planning Commission, the federal body with jurisdiction over changes to the building. The commission's chair, a Trump appointee, argued the demolition fell outside the commission's authority. That same day, House Speaker Johnson kept the chamber out of session, preventing the swearing-in of Representative-elect Adelita Grijalva of Arizona weeks after her election—a delay Democrats said was intended to block a discharge petition related to files concerning Jeffrey Epstein.
Elsewhere in the federal government, officials from the FCC, FBI, IRS, and other agencies were organized into a task force aimed at removing career civil servants characterized internally as part of the "Deep State," and the National Nuclear Security Administration furloughed roughly 80 percent of its staff as the government shutdown surpassed the 2013 record for length, retaining only 375 of 1,775 employees for functions related to the nuclear stockpile. In a smaller but telling episode, leaked group chats from state Young Republicans chapters revealed racial slurs, jokes about gas chambers, and praise of slavery, prompting the shutdown of chapters in Kansas and New York and a state senator's resignation in Vermont.
On the legal front, Judge Karin Immergut in Oregon twice blocked deployment of National Guard troops to Portland, finding the administration's claims about conditions in the city "untethered to the facts," only for a Ninth Circuit panel to vote 2-1 to lift that block over a sharp dissent—the first turn in what would become a week-long legal seesaw.
**Tuesday**
Tuesday deepened several of Monday's threads at once. The ballroom's price rose again, to roughly $300 million, with the White House confirmed to be soliciting funds from corporate donors including Amazon, Google, Meta, and Palantir even as demolition continued and restrictions on employee photography of the site drew additional scrutiny. Arizona's attorney general filed suit against Speaker Johnson, arguing that his refusal to seat Grijalva denied 800,000 constituents their representation and appeared designed to obstruct the Epstein-related discharge petition.
On the litigation over troop deployments, Judge April Perry in Chicago blocked the National Guard from being sent there, calling the Department of Homeland Security's justification "simply unreliable," while a Ninth Circuit judge separately requested that the full court reconsider the Portland ruling—meaning the deployment question was now moving in two directions in two circuits at once. In Boston, Judge William Young issued a 161-page ruling finding that the administration's deportation of pro-Palestinian activists relied on an "unconstitutionally broad" definition of antisemitism and violated due process, a significant check on using immigration authority to punish protected speech.
The day's most striking single item concerned money rather than protest or deployment: Trump pressed his own Justice Department appointees to approve a $230 million settlement compensating him personally for the Russia investigation and the Mar-a-Lago search—an arrangement in which he would, in effect, help determine his own payout. Also on Tuesday, ICE conducted an unannounced raid in Manhattan's Chinatown without notifying city officials, drawing condemnation from New York's mayor, governor, and senators, and Paul Ingrassia's nomination to lead the federal whistleblower protection office collapsed after Senate Republicans said he lacked the votes, following revelations of texts in which he described having "a Nazi streak." Separately, Senator Jeff Merkley held the Senate floor overnight in a formal protest against what he described as threats to democracy.
**Wednesday**
Wednesday brought an escalation of the Chinatown enforcement action. More than fifty armed and masked ICE agents, backed by an armored vehicle, carried out a second and larger raid, detaining vendors and protesters; four U.S. citizens were held for nearly twenty-four hours before release without any charges filed. The same day, after mainstream outlets including the Washington Post, the New York Times, and Fox News refused new Pentagon rules requiring pre-approval of published material, the department installed a replacement press corps of roughly sixty outlets, most fringe or state-aligned, displacing the traditional Pentagon press corps entirely.
Financial and economic tensions surfaced as well: the Treasury moved toward a $40 billion currency-support package for Argentina, drawing criticism from Republican Senator Rand Paul and Representative Marjorie Taylor Greene as inconsistent with "America First," while American ranchers objected to a related proposal to import Argentine beef amid rising farm distress. A Medicare-dependent medical supply company was reported to have secretly donated $2.5 million to the White House ballroom project after benefiting from a delayed federal reimbursement rule, with its chief executive later attending an exclusive Trump donor dinner. Smaller items rounded out the day: a web of multibillion-dollar cross-investments among Nvidia, OpenAI, AMD, and Oracle raised concerns about revenue inflation in the AI sector, and the Department of Homeland Security was reported to have purchased two Gulfstream jets for $172 million, more than triple its original budget, even as the shutdown threatened food assistance for millions.
**Thursday**
Thursday's central story was a second clemency decision, distinct from the Santos commutation the weekend before. Trump pardoned Changpeng Zhao, the Binance founder convicted of anti-money-laundering violations tied to terrorist financing, following a $2 billion transaction between Binance and the Trump family's World Liberty Financial stablecoin venture and lobbying by a Trump associate. The pardon connected directly to the family's financial interests rather than to political loyalty, marking a different category of concern than the earlier clemency for Santos.
The day also brought a check on executive authority: a 9-0 appellate panel, led by a Trump appointee, rejected a request from the Department of Veterans Affairs to pause nearly all pending veterans' disability cases during the shutdown, calling it an "extreme request" given the years-long waits many veterans had already endured. State governments, meanwhile, moved to build their own oversight structures as federal watchdog mechanisms weakened: Illinois established an Accountability Commission to document federal agent misconduct after seventeen inspectors general had been fired, New York launched a public portal for reporting abuses tied to ICE raids, and the Illinois secretary of state opened a hotline for reports of agents swapping license plates.
Separately, watchdog groups warned that a Trump-appointed prosecutor pursuing charges against New York Attorney General Letitia James had used self-deleting Signal messages with a journalist, raising federal records-retention concerns, and the administration was reported to be pressuring the European Union to weaken corporate sustainability rules in order to benefit American LNG exports, even as it approved a new Louisiana export terminal projected to generate roughly 190 million tons of annual emissions.
**Friday**
The week closed on Friday with several threads converging. Asked why he had not sought congressional authorization for lethal strikes on suspected drug traffickers, Trump said plainly that such people would be killed rather than formally declared war upon—a statement bypassing the constitutional framework for authorizing the use of military force. The $230 million DOJ demand, first reported on Tuesday, drew formal congressional condemnation as Representatives Jamie Raskin and Robert Garcia accused Trump of orchestrating a scheme to direct Treasury funds to himself through a confidential process that could delay public disclosure for months.
Immigration enforcement produced its most severe consequences of the week: a Honduran national died in Norfolk, Virginia, after being struck by a vehicle while fleeing an ICE operation, the third such death of the term, and a gunman opened fire at an ICE facility in Dallas, killing two shackled detainees. Separately, ICE was reported to have rushed more than two hundred recruits with disqualifying criminal records or failed exams into service, after shortening training from thirteen weeks to six in order to fill ten thousand new deportation officer positions.
Redistricting fights intensified as well, with North Carolina's legislature passing a new congressional map shifting the state's delegation toward an 11-3 Republican advantage by targeting a Black incumbent's district, prompting a lawsuit from a prominent voting-rights law firm and a special session convened by Virginia Democrats to redraw their own map in response. Trump abruptly announced that trade talks with Canada were "hereby terminated" after taking offense at a Canadian advertisement quoting Ronald Reagan's criticism of tariffs. And the American Farm Bureau warned the White House that farm bankruptcies had risen 56 percent over the past year, with more than half of U.S. farms losing money, even as the administration pursued Argentine beef imports and new tariffs on goods from Southeast Asia.
Taken as a whole, the week showed a government pulling in two directions at once. Millions of Americans exercised their right to assemble and were met by their own government with ridicule rather than acknowledgment. A historic federal building was altered without the review required by law, funded in part by companies with direct business before the administration. A president sought compensation from the agency he oversees for investigating him, and pardoned a financier tied to his family's private business. Immigration enforcement expanded in scale and consequence, producing a civilian death, a mass shooting at a detention facility, and the detention of American citizens without charge.
Yet the week also recorded functioning resistance within existing institutions: judges in Oregon, Illinois, and Massachusetts found executive justifications unsupported by fact; a unanimous appellate panel protected veterans awaiting benefits; a nominee with documented extremist views withdrew after Senate Republicans withheld support; and states began constructing their own mechanisms to document conduct that federal oversight bodies, weakened by dismissals, were no longer positioned to review. Whether those checks would hold against the pace of executive action remained, at week's end, an open question rather than a settled one.
```
