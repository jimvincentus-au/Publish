Week 59: A Standing Clock Amid Widening War Powers
An unauthorized war expanded and coercive enforcement deepened at home, yet the measured week only confirmed risks already scored, not new ones.
The clock did not move this week. It sat at 7:43 p.m., unchanged from the week before. That stillness is not peace. It is the sound of pressure held in place: a war abroad and a coercive apparatus at home, both moving on their own momentum, neither fast enough to register nor slow enough to earn relief.
The Democracy Clock closed last week at 7:43 p.m. It closes this week at the same hour, a net movement of zero minutes. The stillness deserves scrutiny, not comfort. Nearly every trait in play this week moved the same way, toward greater risk. War powers weakened. Law bent further to serve the state. Watchdogs were stonewalled. Dissent narrowed. None of this broke new ground; these were continuations of currents already scored into the record, running at a pace the Clock had already priced in. A week can hold real damage and still leave the hour hand steady, if the damage confirms a known direction rather than opening a new one.
The week's defining fact was war. Trump ordered and defended major combat operations against Iran without congressional authorization, treating the decision to use force as his alone, not a power shared with Congress. Lawmakers did not stay silent. They introduced war-powers resolutions, held hearings, and forced two floor votes, one in the Senate, one in the House, meant to require approval before further hostilities continued. Both failed. These were plain votes, not close calls hedged by procedure; majorities simply declined to reclaim authority the Constitution assigns them. Pentagon briefers had told congressional staff that Iran showed no clear intent to strike first unless provoked. That detail sat uneasily beside the public case for war. It did not stop the operation. It only confirmed how far the war's rationale had drifted from evidence toward assertion.
The costs did not stay contained to foreign policy. Middle Eastern air travel and regional commerce absorbed real disruption. Shipping interference in the Strait of Hormuz pushed energy prices upward, a consequence Senator Marco Rubio all but confirmed the administration had foreseen. Defense officials told lawmakers an emergency supplemental request tied to the operation could reach fifty billion dollars, a figure that will need its own vote, its own reckoning. Members of Congress separately faulted the administration for poor evacuation planning for citizens abroad. None of this reversed the war. It only catalogued what unilateral force costs when no one outside the executive branch agrees to pay for it.
A second current ran through election administration. The House passed the SAVE America Act, imposing proof-of-citizenship and name-match rules alongside criminal penalties for election workers who fail to enforce them. DHS separately told agents to identify naturalized citizens who may have registered or voted before completing citizenship, treating ordinary participation in democracy as a possible offense to investigate after the fact. The FBI searched Fulton County's election office and seized 2020 ballots, reviving claims already tested and rejected in court, this time with agents and a warrant instead of a lawsuit. In Texas, the state supreme court halted extended voting hours in two counties and ordered late ballots set aside, even as a federal judge separately extended hours elsewhere because of election-day confusion. North Carolina proposed new rules that could narrow voter eligibility. Any single item here might pass as routine administration. Together, they describe one project: using the language of integrity to raise the cost and legal risk of voting, registering, and running elections, especially in places the administration distrusts.
Homeland Security supplied the week's most visible personnel drama, and its least reassuring resolution. Kristi Noem faced a bruising Senate Judiciary hearing. Senators from both parties criticized her management, her department's spending, and her truthfulness under oath. The department's own inspector general told Congress that DHS had systematically obstructed access to records, including in a matter with national-security implications. That is not foot-dragging; it is refusal. By week's end, Trump fired Noem and named Senator Markwayne Mullin as her replacement. The choice looked less like correction than continuity, a loyalist chosen for alignment, not for distance from the controversies that forced Noem out. Senator Richard Blumenthal opened a perjury inquiry into her testimony before she had even left office. What emerges is not a department correcting itself under pressure but one cycling leadership while its underlying practices stay the same.
Those practices did not pause during the leadership change. Roughly four hundred federal agents remained deployed in Minnesota, some reportedly using disguises during operations, a tactic that deepens fear in affected communities while shielding individual officers from accountability. Trump announced plans to cut federal payments to sanctuary jurisdictions, turning appropriations into leverage against local immigration policy. A Bronx high school student, detained by ICE, remained in custody after a hearing, his education and family life suspended by an enforcement apparatus indifferent to the harm it causes. In Texas, a detainee named Emmanuel Damas died after what advocates called a failure to provide timely medical care, one more name added to a pattern of neglect inside detention facilities. The administration did move to close the Camp East Montana facility following deaths and abuse allegations, a rare admission that conditions there had become untenable. But closing one site while expanding enforcement elsewhere is not correction; it is redistribution.
Inside the Justice Department, the week showed a clearer turn toward serving the president personally rather than the public impartially. Attorney General Pam Bondi directed DOJ employees to zealously advance and defend Trump's interests, language that erases the line between prosecutorial duty and political loyalty. The department proposed a rule requiring state bars to wait for DOJ review before pursuing ethics complaints against its own lawyers, inserting a buffer between federal attorneys and the outside bodies meant to discipline them. DOJ also reversed course to continue defending the administration's earlier campaign against law firms it viewed as adversarial, a shift that looked like strategy rather than settled legal judgment. Not every thread moved the same way. The department quietly dropped an investigation into Joe Biden's use of an autopen after finding no credible case, a retreat that shows some limit still held on how far politically driven inquiry would go. That single restraint does not offset the larger pattern, but it marks where the floor still held.
Congress and watchdogs did not stand entirely still. The House Oversight Committee subpoenaed Bondi over her handling of Epstein-related files, and Bill Clinton testified before the same committee as part of that inquiry, proof that oversight tools could still be pulled even against politically inconvenient targets. A district court blocked DHS's seven-day notice rule for congressional visits to ICE facilities, preserving lawmakers' ability to inspect detention conditions directly. Courts also authorized expanded inspection steps in a separate case over ICE detention conditions in New York. None of this amounted to a sweeping victory. Each step was narrow, procedural, and contested. But the machinery for resistance had not gone quiet; it was straining to keep pace with the volume of executive action arrayed against it.
The judiciary carried much of that resistance. A federal judge in Oregon issued a preliminary injunction restricting ICE conduct during protests. Courts kept enforcing earlier Supreme Court rulings against Trump's tariffs, forcing Customs and Border Protection to set up a refund system, even as the administration turned around and imposed a new fifteen percent global tariff days later. That move tested how quickly executive trade power could be reasserted after judicial limits. A coalition of two dozen state attorneys general sued over the new tariffs almost immediately. Judge Cobb blocked DHS's congressional-notice rule. Judge Shah issued a second injunction limiting DHS grant conditions used as leverage over local governments. Each ruling mattered on its own terms. None of them, alone or together, stopped the broader current. They set boundaries the administration would test again the following week.
Florida supplied the week's clearest state-level expansion of coercive governance. Lawmakers advanced bills allowing officials to label advocacy groups terrorist or foreign-influence actors. They created a new state counterintelligence unit aimed at domestic threats. Education legislation moved that would cut funding to campuses where students engage in DEI advocacy or political activism. A separate bill would restrict protests near religious sites. Together, these measures are not isolated culture-war skirmishes. They describe a governing model that treats dissent, organizing, and campus activism as security problems, one built at the state level but matched to the federal mood.
Executive power reached into private industry as well. Trump ordered federal agencies to stop using Anthropic's technology and cut related contracts after a dispute over AI safety guardrails. The Pentagon then formally labeled the firm a supply-chain risk, a national-security designation that looks, on the record, like retaliation for a policy disagreement rather than a genuine security finding. Separately, the Department of Labor used political alignment as a screen for legal internships, tying access to public employment to ideological conformity rather than merit. Neither move required new legislation. Both relied on existing executive discretion, pushed harder than before, to reward loyalty and punish resistance across public and private lines alike.
Smaller currents added their own weight. The Department of Education hung a large banner honoring Charlie Kirk outside its Washington building, a use of federal space for partisan symbolism that fits a broader pattern: memory curated by those in power. Florida lawmakers advanced a bill to rename a road in Kirk's honor, extending that symbolism into state law. Kristi Noem refused to retract calling dead protesters "domestic terrorists." Republicans separately pushed the Justice Department to charge January 6th witness Cassidy Hutchinson. Together, these moves show dissenters and unfavorable witnesses still being recast as criminal or subversive, not simply disagreeable.
Economic data added its own note of strain. The country lost 92,000 jobs in February, with unemployment rising. The report offered no comfort to an administration already managing a war, a shutdown, and a cabinet shake-up. BlackRock capped withdrawals from a major private credit fund the same week, a small tremor in financial markets. Paired with the labor numbers, it suggested economic conditions were not obviously improving even as executive attention stayed fixed elsewhere.
None of this week's developments arrived as a single dramatic rupture. That is what makes the standing clock worth sitting with. A week can be heavy with consequence, war expanded, watchdogs stonewalled, elections narrowed, agencies politicized, and still not move the hour hand, because the direction was already known and the risk already scored. What changed this week was not the trajectory but its confirmation: an administration willing to govern by unilateral force abroad, coercive enforcement at home, and loyalty tests everywhere in between, tested against the capacity of Congress, courts, and watchdogs to hold the line. Courts held in places. Congress mostly did not. The tests continue, and so, largely undisturbed, does the war.
