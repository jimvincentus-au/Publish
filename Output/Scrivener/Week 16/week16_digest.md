This Week in Democracy: Media Silenced, a Mayor Arrested, and Due Process in Doubt
From Voice of America's shutdown to Newark's mayor in handcuffs, the week showed executive power testing its limits while courts, Congress, and the streets pushed back
```markdown
This was a week in which words about the law started turning into acts against it. By its end, a sitting mayor had been arrested by federal agents, a senior White House adviser had floated suspending one of the oldest protections in Anglo-American law, and courts across the country were straining to keep pace with an executive branch testing how far it could go. The week's throughline was simple to state and unsettling to watch: a president publicly wondered aloud whether due process applied to everyone, and by Thursday that wondering had produced an arrest.
**The Weekend**
The weekend set the tone. On Saturday, Judge Beryl Howell permanently struck down Trump's executive order targeting the law firm Perkins Coie, ruling that it violated the First, Fifth, and Sixth Amendments and calling it unprecedented retaliation against attorneys for the clients they had represented. Roughly 1,500 lawyers rallied outside a Manhattan courthouse the same weekend in support of the ruling — on its face, a clear win for judicial constraint on executive power.
But the weekend also brought a wave of personnel moves inside the security apparatus: the firing of NSA and Cyber Command chief Tim Haugh, plans to cut 1,200 CIA jobs, and the elimination of diversity programs across intelligence agencies, all consistent with a pattern of stocking sensitive institutions with loyalty rather than continuity. And in a *Meet the Press* interview, Trump said he wasn't sure that everyone in the United States was entitled to due process, framing the constitutional guarantee as an obstacle to the deportations he said he was elected to carry out. The administration also announced a June 14 military parade timed to the president's 79th birthday and the Army's 250th anniversary, at a cost of tens of millions of dollars, and Trump said he would not rule out using military force to gain control of Greenland.
**Monday**
By Monday, the Supreme Court had entered the picture in a way that cut against the weekend's judicial momentum. The Court granted a stay allowing Trump's ban on transgender military service to take effect while litigation continued, overriding lower court blocks and drawing dissent from three liberal justices. It was a reminder that the judiciary was not a uniform check — the same week that produced Perkins Coie also produced a green light for a contested policy to take hold before its legality was settled.
Monday brought lighter theater too: an order to reopen Alcatraz as a federal prison for "ruthless offenders," widely read as symbolic messaging rather than serious policy. More consequential were mounting reports of foreign business deals tied to the Trump family — a Trump-linked crypto firm's role in a $2 billion Abu Dhabi-backed Binance transaction, a new Trump hotel project in Dubai with Saudi-linked partners, and a $5.5 billion golf club deal with Qatar's government-owned Qatari Diar — each renewing questions about an ethics agreement meant to bar exactly this kind of foreign entanglement.
National security leadership shifted again as Mike Waltz was removed as national security adviser following the Signal chat controversy over Yemen war plans, with Secretary of State Marco Rubio installed as his acting replacement, doubling up critical roles under one trusted official. Civic dissent broadened as well: California State University students began a hunger strike over Gaza aid, Bernie Sanders launched his "Fighting Oligarchy" tour, Tesla protests continued abroad, and Bishop William Barber's Moral Mondays campaign targeted proposed cuts to Medicaid and SNAP alongside rising military spending. A federal court also ordered North Carolina to certify Democrat Allison Riggs as the winner of a contested state supreme court race, rejecting a Republican effort to discard more than 60,000 ballots after months of dispute.
**Tuesday**
Tuesday shifted focus to institutions being hollowed from within. The EPA eliminated its office of science and technology, cut $300 million from its budget, and forced 1,500 research staff to reapply for roughly 400 remaining positions — a restructuring critics said would strip the agency of independent scientific capacity just as it was needed most. Harvard, meanwhile, lost access to new federal research grants after rejecting a list of demands from an administration antisemitism task force, compounding an earlier freeze of $2.2 billion; the university responded with a lawsuit.
At the FCC, a "news distortion" investigation opened into CBS over a *60 Minutes* interview with Kamala Harris — echoing Trump's own lawsuit against the network — while Paramount's chair reportedly intervened in CBS coverage and softened diversity policies to help clear the way for merger approval. It was a clear instance of regulatory leverage appearing to bend editorial judgment at a major news organization. Campus protest also continued: more than two dozen demonstrators were arrested at the University of Washington after occupying an engineering building to demand the school cut ties with Boeing over military contracts with Israel.
**Wednesday**
Wednesday's defining story had been building for weeks. Voice of America remained off the air, with 1,300 employees still on leave months after an executive order dismantled its parent agency. The D.C. Circuit, sitting en banc, restored some district court protections for other USAGM grantees, including Radio Free Asia, but staffing relief for VOA itself stayed limited — a case study in how executive action can outlast even partial judicial intervention.
The same day, DHS Secretary Kristi Noem appeared before Congress and refused to answer whether the Constitution guarantees due process to everyone, echoing the president's earlier equivocation, while Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent dodged questions about who actually bears the cost of tariffs. Elsewhere, the legal system pushed back: a federal appeals court upheld an order requiring Tufts student Rümeysa Öztürk, detained for weeks after co-authoring an op-ed critical of Israel, to be transferred to Vermont for hearings on her detention's legality. And at a Senate Appropriations Committee hearing, Senator Susan Collins pressed the administration on funding cuts, grant cancellations, and layoffs in health agencies that appeared to conflict with enacted appropriations law.
**Thursday**
Thursday delivered the week's most visible confrontation. Newark Mayor Ras Baraka was arrested by federal agents outside Delaney Hall during a protest and oversight visit joined by members of Congress, who accused ICE of staging the encounter. He was released after several hours, but allies described the arrest as an act of intimidation against a sitting local official — a marked escalation from earlier threats made against Wisconsin's governor.
The same day brought a cluster of safety-oversight rollbacks: the administration halted an outside review of air traffic control practices after a deadly crash, kept a hiring freeze in place despite controller shortages, removed the NTSB's vice chair, began dismantling the Consumer Product Safety Commission, and had the FAA approve a fivefold increase in SpaceX launches over environmental objections.
Then came the sharpest rhetorical turn of the week: senior adviser Stephen Miller said the administration was considering suspending the writ of habeas corpus — a measure the Constitution reserves for invasion or insurrection — suggesting it would hinge on how courts ruled against the administration going forward. Against that backdrop, courts kept intervening on behalf of detainees: a judge ordered Öztürk's release from ICE custody citing First Amendment and due process violations, and Irish green card holder Cliona Ward was freed after 17 days in detention following a court's reversal of decades-old convictions used against her. Meanwhile, NYPD arrested about 75 pro-Palestinian protesters after they occupied Columbia University's main library, at the request of the university's own president. And in North Carolina, Republican candidate Jefferson Griffin conceded his state supreme court race to Allison Riggs, closing out a six-month legal battle in favor of the judicially certified result.
**Friday**
Friday closed the week with a scattering of smaller but telling moves. The American Bar Association filed suit over the termination of federal grants, part of an escalating conflict that has also touched the ABA's law school accreditation authority. A resurfaced letter from Pope Francis, rejecting Vice President Vance's theological defense of prioritizing national interests over migrants, drew renewed attention after Cardinal Robert Prevost publicly backed the Pope's position and former border czar Tom Homan told the pontiff to keep to church matters.
Trump signed a cluster of executive orders — establishing a veterans homelessness center, limiting criminal enforcement of regulatory offenses, and speeding up Federal Register publication — while the GSA rescinded workplace nondiscrimination guidance tied to gender identity, and the White House blocked a chief-of-staff pick favored by Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth. Finally, Judge Susan Illston granted a temporary restraining order pausing reductions in force tied to an executive order restructuring the federal workforce; the administration appealed immediately to the Ninth Circuit.
**Looking Back**
Taken as a whole, the week showed two institutions moving in opposite directions at once. Courts struck down retaliatory orders, restored some protections to detained students, forced the certification of a contested election, and briefly paused workforce cuts — the ordinary machinery of judicial review still functioning under pressure. But the same week saw the Supreme Court clear the way for a disputed military policy, a mayor detained by federal agents at a protest, a public broadcaster left dark months after being dismantled, and a senior official openly discussing the suspension of habeas corpus. Individually, each of these might be read as a policy dispute or a legal skirmish. Together, they described a government increasingly willing to test whether constitutional guarantees are conditions to be negotiated rather than limits to be obeyed — and a judiciary, a press corps, and a public still finding, unevenly, the means to answer that test.
```
