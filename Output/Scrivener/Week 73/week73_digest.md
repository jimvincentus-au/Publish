This Week in Democracy: Immigration Power Expands, Courts Push Back, and Patronage Questions Deepen
A new immigration funding law, fights over oversight and transparency, and a week of legal checks on executive claims and spending.
The week was shaped by a fast-moving expansion of immigration enforcement power and by the institutions that either enabled it or tried to slow it. In Washington, Congress moved a large enforcement package from approval to enactment in a matter of days. Outside Washington, detention protests, reports of worsening conditions, and new evidence about family separation showed what that agenda meant in practice. Running alongside the immigration story were repeated court interventions against executive overreach, fresh signs of politicization in the Justice Department and intelligence leadership, and a steady blurring of public office with spectacle, private gain, and message control.
Over the weekend, the administration and its allies were already testing the limits of executive power. At a court hearing over the White House ballroom project, Justice Department lawyers advanced a sweeping claim: once executive construction was completed, courts had no role in stopping it even if Congress had not approved it. At nearly the same time, watchdogs and plaintiffs sought to block a planned UFC event on White House grounds, arguing that federal property and public resources were being used for a private commercial show. Together, the two disputes raised a question that would recur throughout the week: how far could the presidency go in treating public space, public money, and public authority as instruments of personal will?
Immigration was the other defining weekend story, beginning with the treatment of people already in custody. An oversight visit to Delaney Hall drew attention to a hunger strike and deteriorating conditions inside the facility, while protesters outside were pepper-sprayed. New refugee admissions data pointed in a different but related direction: nearly all recent admissions were white South Africans, reinforcing concerns that refugee policy was being applied selectively by race and national origin. One development concerned confinement, the other admission, but both reflected the same power to decide who is protected, who is excluded, and on what terms.
That same weekend, Senate Republicans moved to lock in the administration’s enforcement agenda with money and fewer constraints. Democrats forced votes on amendments meant to limit executive spending discretion and block funding for Trump’s ballroom project, but Republicans rejected those checks and approved roughly $70 billion in additional funding for ICE and Customs and Border Protection. The vote came as oversight was weakening in other ways: Senator Andy Kim said he was denied access to detainees at Delaney Hall, and ICE dropped a policy requiring reports to Congress on detainee deaths after release. The significance was not only the size of the appropriation. Congress was expanding enforcement capacity while declining to add new guardrails, even as transparency around detention narrowed.
A whistleblower account added another layer to the immigration story. According to the report, officials had discussed falsely declaring millions of immigrants dead in government systems to pressure them into leaving the country, and thousands were in fact moved into a database that could cut off services. The allegation suggested an enforcement strategy extending beyond arrests, detention, and deportation into the administrative machinery of the state itself. Records systems that ordinarily determine eligibility, identity, and access could become tools of coercion if manipulated for political ends.
The weekend also brought early evidence that courts remained one of the few effective brakes on some administration initiatives. A federal judge temporarily blocked the proposed anti-weaponization fund for January 6 defendants, and Acting Attorney General Todd Blanche said the administration was not moving forward with it. In a separate case, a judge blocked the administration’s $100,000 H-1B visa fee as an unlawful tax imposed without Congress. Neither ruling settled the broader struggle over executive power, but both showed judges still willing to enforce basic limits when the administration tried to raise money or direct benefits outside ordinary legal channels.
By Monday, the pressure had widened from policy to procedure. Trump publicly urged Senate Republicans to fire the Senate parliamentarian after she ruled against using reconciliation for the SAVE America Act, a voting restrictions measure. The demand was notable not just for its substance but for what it implied: that internal legislative rules should give way when they obstructed a presidential priority. The same day, border czar Tom Homan threatened to increase ICE presence in New York City in retaliation for a new state law limiting local cooperation with federal immigration enforcement. The administration was pressing on two fronts at once, trying to bend congressional procedure while warning resistant states and cities that federal enforcement power could be intensified against them.
Monday also brought a harsher official tone on immigration. The White House launched a new website describing undocumented immigrants as “aliens” and promising their return to their “place of origin.” That messaging arrived alongside cuts to refugee cash assistance and new state-level barriers for non-citizens, including restrictive Ohio driver’s license rules. Language, benefits, and legal status were working together. The point was not only to enforce the law but to define who counted as part of the public and who was marked for removal or exclusion.
Elsewhere on Monday, two smaller but telling institutional stories developed. Investigations found that Polymarket and Kalshi had sponsored influencer posts spreading false claims about the Los Angeles mayoral election, with some posts removed or relabeled only after they had circulated widely. The ruling against the $100,000 H-1B fee also held through additional case updates, reinforcing the court’s view that the fee functioned as an unlawful tax. In a separate dispute over public institutions, the Kennedy Center complied with a court order by removing Trump’s name from its website. These were different kinds of cases, but together they showed how much of the week’s conflict turned on whether rules still constrained power, money, and official branding.
Tuesday was the week’s turning point in Congress. House Republicans narrowly passed the $70 billion immigration bill, sending major new money to ICE, CBP, and the Department of Homeland Security through 2029. What had begun as a Senate funding fight was now on its way to becoming durable law. On the same day, Trump kept pressing Republicans to use a broader reconciliation bill to carry the SAVE America Act, extending his effort to use budget procedure to advance voting restrictions under the banner of election integrity. The administration’s immigration agenda was being institutionalized even as it continued to search for procedural shortcuts on election rules.
Tuesday also brought a sharper politicization story inside core national institutions. Trump nominated former personal lawyer Todd Blanche to be attorney general and announced Bill Pulte, who lacked intelligence experience, as acting director of national intelligence. These were not ordinary personnel changes. The Justice Department and intelligence community depend heavily on public confidence that their leaders are chosen for legal judgment, independence, and expertise. Installing a former personal lawyer at Justice and a politically connected outsider atop intelligence oversight suggested a different standard: loyalty first, institutional credibility second.
Reporting that day deepened the sense that public power and private interest were moving too close together. Investigations described a foreign-linked investment of more than $100 million into a startup connected to Donald Trump Jr. after the investor’s family business received favorable treatment from the administration. Another report said the administration was considering using frozen Iranian assets in ways complicated by Jared Kushner’s financial ties in the Gulf. None of this proved a single unified scheme, but the pattern mattered. When family business opportunities and government decisions repeatedly appear in the same frame, the burden on public trust grows heavier.
The White House’s own communications added to that atmosphere. Its social media team posted a digitally altered image mocking House Minority Leader Hakeem Jeffries, using official channels for ridicule rather than public information. On its own, the post was a minor event. In the context of the week, it fit a broader pattern in which the authority of government communications was being used for provocation, branding, and partisan theater.
On Wednesday, the immigration package became law. Trump signed the Secure America Act at the White House, completing a rapid progression from Senate approval to House passage to presidential signature. The speed mattered because it showed how quickly a contested enforcement agenda could move from rhetoric and appropriations into the structure of government itself. The law cemented a large expansion of immigration enforcement capacity, and it did so in a week already marked by weaker oversight, retaliatory threats against sanctuary jurisdictions, and evidence of mounting harm inside detention.
The White House UFC fight, which had begun as a legal challenge over a planned event, also matured into a broader test of public-private boundaries. A watchdog lawsuit argued that the proposed event on the South Lawn violated multiple federal laws and amounted to a private commercial spectacle dressed up as a patriotic celebration. By midweek, the issue was no longer just whether one event would happen. It had become a dispute over whether federal property and taxpayer resources could be repurposed for presidential spectacle and allied profit with little regard for ordinary legal limits.
Wednesday’s litigation docket showed how many constituencies were now turning to courts at once. Cases were filed or advanced over an ICE holding facility in Gilroy, Ryan White grant conditions affecting transgender patients, anti-DEI federal contract terms, labor reporting rules, and access to records about the filming of immigration enforcement. The details varied, but the common thread was institutional stress. When immigration advocates, civil-rights groups, labor interests, and transparency plaintiffs all seek judicial relief in the same week, it suggests a government acting quickly on many fronts and a public increasingly reliant on judges to police the boundaries.
Another major Wednesday story concerned transparency and accountability in the Epstein matter. The Justice Department was reported to be out of compliance with the law requiring release of Epstein files, while a New York Times investigation described internal White House crisis management around the issue. Threats against survivors underscored the human cost of chaotic and partial disclosure. At the same time, conflict-of-interest reporting widened from investments to cryptocurrency, with reports that the Trump family had made at least $2.3 billion from crypto licensing during Trump’s second term. The week’s ethics questions were no longer confined to one investment or one policy decision; they were spreading across sectors and revenue streams.
Thursday produced one of the clearest examples of how personnel choices can affect lawmaking. The House failed to pass a short-term extension of Section 702 of FISA, with opposition tied in part to Trump’s move to place Bill Pulte atop intelligence oversight despite his lack of national security experience. By the end of the day, the administration had shifted toward nominating Jay Clayton instead, but the damage to the surveillance reauthorization effort was already done. The episode showed that trust in oversight is not abstract. Congress’s willingness to renew powerful surveillance authorities depends in part on confidence that the people supervising them are credible.
House Democrats also escalated their response to the Epstein records controversy, saying they would call JD Vance to testify about the administration’s handling of the files. At the same time, reporting showed Trump and allies pressing Congress to pass a symbolic resolution expunging his two impeachments from the historical record. One effort sought testimony and records; the other sought to revise public memory. Both were about control over accountability, but in opposite directions.
On the ground, Thursday made the human consequences of the week’s immigration decisions harder to ignore. Reports described high or severe ICE activity across a wide range of states, from Alabama and Arizona to New Jersey, New York, and Texas. Brad Lander was acquitted in a protest-related case tied to detention oversight, a reminder that immigration enforcement was also generating civic conflict, protest, and legal contest outside federal agencies themselves. JD Vance added another note of politicization when he threatened a Justice Department referral against Minnesota Governor Tim Walz and Attorney General Keith Ellison over alleged social-services fraud involving Somali residents. Critics described the move as a politically motivated use of federal investigative power against state-level opponents, and it fit the week’s broader pattern of using legal threat as a political instrument.
Friday closed with the strongest judicial pushback of the week. A federal judge extended and then strengthened the injunction against the proposed $1.8 billion anti-weaponization fund, ordering officials to halt any effort to create or operate it and to file sworn declarations under penalty of perjury. The court made clear that verbal assurances from the administration were not enough. That insistence on enforceable proof, rather than political statements, captured the judiciary’s role throughout the week: not ending every dispute, but demanding that executive claims be tested against law and evidence.
Friday also brought some of the starkest evidence of what the new immigration regime meant for families. Reporting found that ICE had detained at least 500 babies and toddlers during Trump’s second term and deported the parents of thousands of U.S.-born children. At Delaney Hall, women joined the ongoing hunger strike, extending a protest that had begun earlier in the week over detention conditions. The week’s immigration story had started with funding and procedure in Washington. It ended with detained children, separated families, and direct protest from people in custody.
Other Friday developments kept several of the week’s main arcs open. The administration suspended federal funding to the Los Angeles Homeless Services Authority, widening federal pressure on California through a move that disrupted a major local public-service system. Congress still had not resolved the FISA lapse after the House failure the day before, reflecting bipartisan unease over surveillance powers and the current oversight arrangement. And more reporting sharpened the conflict-of-interest picture: the administration eased sanctions on Indian energy companies in ways that benefited the Ambani family, whose investment had already been linked to a Trump Jr.-connected venture, while Democratic lawmakers sought a Government Accountability Office audit of Venezuelan oil proceeds.
By week’s end, the pattern was clear. Immigration enforcement had moved from a funding fight to a governing reality, with Congress supplying money, the White House supplying rhetoric and pressure, and communities absorbing the consequences. Courts remained a significant counterforce, especially where the administration tried to spend, tax, or reward allies without clear legal authority. At the same time, appointments, messaging, and financial reporting all pointed to a deeper problem: the weakening distinction between public institutions and personal loyalty, between state power and private benefit, and between accountability as a legal process and accountability as something to be managed, delayed, or rewritten.
