Shutdown, Surge, and Retreat
A week of federal killings, a shutdown fight over ICE, and mounting pressure on courts, the press, and the vote
```markdown
This week began with two funerals still fresh and ended with a president musing openly about federal control of elections. In between, a government briefly shut down over a single question — whether Immigration and Customs Enforcement would face new limits — and reopened without answering it. Courts intervened repeatedly, sometimes decisively, sometimes not at all. Journalists were arrested for doing their jobs. And beneath all of it, the ordinary machinery of federal rulemaking kept turning, mostly unnoticed, as it always does.
The weekend opened in the shadow of Minneapolis, where federal agents had shot and killed Alex Pretti and Renee Good, two American citizens whose deaths became the week's organizing wound. Tens of thousands took part in a coordinated day of protest across the country — strikes, walkouts, a demonstration staged inside a Minneapolis Target store. Chicago's mayor ordered his police department to investigate allegations of ICE misconduct. The administration, which had initially described Pretti as a "domestic terrorist," was forced to retreat from that framing after video evidence contradicted it — a rare, involuntary concession from a government that had moved quickly to control the narrative and found the narrative would not hold.
The response to the protests became its own story. Federal agents arrested independent journalist Don Lemon and reporter Georgia Fort during a demonstration at a church, charging Lemon under the FACE Act despite earlier judicial rejections of similar charges against other reporters. Elsewhere, agents detained teenage siblings and bystanders outside a clinic. These were not isolated incidents so much as a pattern: enforcement extending its reach to observers, not only to those it was ostensibly sent to police.
Minnesota asked a federal court to halt the underlying operation — Operation Metro Surge, the 3,000-agent deployment that had produced the two deaths — arguing it violated the Tenth Amendment. The judge declined, finding the state had not met its burden, and the surge continued. In a separate case that same weekend, a different judge ordered the release of five-year-old Liam Conejo Ramos and his father from a Texas detention facility, criticizing the government's treatment of the boy and raising the possibility he had been used as bait during his arrest. The contrast was stark: one court unwilling to disturb a deadly enforcement operation at a state's request, another intervening directly on behalf of a single child. Also that weekend, the Justice Department released roughly three million pages of Epstein-related files, missing a congressional deadline and exposing victims' identities through inadequate redactions — the first sign of what would become a recurring failure rather than a one-time embarrassment. And word emerged that the Trump family had sold a 49 percent stake in a cryptocurrency venture to a buyer backed by an Abu Dhabi royal for $500 million, a deal that closed shortly before the United States agreed to sell the UAE advanced AI chips.
By Monday, courts continued pushing back on immigration policy. A coalition sued the State Department over its suspension of immigrant visa approvals for 75 countries, and a federal judge stayed Homeland Security Secretary Kristi Noem's termination of Haiti's Temporary Protected Status, finding the government had ignored both facts and law. Yet enforcement's human costs kept surfacing: Maher Tarabishi, a caregiver for his son who had died of Pompe disease, was detained despite his supervised release status and denied the chance to attend the boy's funeral. Under mounting pressure, Noem announced that federal agents in Minneapolis would begin wearing body cameras immediately, with a national rollout promised — a partial concession that aligned, for once, Republican and Democratic demands. On a podcast appearance, Trump raised the idea of nationalizing elections in at least 15 places, repeating unsubstantiated claims about noncitizens voting — remarks that would prove to be the opening note of a theme sustained through the rest of the week. Smaller stories moved alongside these: North Carolina's legislature failed to pass a budget while separately proposing to zero out its corporate tax rate, and Bill and Hillary Clinton agreed to testify before the House Oversight Committee after facing a contempt threat over Epstein-related subpoenas.
Tuesday brought resolution, of a sort, to the shutdown. Trump signed a $1.2 trillion appropriations bill, passed by the House 217-214, funding the Department of Homeland Security only through February 13 while other agencies received funding through the fiscal year. The measure ended the shutdown without resolving what had caused it — Democratic demands for ICE reform were deferred rather than answered, guaranteeing another confrontation within two weeks. The same day, the administration escalated its pressure on Harvard University, demanding $1 billion in damages over alleged civil rights violations after earlier dropping a smaller settlement figure, and threatening research funding along with changes to the university's diversity programs. Courts again intervened on immigration: a federal judge formally blocked the termination of TPS for roughly 353,000 Haitians, and Trump demoted the head of Border Patrol amid criticism of enforcement tactics. Meanwhile the Justice Department sued 24 states and Washington, D.C. for refusing to hand over voter rolls, in filings that reportedly contained basic errors, while Trump repeated his call to nationalize elections at a bill-signing ceremony. A separate lawsuit challenged the administration's Gold Card visa program, alleging it let wealthy applicants effectively purchase priority immigration status.
Wednesday's headline development was less what happened than what didn't. Border czar Tom Homan announced that 700 federal agents would withdraw from Minnesota, but reporting indicated roughly 2,000 agents remained in place, and observers continued to document ICE activity in suburban areas — a gap between announcement and reality that would recur later in the week. Congress heard emotional testimony from relatives of Renee Good and Marimar Martinez, both shot by federal agents, though no Republican participation was noted. The Democratic National Committee announced an ambitious outreach effort aimed at more than a million infrequent voters ahead of the midterms. In Florida, a House committee advanced a bill requiring schools to use "Judea and Samaria" rather than "West Bank." The Washington Post announced it would cut roughly a third of its workforce, a decision attributed to owner Jeff Bezos amid broader financial and political pressure on news organizations. And Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent faced congressional questioning over possible market manipulation tied to Trump's social media activity, while also being found to have contradicted his own earlier sworn testimony about tariffs.
Thursday was, by any measure, the week's heaviest day. The Office of Personnel Management issued a rule making it easier to fire federal employees seen as obstructing presidential directives, while shifting whistleblower disclosure handling away from the independent Office of Special Counsel to individual agencies — a structural weakening of civil service protections built to insulate the government from partisan pressure. In Minneapolis, video showed ICE agents surrounding a vehicle with guns drawn, pursuing and detaining a woman who was recording them, and clashing with protesters in a public park using tear gas and stun devices — directly undercutting the administration's claims of de-escalation. A Guardian investigation revealed that ICE had used a private jet owned by a Trump associate to deport shackled Palestinians, including a man named Maher Awad, to a West Bank checkpoint across two flights. The election-nationalization theme sharpened further: Steve Bannon suggested deploying ICE to polling places to prevent alleged fraud, Republicans introduced a new SAVE Act requiring citizenship-proof voter ID, and Attorney General Bondi pressed Minnesota on voter rolls in the same communication addressing the CBP killing of Alex Pretti — collapsing immigration enforcement and election administration into a single line of pressure. Trump also posted a video depicting Barack and Michelle Obama as apes, drawing rare bipartisan condemnation; the White House blamed a staffer and offered no apology. Rounding out the day, the administration canceled roughly 560 arts grants worth $27 million, withheld Gateway tunnel infrastructure funding pending a renaming demand, and disclosed that Trump could be owed $10 billion in taxpayer money over a tax dispute — even as US employers reported more than 108,000 layoffs in January, the worst month since 2009, and the cryptocurrency market recorded losses exceeding $2 trillion since October.
Friday closed the week with a cluster of executive orders that consolidated trade, security, and border authority under direct presidential control: an "America First Arms Transfer Strategy," new tariffs on countries trading with Iran, the lifting of tariffs on India after it agreed to curb Russian oil purchases, and expanded data-sharing for border and visa screening. The Department of Homeland Security pressed California to honor detainers for 33,000 people with criminal convictions, a claim Governor Newsom disputed. At the National Prayer Breakfast, traditionally a bipartisan occasion, Trump again claimed the 2020 election had been rigged and questioned how people of faith could support Democrats — extending, in a nonpartisan setting, a week-long escalation in rhetoric about elections. Florida eliminated non-English driver's license exams, ending translation services that had been available in Spanish, Haitian Creole, and Portuguese. And in Louisiana, a legislative change eliminating filing deadlines for childhood sexual abuse lawsuits led the New Orleans archdiocese and its insurers to agree to pay $305 million to roughly 600 survivors of clergy abuse — a quieter reminder that legal reform can still expand accountability even amid a week largely defined by its erosion elsewhere.
Taken as a whole, the week showed a federal government reaching for enforcement, funding, and public messaging as instruments of pressure — against cities, against a university, against states resistant to sharing voter data, against federal workers themselves — while courts, Congress, and ordinary citizens pushed back with mixed results. Judges blocked a TPS termination and freed a detained child but declined to halt a deadly enforcement surge outright. A shutdown formed and dissolved around the same unresolved question of ICE accountability that will return within days. And rhetoric about nationalizing elections, floated first on a podcast, had by week's end reached a bipartisan prayer breakfast, unchallenged and unretracted. Institutions bent under the pressure; none of them broke, but few of the questions the week raised were actually settled.
```
