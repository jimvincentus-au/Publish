# This Week in Democracy: Minnesota Under Siege

*A federal surge, a killing, a child detained as bait, and a state that fought back*

```markdown
This Week in Democracy: Minnesota Under Siege

The seven days beginning January 17 turned Minnesota into the country's central stage for a confrontation between federal enforcement power and the people and institutions trying to restrain it. What began as a large-scale ICE deployment escalated, day by day, into a fatal shooting the Justice Department refused to investigate, children detained as tools of arrest, a warrantless-entry policy leaked from inside ICE, and finally a statewide economic blackout and clergy arrests at the airport. Around that spine, the administration pressed investigations against political critics, pardoned a crypto executive tied to its own family business, and briefly rattled financial markets with a foreign-policy threat it then abandoned. Courts intervened repeatedly, but not always successfully — a pattern that recurred throughout the week.

The weekend set the tone. Executive orders from the Trump administration stripped federal recognition and funding tied to LGBTQ+ protections and eliminated home-energy assistance for millions of low-income households — changes made unilaterally, without legislative input. At the same time, following the fatal shooting of Minneapolis resident Renee Good by an ICE agent, the Justice Department announced it was investigating Governor Tim Walz and Mayor Jacob Frey for allegedly obstructing federal immigration enforcement. The administration simultaneously deployed roughly 3,000 federal agents to the Twin Cities, the largest such operation to date. The juxtaposition — an investigation opened into local officials even as the shooting itself awaited resolution — established the contrast that would define the week. Elsewhere, the Supreme Court heard arguments in a closely watched transgender school-sports case, and a bipartisan group in Congress moved to check the administration's saber-rattling over Greenland, meeting with Danish officials to lower the temperature.

By Monday, the contrast sharpened into decision. Deputy Attorney General Todd Blanche announced that the Justice Department would not investigate Good's killing, a decision that drew immediate comparisons to the George Floyd case and prompted a wave of resignations among federal prosecutors uncomfortable with it. The same day, federal judge Katherine Menendez issued a preliminary injunction barring DHS agents from using pepper spray or arresting peaceful protesters in Minneapolis — a rare judicial check on the deployment's tactics. Separately, the Justice Department missed its statutory deadline to release files related to Jeffrey Epstein, leaving more than two million documents unreviewed despite a congressional mandate, and a detainee, Victor Manuel Diaz, was found dead at a Texas ICE facility in El Paso after the agency had stopped funding third-party medical care there — the second such death at that facility in two weeks.

Tuesday was, by any measure, the week's heaviest day. President Trump dismissed seventeen independent inspectors general and declared a national emergency at the southern border on the same afternoon, simultaneously stripping oversight from the executive branch and expanding its emergency powers. He signed additional executive orders eliminating federal DEI programs and mandating a two-sex policy in schools, alongside an order framed as ending government censorship that in practice threatened federal funding for universities and the standing of law firms representing his critics. In Minnesota, ICE agents detained five-year-old Liam Ramos in Columbia Heights, using him to determine whether anyone else was inside the home before arresting his father; both were sent to a detention facility in Texas despite an active asylum case and the absence of any deportation order. In a separate incident, ICE agents forced their way into the home of ChongLy Thao, a U.S. citizen, holding his family at gunpoint and detaining him in subfreezing weather before releasing him once agents realized he had no criminal record. A federal judge that same day ruled that DHS could require a week's advance notice before members of Congress could inspect detention facilities, narrowing a tool lawmakers had used just days earlier when Minnesota representatives were blocked from an inspection. Against this backdrop, the "Free America Walkout," organized nationally by the 50501 Movement, drew roughly 50,000 participants across more than 600 local events — a coordinated nonviolent response to the week's accumulating pressure.

Wednesday brought a reversal that showed how quickly legal protections could erode. The Eighth Circuit granted the Justice Department's request for an administrative stay, lifting Judge Menendez's injunction and allowing ICE agents to resume aggressive tactics, including pepper spray, against Minneapolis protesters — a protection granted on Monday and undone within forty-eight hours. The same day, a court filing revealed that a DOGE staffer had signed a secret data-sharing agreement giving a political advocacy group access to Social Security data to hunt for voter fraud, while another staffer had separately sought Florida's voter rolls for the same purpose. ICE, meanwhile, launched "Operation Catch of the Day" in Maine, a targeted crackdown on Somali immigrant communities that produced about 50 arrests and coincided with the administration ending Temporary Protected Status for Somalis. In Washington, the Supreme Court heard arguments testing whether Trump could remove Federal Reserve Governor Lisa Cook over unproven allegations, with justices voicing skepticism about the implications for central-bank independence. Separately, Lindsey Halligan, a Trump-appointed interim U.S. attorney with no prosecutorial experience whose appointment had exceeded legal limits, left her post after a judge ordered her to stop presenting herself in that role.

Thursday combined institutional accountability with fresh executive overreach. Former special counsel Jack Smith testified before the House Judiciary Committee that he had developed proof beyond a reasonable doubt of criminal conduct by Trump related to overturning the 2020 election, facing sustained Republican efforts to discredit him rather than engage with his findings. The administration simultaneously threatened the law firms Perkins Coie and Jenner & Block with the loss of federal contracts and security clearances for representing his opponents, while Trump ordered Justice Department investigations of Miles Taylor, Chris Krebs, James Comey, Letitia James, and Federal Reserve Chair Jerome Powell, and threatened to prosecute DOJ officials unwilling to help overturn the 2020 election. In Minnesota, federal agents transported a two-year-old girl and her father to Texas despite a federal judge's explicit order prohibiting the transfer and requiring the child's release; she was eventually returned to her mother, though her father remained detained. The White House also posted a digitally altered image of Nekima Levy Armstrong, an activist arrested after a church protest, depicting her crying when the original photograph showed her composed — an alteration the administration did not deny. DHS separately ended Temporary Protected Status for more than 500,000 Venezuelans following the capture of President Nicolás Maduro, and the House passed a homeland security funding bill preserving ICE's $10 billion budget while rejecting, after an extended tied vote, a resolution barring troop deployment to Venezuela. A further wave of federal prosecutors resigned over the DOJ's refusal to investigate Good's killing, even as a magistrate judge declined to approve charges against journalist Don Lemon for covering the church protest.

The week closed on Friday with its most visible act of collective resistance. Roughly 50,000 Minnesotans took part in a coordinated economic blackout, the "Day of Truth and Freedom," closing hundreds of businesses and drawing a large indoor rally, while about 100 clergy members were arrested during a demonstration at Minneapolis–St. Paul International Airport. The same day, President Trump pardoned Changpeng Zhao, the Binance founder who had pleaded guilty to anti-money-laundering violations, shortly after Binance's $2 billion investment in Trump's family business, World Liberty Financial. Financial markets, meanwhile, sold off sharply over Trump's renewed tariff threats against Greenland — falling stocks, a weaker dollar, rising Treasury yields — and the president backed down from his aggressive stance by day's end. Philadelphia filed suit against the federal government after the National Park Service dismantled a historical exhibit detailing people enslaved by George Washington, and, in quieter developments, a Chicago jury acquitted a man accused of plotting to hire a hitman against a Border Patrol commander, while Affordable Care Act subsidies were allowed to lapse even as insurance executives testified before Congress about rising costs.

Taken as a whole, the week showed how enforcement power, once concentrated and unaccompanied by consistent oversight, can move quickly from policy to consequence: a shooting the government declined to examine, children used as instruments of arrest, a memo asserting the authority to enter homes without judicial warrants, and prosecutorial threats turned against sitting officials, journalists, and law firms. Courts intervened at several points — barring the use of force against protesters, questioning the removal of a Federal Reserve governor, enforcing limits on irregular appointments — but those interventions proved uneven, sometimes reversed within days by the same system meant to make them durable. What held, in the end, was public response: prosecutors who resigned rather than comply, tens of thousands of citizens who organized walkouts and blackouts, clergy who were arrested rather than stand aside, and a market that, however briefly, imposed its own discipline on executive rhetoric. Whether these acts of friction accumulate into lasting restraint, or simply mark the record of a week in which restraint repeatedly failed to hold, is the question the coming weeks will begin to answer.
```
