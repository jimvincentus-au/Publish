This Week in Democracy: Detention Expands, Epstein Files Fight Continues, and Coal Gets a Federal Mandate
A week of civil service rollbacks, mass detention expansion, a racist presidential social media post, and a grand jury's refusal to criminalize dissent
```markdown
This week in Washington moved on two tracks at once: a steady, structural expansion of executive power over the federal workforce, immigration enforcement, and environmental regulation, and a series of sharper confrontations — over racist presidential messaging, withheld infrastructure funds, and a fight for the Epstein files — that tested how much resistance still remained inside the system. By week's end, both tracks had left their mark, though not always in the direction the administration intended.
The weekend opened with a substantive institutional change. The Office of Personnel Management finalized a rule stripping job protections from roughly 50,000 federal employees, reclassifying them under a new "Schedule Policy/Career" designation that also moved whistleblower complaints away from independent review. The change reversed more than a century of nonpartisan civil service practice, replacing it with a structure more easily reshaped by political loyalty. Alongside it, President Trump withheld $16 billion in funding for the Gateway tunnel project, reportedly conditioning release of the money on renaming Penn Station and Dulles Airport after himself. A federal judge would later call the freeze arbitrary and order it reversed, though the administration appealed — an early sign that courts were prepared to push back on the use of federal spending as personal leverage.
The weekend also produced one of the week's ugliest moments: a video superimposing the Obamas' faces onto primates, posted from Trump's Truth Social account. The post drew condemnation from lawmakers of both parties, including Republican senators, before it was deleted. Trump acknowledged approving it but declined to apologize — a stark reminder that the presidency's communicative power can be turned toward degrading rather than elevating public discourse, with no mechanism to compel accountability beyond public criticism.
Underneath these headline moments, the week's most persistent structural story was already underway: the rapid growth of immigration detention. ICE conducted arrest operations in multiple cities over the weekend as DHS began securing new facilities near Philadelphia and San Antonio. The detained population, which had stood near 40,000, was surging past 70,000 under an expanded detention policy upheld by two Fifth Circuit judges — a near-doubling that would only accelerate as the week went on.
By Monday, the Epstein files dispute had become the week's second major throughline. Ghislaine Maxwell refused to answer questions in a deposition before the House Oversight Committee, invoking the Fifth Amendment, with her attorney suggesting she might testify fully only in exchange for clemency from Trump. Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick testified separately before the Senate, confirming a 2012 visit to Epstein's private island after previously misrepresenting his contacts — prompting calls for his resignation. The same day brought a different confrontation over dissent: former CNN anchor Don Lemon was indicted alongside other journalists and protesters for covering and participating in an anti-ICE demonstration at a Minnesota church, and federal prosecutors charged nine additional demonstrators with conspiracy. Civil liberties concerns deepened as reporting showed that fewer than 14 percent of nearly 400,000 immigrants arrested over the past year had faced violent criminal charges, even as an Irish national with a valid work permit remained in detention for five months. Courts intervened where they could — ordering the release of an asylum seeker whose detention was found unconstitutional and barring ICE from using teargas and projectiles against protesters in Portland — while thousands of high school students staged walkouts nationwide against ICE's tactics.
Tuesday shifted focus to environmental policy and election administration. The EPA announced plans to repeal the 2009 endangerment finding, the legal foundation for regulating greenhouse gases under the Clean Air Act, while also canceling offshore wind funding and ordering aging coal plants kept open. The same day, the FBI seized nearly 700 boxes of 2020 election materials from Fulton County, Georgia, acting on claims from election deniers that had already been debunked — a move that raised questions about using federal law enforcement to relitigate settled disputes over state election administration. Elsewhere, a Pride flag was removed from the Stonewall national monument under a new Interior Department policy, and federal research grants were rescinded under new ideological restrictions on discussing race or gender. Amid this, a Washington grand jury delivered a notable check on executive power, unanimously declining to indict six Democratic lawmakers, including Senators Mark Kelly and Elissa Slotkin, over a video urging service members to refuse illegal orders — a video the administration had characterized as seditious.
Wednesday brought the week's most concentrated cluster of institutional friction. The grand jury's decision was formalized, understood widely as a rebuke of U.S. Attorney Jeanine Pirro's office and a reaffirmation that political speech critical of the executive cannot be criminalized without evidence. The same day, Judge Richard Leon issued a preliminary injunction blocking the Pentagon from reducing Senator Kelly's retired military rank and pension, protections Defense Secretary Hegseth had sought to strip in apparent retaliation over the dissent video. Attorney General Pam Bondi appeared before the House Judiciary Committee and was widely criticized for deflecting questions, attacking Democratic members, and failing to acknowledge Epstein survivors in the room — testimony that came as revelations surfaced that the DOJ had been tracking lawmakers' searches of Epstein files, prompting Representatives Pramila Jayapal and Jamie Raskin to organize formal investigation demands. The EPA formally announced rescission of the endangerment finding, drawing promised legal challenges from environmental groups and states. Trump pardoned another January 6 defendant, and reporting revealed corporations including Mastercard, Deloitte, and ExxonMobil funding "Freedom 250" events tied to Trump's political messaging, with donor anonymity preserved even as Deloitte separately won more than $100 million in new ICE and CBP contracts.
Thursday combined legislative action with continued executive retreat under pressure. The House passed the SAVE America Act by a narrow 218-213 vote, imposing proof-of-citizenship and photo ID requirements for voting that excluded student identification — even as a separate DHS tool meant to flag noncitizens was found to be misidentifying citizens. Senate Democrats blocked a DHS funding package over demands for ICE accountability measures, including body cameras and agent identification, falling short of the 60 votes needed and leaving the department facing a partial shutdown as Congress adjourned. In a rare instance of enforcement retreating under sustained pressure, border czar Tom Homan announced the drawdown of "Operation Metro Surge" agents from Minnesota, where the operation had led to the deaths of two U.S. citizens, following weeks of protests and litigation. Judge Leon's injunction protecting Senator Kelly was formally entered. Separately, ICE Director Todd Lyons admitted that officers had lied under oath in an investigation that led to charges against two innocent men, prompting the DOJ to move to dismiss the charges. ICE's detained population reached its highest level since the agency's founding, with a majority held in for-profit facilities as CoreCivic's ICE-related revenue more than doubled. New Jersey's governor signed an executive order limiting immigration agents' access to state property without a judicial warrant, and DOJ antitrust chief Abigail Slater was forced out amid tension over her scrutiny of corporate mergers.
The week closed on Friday with confirmation that detention expansion was not a temporary surge but a long-term institutional commitment. ICE detailed plans to spend approximately $38.3 billion converting warehouses into detention centers, including sixteen regional processing centers and eight major facilities, funded through the One Big Beautiful Bill Act. DHS Secretary Kristi Noem announced the termination of Temporary Protected Status for Yemen, giving affected individuals sixty days before facing deportation to a country in active crisis. The DOJ faced fresh criticism over Epstein-file redactions that shielded alleged perpetrators' names while exposing victims'. The FDA declined to consider Moderna's application for an mRNA-based flu vaccine, a decision outside experts linked to a broader anti-vaccine posture within the agency, and analysis showed EPA enforcement actions against polluters had fallen sharply, with only one Clean Air Act consent decree filed compared to more than twenty in prior years. Leqaa Kordia, a Palestinian protester held by ICE for nearly a year despite two judicial rulings finding she posed no threat, remained in custody.
Taken as a whole, the week showed an administration extending its reach across the federal workforce, the immigration system, and environmental regulation through executive and agency action, while facing real, if uneven, resistance from courts, grand juries, and public pressure. A grand jury refused to let political dissent be treated as a crime. Judges blocked retaliation against a sitting senator and reversed an arbitrary funding freeze. Sustained protest and litigation ended an aggressive enforcement surge in Minnesota. These were not small things — they marked the limits of what could be imposed without consequence. But they operated alongside a $38 billion commitment to mass detention, the repeal of the legal foundation for climate regulation, and a civil service rule that quietly reduced the independence of the federal workforce itself. The week's lesson was less about any single event than about the uneven contest between institutional checks and administrative momentum — a contest in which both sides showed they could still win.
```
