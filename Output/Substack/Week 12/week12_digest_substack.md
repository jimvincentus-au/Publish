# Tariffs, Takedowns, and Two Weeks in El Salvador

*A week of market chaos, university coercion, and a wrongfully deported man the government wouldn't bring home*

![Header image](week12-digest-featured.jpg)

```markdown
The week of April 5 through April 11 opened with the largest coordinated street protests of the young administration and closed with the government openly defying a unanimous Supreme Court order. In between, financial markets convulsed under a self-inflicted tariff shock, universities faced an escalating campaign to bend them to federal demands, law firms were pressured into pledging nearly a billion dollars in "voluntary" legal tribute, and a single wrongfully deported man in a Salvadoran prison became the clearest test yet of whether the executive branch would answer to the courts at all.

The weekend began with numbers. Organizers with the 50501 Movement and Indivisible drew well over a million people into the streets of more than 1,300 cities under the banner "Hands Off," with tens of thousands filling the Washington Mall. Organizers billed it not as a single event but as the opening move of a sustained resistance effort ahead of the midterms. That same weekend, Trump declared a new tariff regime — a 10% universal tariff followed swiftly by escalating "reciprocal" rates, including 34% on Chinese goods — and markets responded immediately: the Dow lost more than 2,000 points in two days, trillions in value evaporated, and economists began warning openly of recession. Away from the cameras, the Justice Department placed career attorney Erez Reuveni on indefinite leave after he told a federal judge, truthfully, that the deportation of Kilmar Abrego Garcia to El Salvador had no legal basis. Attorney General Pam Bondi made clear that DOJ lawyers were expected to advocate zealously rather than candidly. At the same time, immigration authorities revoked roughly 300 student visas linked to pro-Palestinian activism, canceled all South Sudanese visas en masse, and detained Venezuelan Temporary Protected Status holders despite their statutory protections. Amid it all, more than 80 former employees of the law firm Skadden Arps publicly condemned its decision to settle with the administration rather than fight an executive order — an early sign of the pressure building on the legal profession.

By Monday, the week's central threads had begun to interlock. The Supreme Court, in a 5-4 ruling, allowed the administration to continue deportations under the 1798 Alien Enemies Act, requiring notice and habeas review but declining to block the practice through a nationwide injunction. Hours later, in a separate case, U.S. District Judge Paula Xinis found that Abrego Garcia's deportation had been "wholly lawless" and ordered the government to bring him back from the Salvadoran prison where he had been sent by what officials called an administrative error. The administration responded not with compliance but with an emergency motion to block the order. The law firm story split further that day: Willkie Farr, Milbank, and Skadden struck deals to perform pro bono work for causes aligned with the administration, while Perkins Coie, Jenner & Block, and WilmerHale won court injunctions blocking similar executive orders against them. Trump, meanwhile, turned his attention to the judiciary itself, calling for the impeachment and disbarment of Judge James Boasberg over an adverse deportation ruling — prompting a rare public rebuke from Chief Justice John Roberts — while the Justice Department separately sought to remove Judge Beryl Howell from a law firm case after she ruled against the government. Trump also proposed a $1 trillion defense budget, a sharp reversal from his February calls for reduced military spending, and bipartisan lawmakers introduced bills to reclaim tariff authority from the White House, though party leadership signaled it would keep the measures off the floor.

Tuesday brought a cluster of executive orders reshaping energy and environmental policy: directives to remove barriers to coal production, a two-year exemption for coal plants from mercury emissions standards, and instructions to the Attorney General to halt enforcement of state climate laws in New York, Vermont, and California. The same day, the D.C. Circuit ordered the reinstatement of two independent agency heads — Cathy Harris of the Merit Systems Protection Board and Gwynne Wilcox of the NLRB — citing a 90-year-old precedent shielding such officials from at-will removal, a ruling that set up a likely Supreme Court fight over presidential removal power. The university funding campaign began in earnest, with the administration freezing nearly $1 billion to Cornell and $790 million to Northwestern over allegations tied to campus activism and antisemitism. In smaller but telling developments, a federal judge — a Trump appointee — ordered the Associated Press's White House access restored, finding that barring the wire service over its refusal to adopt the phrase "Gulf of America" violated the First Amendment. And the case of 19-year-old Jose Hermosillo surfaced: a U.S. citizen held for nearly ten days by Border Patrol after an agent falsified a transcript claiming he had admitted to being undocumented.

Wednesday was dominated by the tariff standoff reaching its most volatile point. Trump raised tariffs on China to 104%, then to 125%, as Beijing vowed to "fight to the end." Hours after new reciprocal tariffs took effect against dozens of countries, Trump abruptly announced a 90-day pause for most of them while continuing to escalate specifically against China — a whiplash sequence that left allies and markets uncertain what U.S. trade policy actually was, and prompted questions in Congress about possible insider trading around the reversal. The same day, an executive order targeted Susman Godfrey, the firm that had represented Dominion Voting Systems in its $787.5 million defamation settlement against Fox News, stripping its lawyers of security clearances and restricting its federal contracts. Trump also directed the Justice Department to investigate former CISA director Chris Krebs, who had defended the integrity of the 2020 election, and former DHS official Miles Taylor, who had written critically about Trump; both lost their security clearances. A follow-on executive order required federal agencies to build automatic one-year sunset clauses into energy regulations unless affirmatively renewed, shifting the deregulatory default further in industry's favor.

Thursday delivered the week's most consequential single ruling. The Supreme Court, unanimously, ordered the government to facilitate Abrego Garcia's release from custody in El Salvador — a 9-0 rebuke from a court with a conservative supermajority, though ambiguous language about "effectuating" his return left room for the executive to maneuver. The university pressure campaign widened sharply the same day: the administration suspended dozens of research grants to Princeton, opened a review of $9 billion in contracts with Harvard, and paused $175 million to Penn over transgender athlete policies. Law firms kept adding to the running tally of concessions, with Milbank, Paul Weiss, and Willkie Farr each committing tens of millions more in pro bono work, prompting the American Bar Association to formally condemn what it called a "clear and disconcerting pattern" of attacks on judges and lawyers. Away from Washington, the North Carolina Supreme Court ruled that thousands of overseas and military voters must prove their eligibility within 30 days or have their already-cast ballots discarded — a decision that could overturn Democratic Justice Allison Riggs's narrow November victory and inject retroactive uncertainty into a certified election.

By Friday, the Abrego Garcia case reached its starkest point yet. A government attorney told the court he had no information about Abrego Garcia's whereabouts and that no steps had been taken to comply with the Supreme Court's order; Judge Xinis responded by ordering daily compliance updates as the administration continued to stall. The same day, Harvard became the week's most prominent institution to say no: its lawyers publicly rejected federal demands to install "viewpoint-diverse" faculty, eliminate diversity programs, and cede control over admissions and academic content in exchange for restoring $2.2 billion in frozen funding. Elsewhere, a presidential memorandum created a military buffer zone along the southern border, authorizing active-duty troops to detain migrants as trespassers, with a report due on whether to invoke the 1807 Insurrection Act. Five more major firms — including Kirkland & Ellis and Latham & Watkins — agreed to $600 million in pro bono commitments, pushing the week's total past $1 billion in coerced legal tribute. In a smaller but revealing episode, DOGE staffers attempted to embed personnel within the independent nonprofit Vera Institute of Justice before withdrawing once it became clear the group had already lost its DOJ funding.

Taken as a whole, the week showed an executive branch testing the outer edges of its authority on nearly every front at once — economic, judicial, academic, and legal — while the institutions built to check that authority responded unevenly. Courts pushed back with real force in several instances: reinstating fired agency officials, restoring press access, and, most significantly, ruling unanimously against the government in the Abrego Garcia case. Yet that unanimous order was met not with compliance but with silence and delay, raising a question more fundamental than any single policy dispute: whether judicial rulings still bind the executive when the executive simply declines to answer them. Congress, for its part, showed flickers of bipartisan resistance on tariffs but declined to force a vote. Universities and law firms split between capitulation and resistance, with Harvard's public refusal standing as the week's clearest institutional line drawn against federal coercion. And in North Carolina, a certified election faced the prospect of being unwound after the fact — a reminder that threats to democratic settlement do not always arrive from Washington, and do not always announce themselves as crises until the rules have already changed beneath the voters who trusted them.
```
