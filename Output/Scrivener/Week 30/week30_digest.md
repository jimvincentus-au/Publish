A Federal Takeover of the Capital, and a Data Chief Fired Over Bad News
This week: Trump seizes D.C.'s police force, purges the nation's economic statistics agency, and prepares to meet Putin — while courts, mayors, and lawmakers push back on multiple fronts.
```markdown
This was a week defined by the seizure of a city's police force, the firing of a government statistician, and a running argument, fought out in courtrooms and statehouses, over how much power the presidency can accumulate before something pushes back. By week's end, one of those fights had partially reversed course under legal pressure, while others — over economic data, immigration enforcement, and congressional maps — remained very much open.
The week's central story began quietly, with a threat. Over the weekend, Trump demanded that homeless residents of Washington, D.C. leave the city or face eviction, an ultimatum of uncertain legal grounding, while his lawyers explored ways to unwind the District's Home Rule Act entirely. It was a preview of what was to come. That same weekend, the Justice Department installed acting U.S. attorneys seen as partisan loyalists, a federal appeals court ordered the restoration of a public spending database the administration had taken down, and Director of National Intelligence Tulsi Gabbard declassified a report on Russian interference in the 2016 election — a move critics read as an attempt to rewrite the record, undertaken with Trump's encouragement. Trump also announced he would meet Vladimir Putin in Alaska on August 15, following reports that the two countries were discussing a deal requiring Ukraine to cede occupied territory, including Crimea — a negotiation unfolding without Ukraine or Congress at the table. Elsewhere, Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth reposted content from a Christian nationalist pastor opposing women's suffrage, a signal of what would become a recurring theme at the Pentagon.
By Monday, the D.C. threat had become policy. Trump signed an executive order declaring a crime emergency in the capital, placed the Metropolitan Police Department under the Attorney General's control, and deployed roughly 800 National Guard troops, invoking a rarely used clause of the Home Rule Act to do it. The stated justification collided with the data: violent crime in the District was at a 30-year low. Hegseth, meanwhile, escalated his own campaign, explicitly endorsing a call to repeal the 19th Amendment and announcing that the Pentagon would spend $10 million reinstalling a Confederate memorial at Arlington National Cemetery — one removed in 2003 for romanticizing slavery. Separately, reports emerged that the FBI under Kash Patel had reassigned agents away from monitoring far-right extremist groups. Courts offered some resistance: a fired January 6 prosecutor sued over his dismissal, a judge ordered $95 million released to the National Endowment for Democracy after finding its freeze unlawful, and another judge began hearing California's challenge to the military deployment used during protests in Los Angeles. Also on Monday, Trump nominated Heritage Foundation economist E.J. Antoni to lead the Bureau of Labor Statistics, having already fired the sitting commissioner over a jobs report he disliked.
Tuesday brought the D.C. takeover into full operational reality. At a press conference, Trump confirmed his control of the Metropolitan Police Department and sent in more than 100 FBI agents, 40 ATF agents, and other federal officers alongside the National Guard, with plans to clear homeless encampments. Far from the capital, the Texas Senate passed a Republican-drawn congressional map designed to flip five seats, despite a Democratic quorum-breaking walkout meant to block it; Attorney General Ken Paxton sought to jail Beto O'Rourke over alleged fundraising violations tied to his support for the absent legislators. In Los Angeles, California's governor and attorney general sued the Department of Justice, alleging that roughly 4,000 National Guard troops and 700 Marines had been deployed without authorization, in violation of the Posse Comitatus Act — a suit made more urgent by reports that troops had accompanied ICE arrests. The Justice Department also accused George Washington University of civil rights violations in its handling of 2024 pro-Palestinian protests, and sought sanctions against an immigration attorney accused of "reckless" filings on behalf of a client facing deportation.
Wednesday was the week's most crowded day. Trump formally fired BLS Commissioner Erika McEntarfer, calling her jobs report "rigged," and nominated Antoni as her replacement — completing an arc begun days earlier and confirming, in the eyes of critics, that the firing had been retaliatory rather than a response to any actual data failure. The administration also sent a letter to the Smithsonian announcing a review of its exhibitions, demanding they emphasize "American exceptionalism" and remove content it deemed divisive — an intervention echoing Hegseth's Confederate memorial decision two days earlier. Attorney General Pam Bondi sent letters to 32 cities and several counties threatening prosecution and the loss of federal funding unless they complied with federal immigration enforcement, giving them until August 19 to respond. The administration formally designated the Venezuelan gang Tren de Aragua a terrorist organization — a designation already being used to justify deportations under the Alien Enemies Act, despite intelligence-community skepticism about the extent of the Venezuelan government's involvement. Separately, Democratic Representatives Jerry Nadler and Dan Goldman reported being repeatedly denied entry to ICE detention facilities for oversight visits and filed suit over a new policy requiring a week's advance notice. Courts issued mixed rulings: an appeals court allowed the administration to proceed with cuts to billions in congressionally allocated foreign aid, a judge ordered improved conditions at a New York City detention facility, and a judge again refused to unseal Jeffrey Epstein associate Ghislaine Maxwell's grand jury files, criticizing the Justice Department for creating an "illusion of transparency." Trump also directed the Pentagon to use military force against drug cartels, doubled the bounty on Nicolás Maduro to $50 million, and — amid renewed scrutiny of his ties to Epstein — floated renaming the Kennedy Center after himself.
Thursday shifted some attention outward. Trump signed an executive order largely exempting commercial space launches from environmental review, benefiting companies including SpaceX and Blue Origin. A federal judge blocked the administration from cutting funding to schools and universities that declined to eliminate diversity programs, citing concerns about punishing lawful speech. A lawsuit filed in Louisiana alleged that ICE had unlawfully deported two mothers and their four children — including a boy being treated for cancer, all U.S. citizens — to Honduras without due process. In California, Governor Gavin Newsom announced that Democrats would move ahead with a redistricting plan adding five Democratic-leaning seats, contingent on Texas or other Republican-led states finalizing their own maps; he would later set a November 4 special election to let voters decide. Florida opened a second large immigration detention facility, a 1,300-bed site nicknamed "Deportation Depot," supplementing the already controversial "Alligator Alcatraz." In Washington, federal agents continued sweeping city streets under the National Guard deployment, an operation partly justified by an assault on a former DOGE staffer, and Trump suggested he might seek congressional authority — or bypass Congress altogether through emergency powers — to make his control of the police department permanent. Reporting also detailed Hegseth's removal of transgender troops and senior women and officers of color from the ranks, alongside continued Christian nationalist messaging in Pentagon recruitment material.
Friday brought the week's most significant reversal. Facing a lawsuit from D.C.'s attorney general and a Democratic-led congressional resolution to end the federalization, the White House and District officials reached an agreement returning day-to-day control of the Metropolitan Police Department to Chief Pamela Smith, while the federal government retained emergency-use authority. It was not a full retreat, but it was a real one, achieved through legal and legislative pressure rather than executive reconsideration. The same day brought a darker development in Los Angeles: a man died fleeing an uncoordinated ICE raid at a Home Depot that resulted in ten detentions, and armed Border Patrol agents appeared at a Newsom press event at the Japanese American National Museum, which the governor described as an act of intimidation. Texas Governor Greg Abbott called a second special session to keep pursuing the redistricting map, while Newsom urged Americans to actively resist what he called an assault on democratic institutions. A federal appeals court lifted a block on the mass firing of 1,500 employees at the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau, and, in an unrelated but notable accountability story, New Orleans Mayor LaToya Cantrell was indicted on federal corruption charges. A Georgia judge dismissed domestic terrorism charges against a "Cop City" protester, citing due process violations after prolonged delay. And across the week's final days, civic organizing continued at street level — disinformation-monitoring efforts, planned mobilizations in Raleigh, bystander training for ICE encounters, and pressure campaigns aimed at private companies tied to enforcement contracts.
Taken as a whole, the week traced a familiar shape: an assertion of executive power, justified by claims the record did not support, followed by resistance from courts, local officials, and eventually a negotiated retreat — though only a partial one, with the underlying claim to authority left unresolved. The firing of a nonpartisan economic statistician for delivering unwelcome numbers, and her replacement with a partisan loyalist, raised a different but related question: whether the government's own data can still be trusted to describe the country accurately. Meanwhile, older battles over historical memory, immigration enforcement, and electoral maps continued to widen, each testing, in its own way, how much friction the system's remaining checks — judicial, legislative, and civic — can still generate against the exercise of concentrated power.
```
