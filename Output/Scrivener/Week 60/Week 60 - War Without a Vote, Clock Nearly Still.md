Week 60: War Without a Vote, Clock Nearly Still
Unauthorized war, coerced legislation, and denied civilian deaths met courts and one senator's refusal, leaving the week's net movement almost frozen.
Last week closed at 7:43 p.m. This week ends at 7:43 p.m. as well, though the clock's stillness hides real movement underneath. Week 60 brought war without a vote, coercion without a majority, and falsehood without correction. It also brought pushback: from courts, from one senator's conscience, from a fragile filibuster that held. The net change was small, a loss of twenty-four seconds. The week's damage and the week's resistance nearly canceled each other out. But near-cancellation is not safety. It means two forces of roughly equal strength are grinding against each other inside the machinery of government. That grinding wears the machine down.
The clock moved from 463.4 minutes to 463.0, a shift of four-tenths of a minute. The number is small. It should not comfort the reader. Consider what the week actually held. A president started and escalated an unauthorized war. He threatened to freeze all other legislation until Congress passed a sweeping voting-restriction bill. His administration denied responsibility for a strike that killed children. In the same seven days, federal judges voided unlawful appointments, a Senate leader refused to gut the filibuster under direct presidential pressure, and reporters forced the Pentagon to correct casualty numbers it had tried to bury. The week did not resolve toward tyranny or toward restraint. It sat, uneasily, between the two. The clock recorded that standoff, not a verdict.
The week's most consequential development began on a single morning. Trump ordered air and naval strikes against Iran without asking Congress for authorization. Within hours, Democratic senators filed War Powers Act resolutions, the traditional response to unilateral war-making. Republican leadership set them aside. The president's own account of the war's purpose shifted almost daily. He demanded unconditional surrender, then floated vague terms for ending the conflict, then reversed himself again. He dismissed expected American casualties as an acceptable price. According to aides, he discussed assassinating Iran's Supreme Leader. At a private resort summit, he raised the possibility of regime change in Cuba, hinting at an appetite for war that reached well past the immediate crisis. Congress's oversight tools — hearings, resolutions, classified briefings — could not slow any of it. Power moved decisively toward the presidency. Nothing moved back.
The war's second front was fought over language, not territory. As the campaign proceeded, the administration mixed battlefield claims with outright fabrication. Trump falsely denied U.S. responsibility for a strike that killed roughly 175 people at a girls' school in Iran, most of them children, even after an internal military investigation reportedly traced the strike to American targeting. He claimed Iran possessed Tomahawk missiles, a claim with no basis, apparently meant to shift blame. The Energy Secretary posted a false claim about a Navy tanker escort in the Strait of Hormuz, then deleted it. The Pentagon first reported fewer than a dozen wounded troops; later reporting forced a revision to roughly one hundred forty. White House officials blocked a joint FBI-DHS intelligence bulletin warning of domestic threats tied to the war, apparently judging the political cost of the warning higher than its safety value. None of this was subtle. It was denial met by correction met by more denial, the machinery of official truth-telling running in reverse.
The economic costs of the war landed on ordinary Americans first. Oil prices rose to their highest level since 2022 as the Strait of Hormuz came under threat. Gas topped five dollars a gallon in California. The Pentagon disclosed war costs above eleven billion dollars in the first six days alone, with munitions stockpiles depleting fast enough to raise readiness concerns. Trump dismissed the price increases as good for the country. Meanwhile, sanctions on Russian oil exports were eased during the same period — a benefit to a country waging its own war against a U.S. ally. Senators flagged the contradiction. No one corrected it. The week's economic story was simple: costs moved downward, and no one at the top paid them.
Against this backdrop, the president turned to domestic legislation with the same disregard for procedure. He threatened to veto every bill Congress sent him unless lawmakers passed the Save America Act, a measure requiring proof-of-citizenship documents and eliminating most mail balloting. The House passed it. He then pressed Senate Republicans to eliminate the filibuster specifically to force the bill through. Majority Leader Thune refused. It was a small thing procedurally: one rule, held in place under direct and repeated presidential pressure. But it mattered. It was one of the only moments all week when a Republican official declined to give the president exactly what he demanded. The bill's citizenship-documentation requirements threaten to disenfranchise millions of eligible voters, particularly women whose legal names differ from their birth records. The fight was not settled this week. It was paused, at the edge of a rule that has not yet broken.
Utah supplied a second, more direct case of a captured institution. State Republicans expanded their supreme court and moved to block voters from withdrawing signatures from a repeal petition targeting an anti-gerrymandering map, timed just before the repeal vote. This was not incidental housekeeping. It was a deliberate effort to reshape a court's composition ahead of an unfavorable ruling — an attempt to win, in advance, a case that had not yet been argued. The maneuver targeted a voter-approved reform. That made its purpose only clearer.
Yet the same week that saw this kind of manipulation also saw courts push back with unusual consistency. A federal judge ruled that Kari Lake's leadership of Voice of America's parent agency had been unlawful from the start and voided her mass layoffs and restructuring. Another judge found that three federal prosecutors in New Jersey had been installed without Senate confirmation, violating the Appointments Clause and the Vacancies Act — the second such ruling this year. In Oregon, a federal judge found ICE had conducted warrantless, quota-driven arrests targeting people by ethnicity, a clear Fourth Amendment violation. And a federal judge quashed a grand jury subpoena aimed at Federal Reserve Chair Jerome Powell, finding its true purpose was to punish an official who had displeased the president. Four rulings, four different domains, one consistent message: courts were still willing to name unlawful conduct and undo it. This is why the trait tracking judicial politics moved downward this week rather than up. It is narrow good news, won case by case, not yet settled principle.
Immigration enforcement moved the other way. The administration ended Temporary Protected Status for roughly 2,500 Somali nationals amid a humanitarian crisis in their home country, then launched "Operation Buckeye," a targeted enforcement campaign against Somali American communities in Ohio, following inflammatory rhetoric about the community. At the same time, internal DHS oversight offices meant to investigate detention abuse were slashed even as in-custody deaths climbed past fifteen for the year. A journalist covering immigration enforcement was detained by ICE without agents displaying a warrant. Two teenage mariachi musicians, held despite lawful status, were released only after bipartisan public backlash — a rare case of the system correcting itself under pressure rather than by design. The pattern across these events was consistent: enforcement expanded fastest exactly where oversight was being torn down.
Press freedom came under sharper attack than in recent weeks. The FCC's chairman threatened to revoke broadcast licenses over war coverage he called "fake news." The Pentagon banned photographers from war briefings after unflattering images of the Defense Secretary circulated. That same secretary publicly called for a takeover of a major news network by an allied party, all while refusing to answer basic questions about the girls'-school strike. Voice of America, still under a legally unconfirmed leader for part of the week, announced a content partnership with a partisan outlet before courts intervened. Any one of these actions might be dismissed as bluster. Together, they describe a government reaching for regulatory and ownership leverage over the press, not just rhetorical attack. That is a different and more dangerous tool.
Dissent met force in smaller, more personal ways too. A sitting senator physically confronted and injured a protester at a hearing. A judge, resolving a years-old prosecution tied to the Cop City protests, approved a plea deal that included a ten-year ban on the defendant's participation in protests or "anti-government" rallies. That restriction on political expression will outlast this presidency, regardless of its outcome. Yet organizers announced plans for a nationwide day of action in late March, with thousands of events already scheduled. The capacity for mass peaceful assembly had not been extinguished. It operated alongside a growing willingness, in isolated cases, to punish individual dissenters severely and for years to come.
Money's grip on politics received fresh, if not new, documentation. A New York Times investigation found that three hundred billionaire families now supply nineteen percent of federal election contributions nationwide, skewed roughly five to one toward Republican candidates. Billionaire spending also flowed into California's governor's race and into defeating a state wealth tax. None of this changed how money moves through American politics. It sharpened the picture of an arrangement built over years, now visible in finer detail.
The governing party showed its own strain. A House Republican left the party to become an independent, narrowing an already thin majority. A nominee for a State Department post withdrew after Republican senators voiced opposition. The Speaker declined to condemn openly anti-Muslim remarks by members of his own conference. None of these cracks changed outcomes this week. Together, though, they suggest a coalition holding under more strain than its public unity implies.
Elsewhere, the Justice Department released previously withheld Epstein-related interview documents, partial and incomplete but real — one of the only concrete steps toward disclosure this week. It sits uneasily beside the department's continued refusal to release most of the remaining files. Transparency this week arrived only in fragments, forced rather than offered.
The Senate is scheduled to vote on the Save America Act in the coming days, a vote its own sponsors expect to fail without the filibuster's removal. Utah's court-packing measure and repeal petition remain before voters. Both outcomes will decide whether this week's paused confrontations bend toward restraint or toward further erosion.
Week 60 will likely be remembered less for any single act than for its texture: a government waging war by improvisation, defending its falsehoods by attrition, and testing every legislative and judicial boundary within reach in a single seven-day span. That courts and one Senate leader held several of those boundaries in place matters. It does not undo the war launched without a vote. It does not undo the children killed at a school whose cause was denied for days. It does not undo the community in Ohio targeted for what its members are, rather than what they had done. The clock barely moved this week. The forces beneath it did not stand still.
