Week 45: Rhetoric Becomes Apparatus
A president's call to execute dissenting lawmakers turned into Pentagon and FBI action this week, even as courts pushed back on parallel abuses of power.
The week that ended on the seventh day of Thanksgiving break did not open with a single shock. It opened with a video: six Democratic lawmakers, all military veterans, reminding American troops that they need not obey an unlawful order. What followed showed, with unusual clarity, how a government responds when reminded of a limit. It did not debate the reminder. It moved to punish those who gave it.
At the close of the previous week, the Democracy Clock stood at 7:42 p.m. It closes this week at the same time, a net movement of just over ten seconds backward toward midnight. The number is almost too small to notice, but small numbers can mask large collisions. An extraordinary presidential escalation against sitting lawmakers pushed the clock forward this week; court rulings dismissing unlawful prosecutions and blocking partisan gerrymanders pulled it back almost as far. The result was near stillness — but stillness produced by force meeting resistance, not by calm.
The week's central story began with that video and did not let go. Trump called it seditious. He said the conduct shown was "punishable by death." He posted calls to arrest the lawmakers, and reposted messages urging they be hanged. This was not metaphor from a rally stage. It was a sitting president, using his official channels, urging the killing of members of Congress for stating settled constitutional doctrine: that troops may refuse illegal orders.
The words did not stay words. The Pentagon opened a formal inquiry into Senator Mark Kelly. Defense Secretary Hegseth threatened recall to active duty and court-martial. Days later, the FBI opened a counterterrorism investigation into all six lawmakers, contacting congressional sergeants at arms to arrange interviews. Federal law enforcement and military discipline were aimed at Congress, over a statement of law. Nothing like this had occurred before in the term. It turned rhetoric into apparatus almost overnight.
The traits most affected reflect this shift precisely. Dissent reframed as treason moved up sharply, as did the labeling of dissenters as unpatriotic or subversive. Police and military alignment with elite preservation rose as well, since the instrument used against Kelly and the others was not a mob or a cable news host, but the Department of Defense itself. Power did not simply speak against its critics this week. It sent institutions built for war after them.
A second story ran alongside it, and for a moment seemed to cut the other way. A federal judge dismissed the indictments against James Comey and Letitia James. The court ruled that the prosecutor Trump had installed, Lindsey Halligan, was never lawfully appointed, finding a clear Appointments Clause violation: the president had bypassed Senate confirmation to install a loyalist prosecutor aimed at critics. It was a clean ruling, unmoved by political pressure, and it mattered.
But the dismissal did not end the underlying pattern. The same week, Trump publicly ordered Attorney General Bondi to prosecute Comey, James, Schiff, and others by name. A grand jury was empaneled to pursue what was described internally as a "lawfare Democrats" probe. The Justice Department, meanwhile, lost or reassigned roughly 5,500 career attorneys. Civil rights and corruption enforcement hollowed out while resources shifted toward the president's declared enemies. The court's ruling closed one door; the administration was already working through several others.
Elsewhere, foreign policy took its own strange turn. Leaked transcripts and bipartisan senator briefings revealed that a "peace plan" for Ukraine, presented publicly as an American document, had in fact been substantially drafted with Russian officials — including a sanctioned envoy — and delivered through Trump aides Witkoff and Kushner. Secretary of State Rubio publicly insisted the plan was American work. Senators said he had privately described it, in their own accounts, as a Russian wish list. Witkoff was reported to have coached Russian officials on how to present the plan so Trump would accept it.
This was not a policy disagreement. It was, if the reporting holds, a foreign government helping shape and disguise the authorship of a major American diplomatic initiative. The trait tracking foreign influence welcomed when useful to those in power moved by a wide margin this week, one of the largest single shifts in the period. A government that cannot say plainly where its own policy came from has surrendered something basic: the public's ability to know who is actually governing.
Immigration enforcement expanded on several fronts at once. Each move was individually defensible in official language; together they formed a pattern of collective punishment. Temporary Protected Status was terminated for nearly four thousand Myanmar nationals amid active civil war, and later in the week for hundreds of thousands of Haitians amid gang-controlled displacement. A single shooting in Washington, attributed to an Afghan asylee, triggered a blanket halt to Afghan immigration processing and a review of prior asylum grants. An entire nationality was made to answer for one act.
Deportations proceeded in at least two cases despite active court orders barring them, including the removal of a college student and a trans woman found likely to face torture. Sanctuary protections for churches were formally lifted, permitting ICE arrests inside houses of worship for the first time in years. When clergy gathered peacefully outside a Chicago detention facility to protest, they were met with pepper spray and mass arrest. The image of faith leaders forced back from a fence line captured, more than any single policy memo, what enforcement without restraint now looks like in practice.
Trump's own language sharpened alongside the policy. On Thanksgiving, he posted a message using a slur against Governor Walz and spreading false ethnic claims about Somali refugees and Representative Ilhan Omar. He separately floated denaturalization and a permanent migration bar from what he called "third-world countries." These were not slips. They formed a pattern: citizenship treated as conditional, revocable, and sorted by heritage rather than law.
Pressure on the press took a more lasting, institutional form this week than in prior periods. The White House launched an official government website, styled as a "Media Bias Tracker," naming specific journalists and outlets as offenders. This differs from a presidential insult, which passes with the news cycle — a government website persists. It is infrastructure, not mood. Trump also personally and repeatedly insulted the appearance and competence of female reporters covering his health and public statements. The administration reportedly discussed removing CNN hosts through corporate ownership channels.
A quieter but consequential story ran through redistricting. Trump pushed a coordinated national campaign to redraw congressional maps outside the normal decennial process, threatening primary challenges against Indiana state senators who balked. Courts pushed back in two states: a federal panel blocked a Texas gerrymander, and a Utah judge struck a map that violated a voter-approved anti-gerrymandering amendment. Missouri voters pursued both litigation and a referendum against their own state's map. The fight is unresolved, but it showed both the reach of the executive's ambition and the limits courts and citizens can still impose on it.
Economic governance showed signs of strain met mostly with evasion. The administration canceled the advance Q3 GDP estimate days after officials credited artificial intelligence investment with driving much of the year's growth, a cancellation that invites suspicion regardless of its stated cause. Treasury Secretary Bessent dismissed inflation concerns even while acknowledging sectoral contraction. Utility shutoffs rose amid climbing electricity costs, and Starbucks workers escalated a strike during the retail industry's busiest week. None of this rose to scandal on its own, but together it described a government more comfortable managing appearances than addressing hardship.
Self-enrichment and unilateral claims of power rounded out the record. Trump pardoned former Honduran president Juan Orlando Hernández, convicted of running a large-scale cocaine trafficking operation. The pardon stretched clemency power across borders to protect a politically aligned figure. He claimed, without clear legal grounding, that he could nullify his predecessor's executive orders on the theory that an autopen signature was invalid, while threatening Biden with perjury charges. A taxpayer-funded ballroom expansion continued at his own property, reportedly exposing workers to asbestos. None of these actions required force. They required only that no one stop them.
Two smaller developments deserve their own note, precisely because they were not escalations. Northwestern University reached a settlement conditioning restoration of $790 million in federal research funds on new limits on campus protest, extending a coercive funding pattern to another major university. FBI Director Patel's placement of a twenty-two-year-old with no relevant background atop counterterrorism operations, alongside a vaccine-skeptic loyalist installed at CDC, showed the ongoing preference for loyalty over competence in sensitive posts. Neither event broke new ground. Both confirmed ground already broken.
The week closed with two threads left open. A bipartisan Senate Armed Services Committee announced a formal inquiry into potentially unlawful follow-on military strikes, its outcome unknown at week's end. Texas's appeal of its blocked gerrymander, alongside Republican efforts to challenge California's map, left the redistricting fight for 2026 unresolved as the period closed.
What this week showed, more than any single act within it, was how thin the line has become between a government asserting authority and a government threatening those who question it. The call for lawmakers' execution did not stand alone. Within days, the machinery of the Pentagon and the FBI moved to make the threat real. That two federal courts, in the same week, found room to say no — to an unlawful prosecutor, to a racial gerrymander — is real, and matters. But it does not erase what stood beside it: a president naming enemies by title and office, and agencies of the state, however briefly, answering the call.
