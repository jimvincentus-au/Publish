# The Week the Courts Pushed Back — and the Executive Pushed Harder

*Mass deportations, a defense secretary's unraveling, a university's defiance, and a sitting judge's arrest defined a week of escalating confrontation between branches of government*

![Header image](week14-digest-featured.jpg)

```markdown
The week of April 19 to 25 opened with courts intervening to stop deportations already in motion and closed with the FBI arresting a sitting judge. In between, a university refused to bend to federal pressure, a Cabinet secretary's handling of classified information collapsed into a full leadership crisis, and the administration signed nine executive orders in a single day rewriting how the federal government enforces civil rights. By any measure, it was a week in which the branches of government tested each other's limits — at one point prompting a federal appellate judge to warn publicly of constitutional breakdown.

The weekend began with an overnight emergency order from the Supreme Court halting the deportation of Venezuelan men already loaded onto planes bound for removal under the Alien Enemies Act. Justices Thomas and Alito dissented, but the majority acted again hours later for a second group headed to El Salvador. At the same time, the administration was telling a separate court that it had no obligation to correct the wrongful deportation of Kilmar Abrego Garcia, even after admitting in filings that the removal had been a mistake. The juxtaposition — courts intervening in real time to stop removals, while the government resisted fixing an acknowledged error — set the tone for the week ahead.

That same weekend, the White House opened a new front, announcing funding freezes against Brown, Northwestern, Princeton, and Cornell as part of a campaign pressuring universities over curriculum, admissions, hiring, and diversity programs — the opening move in what would become one of the week's defining confrontations. Meanwhile, hundreds of thousands of people turned out in more than 400 cities in protests organized by groups including 50501 and Indivisible, timed to the 250th anniversary of Lexington and Concord and citing due-process violations and fears of executive overreach. After Senator Chris Van Hollen visited Abrego Garcia in El Salvador, the White House posted a digitally altered image purporting to show gang tattoos on his hand and doctored a New York Times headline to falsely label him an MS-13 member — a fabrication of evidence aimed at a person still subject to contested legal proceedings. Away from these clashes, the administration promoted coal expansion while cutting nearly 900 positions at the agency that screens miners for black lung disease and pausing enforcement of a new silica dust rule, a quieter but consequential retreat from worker protection.

By Monday, a new crisis was emerging at the Pentagon. Reporting revealed that Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth had shared detailed information about a Yemen strike — including flight schedules — in a private Signal chat with his wife, brother, and personal associates, a second such disclosure separate from an earlier chat involving National Security Adviser Mike Waltz. Trump publicly defended Hegseth, but the secretary's own former spokesman published an editorial describing "total chaos" inside the department. The story would not go away; it would deepen across the week. Monday also brought fresh evidence of enforcement reaching citizens and legal residents: a 19-year-old American, Jose Hermosillo, had been held for ten days by Border Patrol despite his family providing his birth certificate; an Irish green card holder was detained at San Francisco's airport over decades-old, expunged convictions; a Palestinian green card holder was detained during his own naturalization interview. In Congress, the House passed the SAVE Act, which would require in-person proof of citizenship to register to vote and ban counting of late-arriving mail ballots — a measure with uncertain prospects in the Senate but clear implications for ballot access. Detained activist Mahmoud Khalil was denied release to attend the birth of his son, participating only by phone, while the EEOC sent unsolicited surveys to Barnard College employees asking about Jewish or Israeli identity as part of an antisemitism inquiry. At the Interior Department, Secretary Doug Burgum delegated control over 70,000 employees and the full budget to an unconfirmed political appointee, sidestepping the Senate's constitutional role in appointments.

Tuesday sharpened the university confrontation. Harvard became the first institution to sue the administration, challenging the freeze of $2.2 billion in funding and threats to its tax-exempt status after refusing to submit to government audits of viewpoint diversity or dismantle its diversity programs. University President Alan Garber said Harvard would not surrender its independence. The same day, Judge Royce Lamberth ordered the restoration of Voice of America, ruling that the administration's attempt to dismantle the congressionally funded broadcaster was a direct affront to legislative authority. In comments that crystallized the week's central conflict, Trump stated that judges could not require trials before deportation, even as allied commentators publicly floated ignoring the Supreme Court altogether or deporting judges themselves. Secretary of State Marco Rubio announced a sweeping State Department reorganization eliminating bureaus for democracy promotion, human rights, and refugee assistance and cutting over 700 positions. A congressional delegation visited detained students Khalil and Rümeysa Öztürk, calling their detention political imprisonment, while reporting revealed DOGE was consolidating immigration, tax, Social Security, and voting records into a single government database.

Wednesday was the week's most concentrated exercise of unilateral power. President Trump signed nine executive orders in a single day, eliminating disparate-impact liability across federal civil rights statutes, overhauling college accreditation standards to target diversity programs, rescinding Obama-era guidance on racial disparities in school discipline, mandating foreign-funding disclosure by universities, and launching new initiatives on historically Black colleges and AI education. The disparate-impact change alone removed a legal tool used for decades to address systemic discrimination in employment, housing, and education — a shift whose practical consequences would surface within a day. That same Wednesday, the Fourth Circuit Court of Appeals denied the administration's request to halt orders requiring compliance in the Abrego Garcia case, and Judge Harvie Wilkinson — a Reagan appointee — warned that continued defiance risked the branches "grinding irrevocably against one another." Elsewhere, a federal jury found the New York Times not liable in Sarah Palin's defamation retrial, reaffirming the actual-malice standard that protects reporting on public figures. Hegseth, for his part, blamed ousted officials for leaking word of his Signal use and defended his reliance on encrypted messaging, even as reports surfaced of an unsecured internet line installed in his office to bypass Pentagon security protocols. On Ukraine, Rubio withdrew from scheduled peace talks in London, the U.S. recast its proposal as a "final offer," and Trump publicly pressured Ukrainian President Zelensky to accept Russian control of Crimea, blaming him for prolonging the war.

Thursday showed the disparate-impact order's consequences almost immediately: the Justice Department dropped a 2023 settlement that had protected a majority-Black Alabama community from raw sewage exposure, calling the agreement an unlawful diversity initiative. A separate executive order stripped job security from probationary federal employees, shifting the burden onto workers to justify their continued employment and enabling faster, less accountable dismissals — a structural change to the civil service beyond any single firing. Trump also directed the Justice Department to investigate ActBlue, the Democratic Party's primary fundraising platform, based on unsubstantiated claims of fraud, a move widely read as targeting the opposition's financial infrastructure ahead of the midterms. Yet the week's pattern of judicial pushback continued at scale: federal judges blocked eleven separate administration actions within days of each other, including the dismantling of Voice of America, Alien Enemies Act removals in Colorado and New York, anti-diversity education rules, funding impoundment against noncompliant cities, and the elimination of federal collective bargaining rights. The volume of rulings illustrated courts functioning less as isolated obstacles than as a sustained, cumulative check. Separately, the Interior Department invoked a disputed energy emergency to cut environmental review timelines for fossil fuel permits from years to 28 days, drawing immediate legal challenges, while a leaked HHS draft proposed eliminating disability services and research even as the NIH announced it would begin collecting private medical records to study autism.

Friday delivered the week's most dramatic single event: the FBI arrested Milwaukee County Circuit Judge Hannah Dugan at her own courthouse, accusing her of helping a defendant evade ICE agents during a courtroom proceeding. Attorney General Pam Bondi called judges "deranged" on television and warned of prosecuting officials who "harbor fugitives." It was the first known criminal arrest of a sitting local judge over a discretionary courtroom decision, landing as the culmination of a week that had already seen a sitting president reject due process as a precondition for deportation and a federal appellate judge warn of the branches grinding against one another. Two more rulings that day reinforced the pattern of judicial resistance: Judge William Orrick found the administration's threats to withhold funding from sanctuary cities unconstitutional, and Judge Colleen Kollar-Kotelly blocked the executive order requiring proof of citizenship for voter registration, ruling that the president lacked authority to bypass Congress on the matter. But Friday also brought the week's gravest human toll: ICE deported three U.S. citizen children, including one with Stage 4 cancer sent without medical consultation, and separately deported a two-year-old citizen to Honduras — a removal a federal judge called "illegal and unconstitutional" while scheduling an emergency hearing. These were not documented immigrants caught in enforcement error; they were American children, removed alongside detained mothers. Elsewhere, former congressman George Santos was sentenced to 87 months in prison for fraud and identity theft, a reminder that ordinary accountability mechanisms continued to function even amid broader strain. The administration also reversed its earlier revocation of thousands of foreign student visas after 104 lawsuits and more than 50 injunctions across 23 states forced its hand, and China canceled over 12,000 metric tons of American pork purchases in retaliation for tariffs, with effective duties reaching 172 percent and rural exporters absorbing the cost.

Taken as a whole, the week showed two things happening at once. Courts, at every level from district judges to the Supreme Court to a Reagan-appointed appellate judge, repeatedly intervened against executive actions on deportation, funding, worker protections, and broadcasting — and largely prevailed in the sense that their orders were issued and, for now, stood. Universities, led by Harvard, demonstrated that institutional resistance to federal pressure remained possible. Yet the administration met that resistance not with retreat but with acceleration: a single day of nine executive orders narrowing civil rights enforcement, a president publicly rejecting the premise of due process, and, by week's end, the arrest of a judge for a discretionary act on the bench. The deportation of citizen children — one gravely ill, one a toddler — stood apart even within a week already marked by wrongful detentions, a signal that the machinery of enforcement was reaching people the law was never meant to touch. Whether the courts' persistence or the executive's escalation would define the weeks ahead was, by Friday, still an open question, but the shape of the confrontation was no longer in doubt.
```
