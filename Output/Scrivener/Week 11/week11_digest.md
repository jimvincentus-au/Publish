Tariffs, Term Limits, and the Price of Defiance
A week in which a university surrendered, a senator talked for 25 hours, and the president's trade war erased trillions from the market
```markdown
This week, spanning the last weekend of March through the first Friday of April, brought together two forces that would define its shape: a steady tightening of executive pressure on universities, courts, and immigrants, and a sudden economic shock the president brought on himself. By week's end, a research university had surrendered part of its academic governance, a senator had spoken for a full day on the Senate floor, a wrongfully deported man remained in a Salvadoran prison despite a judge's order, and the stock market had lost more value in forty-eight hours than at almost any point in American history.
The weekend set the tone. Facing a threatened $400 million cut in federal funding over allegations that it had failed to address antisemitism on campus, Columbia University placed its Middle Eastern studies department under outside receivership and imposed new restrictions on pro-Palestinian protest. The university's interim president resigned within days. Faculty unions sued, students and alumni protested, and some graduates publicly destroyed their diplomas. It was a stark demonstration of how directly federal money could reshape what a university teaches and permits its students to say — and it would not be the last such demonstration this week. At the same time, the administration moved against individual activists: Secretary of State Marco Rubio defended revoking visas from student protesters, and the government pursued deportation of Columbia graduate Mahmoud Khalil under rarely invoked provisions of immigration law, even as a federal judge blocked a similar action against another Columbia student. Institutional capitulation on one side, individual targeting on the other — the pairing became a pattern that would recur all week.
Also over the weekend, in an NBC interview, Trump said he was "not joking" about seeking a third term and claimed unspecified "methods" existed for bypassing the 22nd Amendment. Days later his own attorney general, Pam Bondi, publicly downplayed the idea, calling it "a heavy lift" — a rare instance of a senior official contradicting the president on the constitutional limits of his own power. Separately, Elon Musk turned a Wisconsin Supreme Court race into a spectacle of money in politics, spending more than $20 million backing a candidate and personally handing out $1 million checks to voters who signed a petition. A state lawsuit challenging the payments went nowhere on procedural grounds, while "Tesla Takedown" protests spread to 253 cities. Meanwhile the administration sued CBS News for $10 billion and the Des Moines Register over a pre-election poll, and the FCC opened investigations into NBC, NPR, and PBS — a widening effort to use litigation and regulatory power against unfavorable coverage.
By Monday, the week's central act of legislative resistance began. Senator Cory Booker took the Senate floor at 7 p.m. and did not stop talking for twenty-five hours, breaking the record Strom Thurmond had set in 1957. Booker used the time to catalog what he called threats to democracy — economic disruption, cuts to health and education programs, erosion of due process — turning a procedural tool into a sustained act of public protest that would be invoked again and again through the week as a symbol of resistance within institutions still capable of resisting. His speech landed alongside a wave of federal agency retrenchment: EPA Administrator Lee Zeldin terminated $14 billion in climate grants and moved to cut more than 1,100 scientist positions, plans a federal judge would later block. The administration also froze Title X family-planning funds, canceled $1 billion in food-bank funding, and, at HHS, began laying the groundwork for what would become mass layoffs the next day.
Monday also produced the week's clearest instance of admitted government error met with refusal to correct it. In a court filing, the government acknowledged it had deported Kilmar Abrego Garcia, a legal resident with protected status, to a Salvadoran prison due to "administrative error" — but argued it lacked the authority to bring him back. Vice President JD Vance compounded the episode by falsely claiming on social media that Abrego Garcia was a "convicted" gang member. The case would anchor the week's due-process concerns, alongside a lawsuit alleging ICE had deported three U.S.-citizen children, including a four-year-old with stage-4 cancer, without due process or the chance to arrange alternative custody. Elsewhere, House Republican leadership tried and failed to block a bipartisan proxy-voting measure for new parents — a minor but telling sign of diminished control over their own caucus — and federal judges blocked the firing of CIA and ODNI employees tied to DEI programs and delayed termination of Temporary Protected Status for Venezuelan migrants, with one judge calling the government's justification "predicated on negative stereotypes." Democratic attorneys general filed the first of three lawsuits challenging Trump's executive order imposing proof-of-citizenship and mail-ballot restrictions on federal elections.
On Tuesday, the administration's campaign against federal health capacity reached its fullest expression. Health Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr. oversaw layoffs eliminating roughly a quarter of the HHS workforce — about 10,000 employees — hitting programs on lead poisoning, tobacco policy, bird flu surveillance, and maternal health monitoring, even as measles and whooping cough outbreaks remained active. The same day, the administration announced a review of $9 billion in federal grants to Harvard, extending the funding-pressure template first applied to Columbia, while USCIS said it would begin monitoring immigrants' social media for "antisemitic activity" — a policy with clear potential to target pro-Palestinian speech broadly. Pressure on the judiciary also intensified: Trump and Musk escalated attacks on judges who had ruled against the administration, including calls to impeach Judge James Boasberg, while House Republicans displayed "Wanted" posters of judges outside their offices and held a hearing on curbing judicial power. In a related development, a federal judge dismissed corruption charges against New York City Mayor Eric Adams with prejudice, finding the arrangement "smacks of a bargain" tied to his cooperation on immigration enforcement — a judicial finding that prosecutorial discretion had been traded for policy compliance. House Republicans also advanced the SAVE Act, requiring documentary proof of citizenship for voter registration, and a Trump appointee referred New York Attorney General Letitia James to the DOJ for criminal investigation, part of a broader pattern of federal law enforcement being turned against officials who had resisted administration directives.
Wednesday belonged almost entirely to one event. Trump signed an executive order imposing a 10 percent baseline tariff on all imports, with country-specific rates as high as 49 percent for major trading partners, justified by a declared national emergency over trade deficits. He called it "Liberation Day." The market response was immediate and historic: a two-day loss of $6.6 trillion in value, the largest in American history, as economists across the political spectrum called the underlying tariff formula incoherent. The scale of the shock briefly cracked party discipline — four Republican senators joined Democrats to pass a resolution rescinding tariffs specifically on Canadian goods, defying a direct public appeal from Trump to block the measure, though the House was expected to let it expire without a vote. The law firm settlement pattern continued as well, with Milbank LLP becoming the latest major firm to commit $100 million in pro bono work to avoid a threatened executive order, following a similar deal struck by Willkie Farr & Gallagher days earlier — coercion of the legal profession running in parallel with coercion of the university system.
By Thursday, the consequences of Wednesday's announcement were spreading internationally and domestically. China imposed 34 percent retaliatory tariffs, restricted exports of rare earth minerals critical to defense and technology manufacturing, and blacklisted several U.S. defense firms, while American markets extended their losses and economist Lawrence Summers publicly derided the tariff methodology. Health Secretary Kennedy acknowledged that roughly 20 percent of the prior week's HHS layoffs had been made in error and would be reversed, an admission that undercut the administration's stated justification for the cuts in the first place. The Pentagon's inspector general opened a formal investigation into Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth's use of the Signal messaging app to discuss classified military operations, after a journalist was inadvertently included in the chat. Two more lawsuits were filed against Trump's election executive order, bringing the total to three, and the Social Security Administration briefly canceled Maine's vital-records contracts in apparent retaliation against the state's governor before reversing course under public pressure — a small but revealing episode in the use of federal agency power as political punishment. North Carolina's General Assembly introduced a bill making it illegal to use standard voter registration forms in registration drives, extending the week's pattern of new restrictions on voter access to the state level.
The week closed on Friday with direct confrontation between the executive branch and a federal court. Judge Paula Xinis ordered the government to return Kilmar Abrego Garcia by April 7, finding his deportation "wholly lawless" and a "clear constitutional violation." Deputy Chief of Staff Stephen Miller responded by calling her a "Marxist judge," and the White House publicly disputed her authority over the case — turning what had begun as an admitted administrative error into an open dispute over whether a court order would be obeyed at all. In North Carolina, a divided Court of Appeals ruled that a Republican candidate who had lost a state supreme court race by 734 votes could proceed with a challenge seeking to invalidate more than 65,000 ballots, prompting an appeal from his opponent and raising the prospect of a court overturning an election outcome rather than a recount or a new vote. The U.S. Supreme Court, in a 5-4 emergency order, allowed the administration to freeze $65 million in teacher-training grants tied to diversity programs, overturning a lower court's restraining order and testing the boundaries of congressional spending authority. And as markets continued their historic decline, Trump held a $1 million-per-person fundraiser at Mar-a-Lago for his super PAC and skipped the dignified transfer ceremony for four service members killed in a training accident, attending a golf tournament at his resort instead. Former President Obama, in a public speech, urged universities, law firms, and citizens to resist executive coercion and defend academic freedom and due process even at financial cost — a coda that echoed Booker's speech from six days earlier.
Across the week, a recurring thread ran beneath the individual stories: officials speaking with confidence about things that were not true. A DOGE staffer made false claims about noncitizen Social Security numbers and voting; Kennedy falsely claimed vitamin A shipments had reached Texas measles communities; Vance misdescribed a wrongfully deported man as a convicted gang member; a member of Congress promoted a decades-old assassination conspiracy theory on national television. No single instance dominated the news, but together they formed a steady erosion of the assumption that government statements could be taken at face value.
Taken as a whole, the week showed a government testing the outer edges of several restraints at once — the two-term limit, the separation of trade authority between Congress and the presidency, the independence of universities and law firms from federal financial coercion, and the finality of judicial orders concerning wrongful deportation. Some of these tests met resistance: a bipartisan Senate vote, a federal judge's order, a former president's public appeal, a marathon speech meant to hold public attention. Others met little resistance at all: a university surrendered without a fight from its trustees, a market absorbed trillions in losses without altering the policy that caused them, and a deportation error remained uncorrected past the deadline a court had set. The week did not resolve any of these tensions. It simply made clear how many of them were now open at the same time.
```
