This Week in Democracy: A Slush Fund Blocked, a Senator Pepper-Sprayed, and a President's Face on the Money
As Iran diplomacy collapsed and inflation bit harder, federal agents turned chemical spray on a sitting senator, judges blocked a $1.8 billion insider payout, and the White House pushed to put Trump's portrait on U.S. currency.
```markdown
This week began with a foreign policy announcement that didn't survive the weekend. The administration floated a memorandum that would have released $25 billion in frozen Iranian assets and lifted the naval blockade, but the framework collapsed almost as soon as it was announced. Iran's state media disputed the terms outright. Prime Minister Netanyahu called Trump directly to demand full nuclear dismantlement instead. Senate Republicans, including Marco Rubio's predecessor Mike Pompeo along with Ted Cruz, Lindsey Graham, and Roger Wicker, publicly denounced the framework as a giveaway. By the next day, Trump was telling reporters the blockade remained in force after all — a reversal suggesting the announcement had been made without securing buy-in from allies or his own party.
That same weekend, Trump's ongoing effort to punish Republican dissenters claimed new casualties. Senator Bill Cassidy lost his Louisiana primary, a defeat tied directly to his 2021 vote to impeach Trump. Five Indiana state senators who had resisted redistricting demands lost their seats as well, and Trump publicly attacked Representative Brian Fitzpatrick over independent votes. Yet the purge carried an unintended consequence: freed from electoral pressure by his own defeat, Cassidy indicated he would now support a war powers resolution challenging Trump's Iran policy — a reminder that removing the incentive to survive can also remove the incentive to comply.
Elsewhere over the weekend, a federal judge dismissed a criminal indictment against Kilmar Ábrego García, finding that the administration would not have prosecuted him had he not challenged his unlawful deportation to a Salvadoran prison — an unambiguous finding of vindictive prosecution, in which the government used the criminal justice system to punish a man for exercising his legal right to contest his own deportation. Separately, an overheating tank of methyl methacrylate at a Garden Grove aerospace facility forced evacuation orders for roughly 50,000 residents near Disneyland, prompting Governor Newsom to declare a state of emergency while firefighters worked through the weekend to cool the tank.
It was also over the weekend that the week's central story began to take shape, though almost no one recognized it yet. At the Delaney Hall immigration detention facility in Newark, New Jersey, roughly 300 detainees began a coordinated hunger and work strike, alleging worm-infested food, inadequate medical care, and pressure to self-deport. It would not stay a local story for long.
On Monday, Memorial Day, the Delaney Hall situation escalated sharply. Federal agents deployed pepper spray against roughly 150 protesters gathered outside the facility — and against Senator Andy Kim, who was present. The Department of Homeland Security later denied the incident had occurred at all, characterizing the protesters as dangerous rioters, a claim that stood in direct contradiction to video evidence showing the senator sprayed alongside demonstrators. The same day, Trump posted an AI-generated image depicting Representative Ro Khanna as a devil, along with violent AI-generated imagery of bombers attacking Iran and images suggesting territorial designs on Greenland, capping the holiday with a message attacking Democrats as disrespecting the military. Representative Thomas Massie, freshly defeated in his primary, used a television appearance to promise he would continue naming individuals from the Epstein files in the months ahead.
By Tuesday, the week's throughlines began to multiply. A three-judge federal panel — including two Trump appointees — found that Alabama's congressional map intentionally discriminated against Black voters and ordered a map with two majority-Black districts reinstated; Alabama announced it would appeal to the Supreme Court. In Texas, Governor Abbott designated the Council on American-Islamic Relations a foreign terrorist organization under state law and urged sheriffs to investigate alleged "sharia law" enforcement, while both Senate candidates in the state's runoff, John Cornyn and Ken Paxton, deployed anti-Muslim messaging in their campaigns — Cornyn going so far as to introduce a "Boot Sharia Law Act." At Delaney Hall, Governor Mikie Sherrill was refused entry to the facility she was attempting to inspect; DHS dismissed her visit as a "political stunt" as the hunger strike entered its fifth day. In a case with clear implications for the treatment of political speech, the Third Circuit paused re-detention of Palestinian-American activist Mahmoud Khalil pending Supreme Court review. And amid public concern over his visible health, Trump underwent a third physical examination at Walter Reed, after which he did not appear publicly for more than a week.
Wednesday brought the sharpest escalation yet at Delaney Hall. Senator Kim was pepper-sprayed a second time while attempting to enter the facility alongside Governor Sherrill. That evening, ICE agents deployed pepper spray and tasers against protesters, chasing some onto train tracks and spraying at least seven journalists in the process. DHS Secretary Mullin denied the hunger strike was even occurring and accused lawmakers of "spreading smears" — a claim increasingly difficult to square with the accumulating documentation. The same day, in South Carolina, the state senate rejected a mid-decade redistricting proposal targeting Representative Jim Clyburn's district by a 26–18 vote, with fourteen Republicans joining Democrats despite direct phone calls from Trump urging passage — a rare instance of the president's pressure campaign failing outright. Away from the political battles, a tank holding 600,000 gallons of caustic liquor ruptured at a paper mill in Longview, Washington, killing at least two workers with nine more missing and presumed dead, contaminating the Columbia River and prompting a federal safety investigation into what could become the deadliest industrial accident in the state's modern history.
Wednesday also surfaced a cluster of reporting on executive self-dealing. ProPublica revealed that a White House adviser had pressured the Pentagon into approving a $620 million loan to a startup linked to Donald Trump Jr. Separate reporting showed Trump had purchased Dell stock shortly before the Pentagon awarded Dell a $9.7 billion contract. The administration was also found to be diverting national park entrance fees to fund White House beautification projects, including an inflated no-bid contract for work on the Reflecting Pool. Elsewhere, the Office of Personnel Management circulated a draft nondisclosure agreement that would bar federal employees from speaking to journalists, backed by civil and criminal penalties. Reporting also disclosed that at least ten men had died by suicide in ICE custody since the start of Trump's second term, seven of them in the current fiscal year alone — the highest total in the agency's history. Trump, meanwhile, falsely claimed that Somali Americans in Minnesota are "all crooks," while the Justice Department refiled a $10 billion defamation suit against the Wall Street Journal over its Epstein-related reporting and separately argued in court that it holds unlimited power to fire federal employees for political reasons.
Thursday extended the self-enrichment thread into new territory. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent announced a push for legislation to override an 1866 law banning the depiction of living persons on U.S. currency, with the Washington Post reporting that political appointees had pressured the Bureau of Engraving and Printing to design a $250 bill bearing Trump's likeness, reassigning the director who objected. DHS Secretary Mullin floated withdrawing customs agents from international airports in sanctuary cities to pressure local governments on immigration enforcement, a proposal the travel industry pushed back against forcefully. The Justice Department opened a criminal investigation into E. Jean Carroll, the writer who successfully sued Trump for sexual assault and defamation, examining whether she had committed perjury regarding funding for her lawsuits — though the Acting Attorney General recused himself given his prior work representing Trump. Economic data released the same day showed inflation had reached a three-year high, gross domestic product for the first quarter had been revised down, and the personal savings rate had plunged to its lowest level since 2022; Bessent characterized the collapsing savings rate as evidence of consumer confidence. Also on Thursday, a $106 million federal prison contract was awarded to a company led by a pardoned Trump fundraiser with a history of illegal lobbying, and the Commodity Futures Trading Commission gutted its cryptocurrency enforcement staff while retaliating against officials who had raised conflict-of-interest concerns involving firms tied to the president's family. At Delaney Hall, ICE deployed chemical spray and pepper balls against demonstrators again, striking some with batons and pushing others into traffic, and arrested six protesters after the hunger-strike leader was transferred to another facility.
The week closed with courts asserting themselves forcefully. A federal judge issued a temporary restraining order blocking the creation or disbursement of Trump's $1.8 billion "Anti-Weaponization Fund," after thirty-five former federal judges petitioned to reopen the underlying lawsuit, alleging the fund amounted to a collusive pretext and a fraud on the court; a hearing was set for June 12. Separately, another judge ruled that only Congress has the authority to rename the Kennedy Center, voiding its rebranding under Trump's name and blocking a board-approved closure. The same day, Trump signed an executive order directing the CDC to nearly halve the recommended childhood vaccine schedule, based on an assessment from Health and Human Services Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr.; fifteen Democratic-led states sued within hours, arguing the change lacked scientific basis. Former Attorney General Pam Bondi appeared before the House Oversight Committee for a downgraded, unsworn interview on the Epstein files, acknowledging redaction errors while refusing to answer questions about Trump's involvement, as a Justice Department attorney repeatedly intervened to block such questions. At Delaney Hall, New Jersey announced that state police would assume public safety operations outside the facility and establish a protected protest zone, even as a fresh wave of federal agents arrived to investigate demonstrators and the Justice Department filed charges against a protester accused of assaulting an ICE officer. The Office of Management and Budget, under Director Russ Vought, proposed placing federal research grants under political appointee control rather than scientific peer review — a change estimated to potentially discontinue nearly half of NIH clinical trials, including more than a thousand cancer trials. The Justice Department also opened an inquiry into a nonprofit backed by Reid Hoffman that had helped fund Carroll's lawsuits, extending scrutiny of the president's critics into their financial networks. And in a scattering of related cases, a federal jury convicted three ICE protesters, including an Army veteran, on felony conspiracy charges; the Justice Department sued four additional states seeking unredacted voter data; a judge dismissed protest-related charges in Chicago over prosecutorial misconduct; and Minneapolis prosecutors charged a second ICE agent with assault.
Taken as a whole, the week recorded a government pulling in two directions at once. Federal power was used to disperse protesters and a sitting senator with chemical agents, to prosecute a plaintiff who had already won her case in court, to channel public money toward the president's family and allies, and to press for the president's image on the nation's currency. At the same time, courts intervened repeatedly and with unusual force — freezing a $1.8 billion fund, restoring a congressional map found to discriminate against Black voters, protecting a federally chartered cultural institution from unilateral rebranding, and pausing the re-detention of a political activist. Even within the president's own party, pressure did not always produce compliance: South Carolina Republicans rejected a redistricting map despite direct calls from the president, even as a Louisiana senator lost his seat for defying him months earlier. The week did not resolve which of these forces would prevail. It left, instead, a record of an executive testing the limits of its authority on multiple fronts simultaneously, and of institutions — judicial, legislative, and civic — testing whether those limits would hold.
```
