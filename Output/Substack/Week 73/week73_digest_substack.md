# This Week in Democracy: War Powers on a Truth Social Feed

*A week of announced-then-cancelled airstrikes, a president expunging his own impeachments, and a Kennedy Center sign that came off only after midnight*

![Header image](week73-digest-featured.jpg)

```markdown
This was a week in which volatility at the top of the executive branch collided, again and again, with resistance built into the machinery beneath it. A president who could not sustain attention during an active attack on American forces spent the week lurching between threats of war and claims of peace, while federal judges, a divided Congress, and even a besieged nonprofit's own board became the last obstacles standing in his way. By Friday, the pattern was unmistakable: courts blocking, agencies defying, and money moving in directions Congress had explicitly forbidden.

The weekend set the tone. As Iran launched missile and drone strikes on U.S. facilities, Trump posted about golf course renovations at a military installation and shared AI-generated videos glorifying himself, then appeared to doze off during a signing ceremony. Days later, when NBC's Kristen Welker pressed him on his unsubstantiated claims that the 2020 election and a recent California election had been rigged, he called her "crooked or stupid," tore off his microphone, and walked out before flying to Bedminster. In between, Senator Andy Kim was denied access to detainees during an oversight visit to Delaney Hall in New Jersey, where he witnessed a detainee in apparent medical distress that staff refused to explain. A Justice Department attorney told the D.C. Circuit, in arguments over the administration's $400 million White House ballroom project, that courts have no authority to review executive demolition of federal property after the fact — unable to say whether the same reasoning would bar review of bulldozing the Statue of Liberty. A whistleblower separately revealed that Department of Government Efficiency officials had devised a scheme to falsely mark roughly 2.7 million people, including U.S. citizens, as dead in federal records to coerce self-deportation; about 6,100 people were actually entered into the Death Master File. At Normandy, Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth used a D-Day commemoration to describe European immigration as an "invasion," borrowing language associated with white nationalist ideology at a solemn Allied ceremony.

By Monday, courts began pushing back in earnest. Judge Leo Sorokin struck down Trump's $100,000 H-1B visa fee as an unlawful tax, ruling that only Congress holds the power to levy such charges. The same day, Judge Christopher Cooper ordered Trump's name stripped from the Kennedy Center, ruling that only Congress can rename a congressionally chartered institution, and temporarily blocked a related $257 million renovation. Elsewhere, border czar Tom Homan said he had reviewed plans to surge an unprecedented number of ICE agents into New York City, timed to coincide with major sporting events, in what read as retaliation for the state's new sanctuary law. Trump, in a separate NBC interview, declined to rule out using a nearly $1.8 billion fund to compensate people convicted of assaulting police during the January 6 attack — despite his own acting attorney general having previously called the fund dead. Reporting meanwhile deepened on the financial entanglements surrounding Trump's family: Jared Kushner's $2 billion in Saudi investment, a Kushner-Ivanka resort project in Albania drawing mass protest over environmental damage, and an $8 billion Penn Station plan raising fresh conflict-of-interest questions. In the Senate, Republicans blocked Democratic amendments that would have barred a presidential slush fund, prohibited William Pulte from holding two federal posts simultaneously, and restricted unapproved ballroom spending.

Tuesday introduced what would become the week's second major crisis. Trump announced that Pulte — the head of a housing finance agency with no intelligence background — would become acting Director of National Intelligence while retaining his existing post, and floated eliminating the DNI office altogether. The same day, after an Apache helicopter was reportedly shot down near the Strait of Hormuz, Trump ordered Central Command to strike Iranian radar and air-defense sites; Iran responded with strikes on U.S. bases in Kuwait, Bahrain, and Jordan. Vice President Vance said he would refer Minnesota's governor and attorney general for criminal investigation over unproven claims involving Somali immigrant fraud, drawing accusations of political retaliation against opposition officials. House Oversight Chair James Comer declined to subpoena Todd Blanche in connection with the Epstein matter even as the committee heard testimony from a longtime Epstein assistant. In Maine, Graham Platner won the Democratic Senate primary with 72 percent of the vote despite documented misconduct allegations against him, a result that raised its own questions about party accountability heading into a competitive general election.

Wednesday brought the two crises together. The House passed the $70 billion Secure America Act along strict party lines, 214–212, funding ICE and CBP through 2029 without any of the oversight reforms Democrats had sought following civilian deaths during enforcement operations. The Justice Department briefly established the $1.8 billion fund for Trump allies — including individuals convicted of assaulting police on January 6 — before abruptly cancelling it under bipartisan legislative pressure. At the same time, Trump doubled down on installing Pulte at DNI even as bipartisan opposition mounted, threatening the pending reauthorization of FISA Section 702 before it expired. ProPublica reported that Indian billionaire Mukesh Ambani had invested at least $100 million in a Texas startup secretly tied to Donald Trump Jr. around the same period his family secured tariff exemptions and sanctions relief from the administration. Trump also pressed Congress to fast-track a $350 billion military spending bill alongside a restrictive voting measure requiring proof of citizenship and curbing mail-in ballots, and, asked about rising prices, said he "loves" inflation even as markets fell and oil prices spiked following the renewed strikes on Iran.

Thursday carried the Iran volatility to its sharpest point yet. Trump announced imminent "very hard" strikes on Iran and floated seizing Kharg Island and Iran's oil markets outright — then, hours later, claimed to have cancelled the strikes after reaching a deal, an account Israeli officials publicly disputed. The whiplash was matched by legislative collapse: the House failed even a short-term FISA extension in a 198–218 vote amid Democratic protest over the Pulte nomination, allowing the surveillance authority to lapse, though existing court certifications kept some collection running. The Department of Homeland Security published a waiver suspending the National Environmental Policy Act, the Endangered Species Act, the Clean Water Act, and tribal grave protections to build 17 miles of border wall and roads through Big Bend National Park — an area accounting for less than half a percent of unauthorized crossings. About 100 FBI agents raided the offices and homes of board members connected to the Ohio Organizing Collaborative, a voter-registration nonprofit, citing a fraud justification despite the statistical rarity of such fraud. Separately, former and current FBI agents described a wave of departures — reportedly up to 2,800 since January 2025 — attributed to pressure to violate professional standards under Director Kash Patel. Federal prosecutors also unsealed charges against eight pro-Palestinian activists accused of an intimidation campaign tied to divestment demands at the University of Michigan.

Friday closed the week with the Kennedy Center saga reaching its resolution. After the D.C. Circuit denied Trump's emergency stay of the renaming order, the Kennedy Center's board amended its own bylaws to threaten bankruptcy if Trump's name were removed — a maneuver legal experts described as a violation of fiduciary duty. The name came down only after midnight. The same day, the administration diverted $352 million in Secret Service funds toward the ballroom project, despite earlier promises it would be privately financed and after Congress had explicitly refused to appropriate $1 billion for it. Judge Leonie Brinkema extended, then formalized, an injunction blocking the $1.8 billion allies' fund, ordering senior officials to certify compliance under penalty of perjury. Trump separately pressed Congress to pass a symbolic resolution nullifying his two impeachments, despite constitutional scholars noting no such mechanism exists in law; Speaker Johnson called it a priority for after the midterms. At Delaney Hall, nearly 40 women began a hunger and labor strike demanding the release of vulnerable detainees and improved conditions; ICE and its private contractor responded with new restrictions, police used tear gas and made arrests against supporting protesters outside the facility, and the Department of Homeland Security publicly denied the strike was happening at all. Reporting also revealed that Joint Chiefs Chairman Dan Caine had made a rushed secret trip to brief Trump on a plan for a ground invasion of Iran aimed at seizing enriched uranium — an operation Trump ultimately paused after warnings of severe retaliation. And in Minnesota, Vance Boelter pleaded guilty to murdering former House Speaker Melissa Hortman and her husband, and to wounding a state senator and his wife, in a politically motivated attack, receiving two consecutive life sentences.

Taken as a whole, the week showed a presidency operating at the edge of its own institutional restraints, and a system of checks straining, unevenly, to hold. War powers were exercised and reversed on a private social media timeline rather than through congressional debate. An unqualified political loyalist's path to the nation's top intelligence post proved so contentious that it took down a foundational surveillance law rather than reforming it. Courts intervened repeatedly and, in several instances, successfully — vacating an unlawful tax, enforcing a renaming order against an institution's own resistance, and blocking a fund that would have rewarded people convicted of assaulting police officers. But other checks bent rather than held: Congress passed enforcement funding without conditions even as detainees at a New Jersey facility went on hunger strike and were met with denial and force, and money continued to move — from Secret Service accounts to a ballroom, from foreign investors to family businesses — in patterns that outlasted any single day's headlines. The week's throughline was less about any one crisis than about how many simultaneous ones the country's institutions were being asked to absorb at once.
```
