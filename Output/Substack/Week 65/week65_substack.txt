Week 65 opened with a blockade order and closed with an unverified victory lap. In between, the machinery of the state kept doing what it had done for months: bending toward the man at its center, testing how far law, memory, and oversight would stretch before something broke. Nothing this week arrived as rupture; it arrived as confirmation. The Democracy Clock had been drifting for a long time. This week it barely moved at all, and that stillness is itself part of the story.

At the close of the previous period, the clock stood at 7:43 p.m. It closed this week at the same public time, a net shift of roughly two-tenths of a minute. The number understates the churn beneath it. Executive overreach on war powers, retaliation against whistleblowers, and continued use of federal funds as a weapon pushed the clock backward. A disbarment, a construction injunction, two ethics-driven resignations, and a bipartisan immigration bill pulled just enough the other way to hold the line. The week did not deepen the crisis. It confirmed that the crisis has become the operating condition.

The gravest single development was the president's blockade of the Strait of Hormuz. He ordered it alone, threatened to destroy Iranian infrastructure in retaliation for future attacks, and never asked Congress for authorization. The House tried to answer: a War Powers Resolution failed by a single vote, 214 to 213. Bernie Sanders led a parallel Senate effort to halt arms sales to Israel; it also failed. The margins were thin enough to suggest real resistance, and too thin to constrain anything. Meanwhile Rep. Mike Turner, a senior member of the Armed Services and Oversight committees, declined to press for accountability at all, deferring instead to the president's own social media posts as if they were briefings.

The blockade itself proved oddly hollow. International shippers appear to have mostly ignored it; a sanctioned Chinese tanker transited without incident. By week's end, Trump was declaring the war over and claiming sweeping Iranian concessions, posts that Iranian officials and independent reporting directly contradicted. Iranian state media and aligned accounts mocked the episode online. House Democrats filed six articles of impeachment against Defense Secretary Hegseth over the war's conduct. The pattern is now familiar: announce a dramatic use of force, watch it fail to change facts on the ground, and substitute a declaration of victory for an actual resolution.

The costs of that pattern showed up quickly in the economy. Inflation rose to 3.3 percent and wholesale prices to 4 percent, driven by war-linked fuel costs. Gas prices climbed to four and five dollars a gallon in parts of the country, straining disabled, elderly, and low-income Americans who depend on affordable transportation for medical care and work. Farmers reported they could no longer afford fertilizer amid tariff pressure layered atop war-driven costs. Kevin Hassett, a senior economic adviser, described rising oil-company profits as a kind of upside to the pain at the pump. The remark captured something structural: policy made at the top was generating hardship spread unevenly at the bottom, and officials seemed untroubled by the arithmetic.

A second major thread ran through the week's treatment of internal watchdogs. The Department of Justice fired six immigration judges, including two who had ruled against the administration in high-profile deportation cases. DNI Tulsi Gabbard issued criminal referrals against the 2019 Ukraine whistleblower whose complaint led to Trump's first impeachment, and against Michael Atkinson, the inspector general who lawfully forwarded that complaint years ago. The DOJ simultaneously moved to dismiss seditious conspiracy convictions against Proud Boys and Oath Keepers members from January 6th, erasing legal accountability for an organized attack on the Capitol. Taken together, these were not isolated personnel actions. They formed a single pattern: officials who exercised lawful oversight functions, however long ago, are being punished, while those convicted of conspiring to overturn an election are being unwound.

Against this, a few courts held their ground. Kentucky's Supreme Court and West Virginia judges rebuffed legislative attempts to impeach judges over disfavored rulings. That resistance mattered, because the same pattern of intimidation was spreading elsewhere. Legislators in North Carolina, Pennsylvania, Tennessee, and Utah threatened or attempted similar impeachments against judges who had ruled on redistricting and voting access. North Carolina's elections board voted to send voter data to DHS for citizenship cross-checking against a database known to be error-prone. ProPublica reported that roughly two dozen election deniers have now been installed in election-related positions nationwide, while some seventy-five officials who resisted the 2020 overturn effort have been removed. This is not a single scandal. It is the quiet reconstruction of election administration itself, brick by brick, in favor of people who tried to overturn the last one.

Congress did not sit entirely still. Two members left under pressure within forty-eight hours of each other. Rep. Eric Swalwell resigned amid sexual assault allegations, and Rep. Tony Gonzales resigned amid an expulsion threat tied to an affair with a staffer who later died by suicide. Both departures came through bipartisan pressure, evidence that self-policing has not entirely disappeared even in a weakened institution. Separately, fifty House Democrats led by Jamie Raskin introduced legislation to create a 25th Amendment commission on presidential fitness. It was an extraordinary step, reflecting genuine alarm, reported elsewhere this week, from retired generals and diplomats about the president's conduct.

That alarm found strange expression in a run of religious imagery. Trump posted, then deleted, an AI-generated image depicting himself as Jesus Christ, later offering an implausible explanation that it showed him as a doctor. He also posted an image of himself as Pope, alongside a fabricated Trump Tower on the moon. At the same time he attacked the actual Pope, Leo XIV, for criticizing the war, calling him weak and refusing to apologize even after allied leaders called the remarks unacceptable. Defense Secretary Hegseth compared journalists to Pharisees while restricting Pentagon briefings to conservative outlets. None of this was accidental. Religious language and imagery were being used, in parallel by two officials, to frame political and military authority as something closer to sacred than contestable.

Immigration enforcement kept expanding past its stated boundaries. ICE detained the wife of an active-duty Army sergeant despite her legal protections. Local police conducted unauthorized "wellness checks" at Cincinnati schools, acting as informal immigration agents without warrants; officers were eventually placed on leave. At a Florida detention facility nicknamed "Alligator Alcatraz," guards cut off detainees' phone and legal access and used force against them, in open defiance of a standing court order. There were partial checks: a Hennepin County prosecutor charged an ICE agent with felony assault after video contradicted the agency's account of a shooting, and the acting ICE director announced his departure amid mounting criticism. These were real, if narrow, points of accountability inside a much larger pattern of expanding impunity.

Civil society did not go quiet. A multi-week, one-hundred-sixty-mile march from Philadelphia to Washington began this week. Nearly a hundred people were arrested in a sit-in demanding limits on arms sales to Israel. Activists disrupted a congressional hearing to protest cuts to a global health program stripped of its appropriated PEPFAR funds. Nationwide Tax Day protests connected military spending to immigration enforcement, and a coordinated day of action defended universities against political pressure. Against this mobilization, the Justice Department applied a FACE Act religious-freedom provision for the first time to charge anti-ICE protesters and a journalist, even as it abandoned the statute's original reproductive-rights purpose. Dissent persisted. So did the state's habit of recasting it as disorder.

The week's clearest institutional counterweight came from the courts. California's Supreme Court upheld the disbarment of John Eastman, the attorney who helped engineer the fake-elector scheme to overturn the 2020 election. It stands as one of the few durable consequences imposed on any architect of that effort. A federal judge separately halted above-ground construction of a White House ballroom project, ruling that national-security justifications did not exempt it from ordinary legal review; Trump responded by attacking the judge publicly. Justice Ketanji Brown Jackson criticized the Supreme Court's own use of its emergency docket to favor the administration, an unusual public rebuke from inside the institution itself.

Congressional oversight of executive spending also showed friction. OMB Director Russell Vought denied, under Senate questioning, that the administration had illegally withheld congressionally appropriated PEPFAR and global health funds, despite documentation suggesting otherwise. House Democrats formed a task force to investigate Trump family self-dealing and opened a separate Judiciary Committee inquiry into Jared Kushner's financial conflicts, including his dual role during Iran negotiations. New legislation aimed at closing a loophole allowing conversion of public funds through litigation settlements was introduced the same week. None of these efforts produced immediate results, but they show that the instruments of oversight, however slow, have not been fully disabled.

Federal power was again turned against jurisdictions the administration disfavors. Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy withheld $73.5 million in funding from New York over a dispute involving driver's licenses for immigrants, extending a pattern first seen in Minnesota into a new state. An $11 million contract with Catholic Charities serving migrant children was canceled shortly after religious leaders criticized war policy, suggesting funding decisions doubling as retaliation against critics. These actions are small in dollar terms and large in signal. Federal money is treated as leverage against disagreement, not as a shared public trust.

One genuine bright spot arrived from outside the country's borders. Hungary's opposition won a decisive electoral victory over Viktor Orbán, despite active campaigning on his behalf by figures within the Trump administration. It stood as a reminder that electoral accountability can still function, even in a system that had spent years consolidating executive control. The contrast with the domestic picture was not subtle.

Congress passed bipartisan legislation extending Temporary Protected Status to roughly 350,000 Haitian residents, with eleven Republicans crossing over to support it. It now awaits Senate action. Coming in the same week as the failed war-powers vote, it showed that bipartisan cooperation remains possible on some questions, even as it remains impossible on others, particularly those touching directly on the president's power to make war.

Taken as a whole, Week 65 does not read as an acceleration. It reads as consolidation. The administration's use of war powers, retaliation against oversight officials, and pressure on federal funding recipients were not new inventions. They were the continued, confident use of tools already deployed in prior weeks. The counterweights that emerged — a disbarment, a construction injunction, two resignations, a bipartisan immigration bill, new investigations into self-dealing — were real but modest. They held the clock nearly level rather than reversing its direction.

The steadiness of the number should not be mistaken for calm. A republic that holds its ground only through narrow votes, individual judges, and scattered acts of civic resistance is not stable. It is strained in a way that has become routine, absorbing blow after blow without either collapsing or recovering. Week 65 offers no dramatic turn, no single moment historians will circle in red. It offers instead a portrait of endurance under sustained pressure, the kind that either builds toward eventual repair or wears down until repair becomes impossible. The record does not yet say which.
