# This Week in Democracy: Courts Push Back as the Administration Targets Critics

*A congresswoman's home is raided, a press ban is overturned twice, and the president floats pardoning himself — all in one week*

![Header image](week91-digest-featured.jpg)

```markdown
This was a week in which courts tried repeatedly to restrain an administration that kept testing how far it could go, while federal power was turned with new intensity on political opponents and the war in Ukraine slipped further from steady American support. By week's end, two congresswomen faced federal scrutiny, a presidential golf project had lost its trees over a judge's objection, a newly appointed press secretary's credentials had collapsed under scrutiny, and the president himself had raised the prospect of pardoning everyone in his orbit, including himself.

**The Weekend**

The tone was set early. Trump asked the Supreme Court to revive a racketeering suit he had filed as a private citizen against Hillary Clinton, Marc Elias, and others, seeking to erase nearly $1 million in sanctions — an unusual request from a sitting president to undo the legal consequences of his own lawyers' conduct in a personal grievance lawsuit. Quietly, on a Friday evening, the Pentagon revised its casualty figures from the Iran war upward, acknowledging more deaths and wounded than previously stated, months after Defense Secretary Hegseth had dismissed reports of undercounting as false. In a move whose consequences would unfold over the following days, Trump named Katie Zacharia — a former Truth Social communications adviser who had promoted the Pizzagate conspiracy theory — as his new White House press secretary.

Abroad, Trump announced what he called an "energy ceasefire" between Russia and Ukraine and said he had struck a deal with Vladimir Putin to temporarily lift sanctions on Russian diesel imports. Both announcements were made unilaterally, without Ukrainian confirmation, unsettling Kyiv. Separately, Elon Musk amplified an unfounded claim that Texas Senate candidate James Talarico had been hospitalized for mental health treatment — a falsehood that would spread through the week.

**Monday**

By Monday, the administration's friction with the courts had become overt. Despite a federal judge's order to halt DHS-funded political advertisements found likely to violate appropriations law, the ads kept running the very next day. At East Potomac Golf Links, where Trump has pushed for redevelopment, crews felled more than 400 trees in defiance of a judge's explicit prior warning against irreversible changes; an emergency hearing followed in which the judge said the government had "lost all credibility" and ordered officials to testify and preserve records.

That same day, federal agents conducted a predawn raid on the home of Representative Ilhan Omar, citing a fraud investigation into immigration paperwork her father had filled out in the 1990s, when she was a minor — timing and substance that departed sharply from Justice Department norms against pre-election investigative action targeting sitting lawmakers. In Houston, an ICE vehicle struck a bystander, Miguel Parra-Hernandez, breaking his leg; he remained in ICE custody while local prosecutors weighed charges against the agent, and ICE declined to cooperate with the investigation.

The Justice Department also reversed its own prior legal position, now arguing that states may purge noncitizens from voter rolls during the 90-day pre-election "quiet period" meant to prevent last-minute disenfranchisement — a shift that came as the Supreme Court agreed to hear a related case. At a rally, Trump disparaged Somali Americans in Minnesota, prompting chants for deportation, and mocked a female protester being punched by his own supporters as a "paid protester." Meanwhile, the Talarico disinformation campaign widened, with MAGA influencers and Musk continuing to spread the false hospitalization claim as his opponent, Ken Paxton, seeded further public doubt.

**Tuesday**

Tuesday brought the week's heaviest run of judicial rebukes. Judge Timothy Kelly ruled that Trump's ban of more than 70 journalists from CNN, MSNBC, and Politico from the White House press room was unconstitutional, violating free speech and due process protections, and denied the administration's request to stay the ruling; the Justice Department said it would appeal. Judge Ana Reyes issued a temporary restraining order halting further tree removal at the golf course, finding the administration's environmental review likely unlawful and expressing skepticism that the cutting was anything but preparation for Trump's own development interest.

The Omar investigation escalated the same day: agents seized her phone, computers, and passport, with officials explicitly describing the inquiry's goal as stripping her citizenship. At the same time, the Justice Department pursued a separate criminal case against Representative LaMonica McIver over a confrontation in which no one was injured — placing two Democratic congresswomen of color under simultaneous federal legal pressure. Separately, prosecutors sought a grand jury indictment of Cassidy Hutchinson, the former Trump aide whose testimony to Congress had detailed his conduct around January 6, on perjury charges.

Two more rulings added to the day's pattern: a judge found that sanctions against human rights groups assisting the ICC's Gaza investigation likely violated the First Amendment, and another blocked USDA office closures found to likely violate appropriations law. Amid all this, Zacharia held her first press briefing — fourteen minutes long, with only three hand-picked questions, marked by factual errors and evasive answers — as questions mounted over whether she had ever been licensed to practice law despite repeatedly claiming to be an attorney.

**Wednesday**

Wednesday deepened two of the week's central threads. Judge Kelly formally ordered the White House to restore press credentials to CNN, MS Now, and Politico. Almost immediately after major networks publicly condemned the ban, the Justice Department announced an antitrust investigation into five television networks — a sequence that invited suspicion the probe was retaliatory. Judge Rochon, meanwhile, extended the restraining order on taxpayer-funded political ads another fourteen days and demanded the administration explain why the ads had kept running despite representations to the court that they would stop.

Overseas, the consequences of the weekend's unilateral Ukraine moves became concrete: Ukraine received no advance intelligence warning of an overnight Russian strike, and reporting indicated that Starlink and Starshield communications used by its military had been throttled by SpaceX during a major barrage — raising the question of whether the administration was following through on earlier threats to curb support for Kyiv.

**Thursday**

Thursday's dominant story moved to the ballot box itself. The Justice Department sued California, seeking to block certification of ballots based on an unverified list of more than 20,000 alleged noncitizen voters, after state officials refused to act without a clear methodology — a legal effort to halt vote-counting days before the midterms. In a Newsmax interview the same day, Trump said he would consider pardoning everyone in his administration, including himself, to spare them years of legal proceedings.

Smaller threads also resolved. Rhein Amacher, the source of the Talarico health conspiracy, retracted his claim once he faced potential HIPAA liability, though he denied fabricating it outright. And a civil lawsuit filed by two women alleged that former congressman and Trump attorney general nominee Matt Gaetz had coordinated payments and travel for commercial sex, drawing on findings from a 2024 House Ethics report.

**Friday**

The week closed with revelations that deepened concerns about transparency inside the administration. The Washington Post reported that a nonprofit intermediary had been used to obscure the sources of $330 million in donations for a White House ballroom renovation, with most of the money traced to companies and individuals with pending federal interests, including Nvidia, Apple, and Lockheed Martin. The White House separately asserted that all conversations about renovating the Kennedy Center were protected by executive privilege — an unusually expansive claim extending well beyond the doctrine's traditional bounds.

The Wall Street Journal confirmed that Zacharia had failed the California bar exam and held no law license anywhere, prompting a nonpartisan watchdog to request an investigation into whether she had practiced law without a license — closing out a credibility story that had shadowed her from the moment of her appointment. On Ukraine, Trump denied any knowledge of the Starlink restrictions affecting Kyiv's military and, in a separate interview, called President Zelenskyy "more difficult to deal with than Putin." And the ACLU filed a motion alleging systemic abuse, medical neglect, and deaths at Camp East Montana, the nation's largest immigration detention facility.

**In Sum**

Taken as a whole, the week showed two institutions moving in opposite directions under strain. Federal courts issued ruling after ruling against the administration — on political advertising, environmental process, press access, and sanctions — each grounded in specific statutory or constitutional findings, from appropriations law to the First Amendment. The administration's response was not uniform: some rulings it appealed through ordinary channels, others it simply ignored in the interim, continuing conduct that judges had already found unlawful.

Layered atop that friction was the use of federal investigative power against two sitting Democratic congresswomen and a congressional witness, a credibility crisis inside the administration's own communications operation, and a widening gap between stated and actual U.S. support for an ally at war. None of these threads resolved by week's end — courts and agencies, Congress and the executive, remained locked in the same contest over what government may do, who it may do it to, and who gets to find out.
```
