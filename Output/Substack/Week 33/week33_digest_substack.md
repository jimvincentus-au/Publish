# This Week in Democracy: War Department, Weaponized Health Agencies, and a Nationwide Fight Over Troops

*A week of renamed institutions, purged public health leadership, expanding immigration enforcement, and a legal system straining to hold the line*

![Header image](week33-digest-featured.jpg)

```markdown
This week unfolded as a contest between an executive branch testing the outer limits of its authority and a set of institutions — courts, career officials, local governments, and even members of the president's own party — trying to hold a line. By week's end, the Pentagon had a new name, the CDC had a new director with no medical background, and federal troops were once again the subject of a standoff with a major American city. But alongside those moves came a string of court rulings that curbed executive power on trade, agency independence, university funding, and the treatment of migrant children. Neither trend fully prevailed; the week is best understood as a record of pressure applied and resistance offered, with outcomes still very much unsettled.

The weekend opened with turmoil at the Centers for Disease Control. The administration fired Director Susan Monarez after she resisted vaccine policy changes pushed by Health and Human Services Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr. Four more senior CDC officials resigned in protest, and a biotech investor without medical credentials was installed as acting director. It was an unusually blunt demonstration of political control displacing scientific leadership at the nation's premier public health agency, and it set the tone for a week in which loyalty tests recurred across multiple institutions.

Other developments followed in quick succession. The Office of Management and Budget, under Russell Vought, unilaterally canceled $4.9 billion in foreign aid that Congress had already approved, asserting executive control over spending the Constitution assigns to the legislature. A federal appeals court, by a 7-4 vote, ruled that Trump's sweeping tariffs exceeded his authority under emergency powers law, though it delayed enforcement of its own ruling until mid-October to allow a Supreme Court appeal. And Trump signaled he would sign an executive order mandating voter ID nationally and restricting mail voting — a plan resting on uncertain legal footing, since election administration is constitutionally reserved to the states. On a related but distinct track, Trump continued pressing Republican legislators in Texas, Indiana, and Missouri to redraw congressional maps ahead of the 2026 midterms, a campaign aimed at reshaping representation rather than voting procedure.

The weekend also brought quieter but consequential stories: renewable energy funding pulled from offshore wind projects in New Jersey, Connecticut, and Rhode Island, prompting governors to intervene; a Social Security Administration whistleblower resigning after alleging DOGE staff had uploaded sensitive personal data to an insecure server; ICE regaining access to phone-hacking spyware; and Director of National Intelligence Tulsi Gabbard publicly disclosing the identity of an undercover CIA officer while stripping her clearance without consulting the agency. In Chicago, Mayor Brandon Johnson signed an order barring city police from assisting federal immigration enforcement, anticipating a federal deployment that would become one of the week's defining conflicts.

That conflict escalated on Monday, Labor Day, when the administration intensified threats to send federal law enforcement and National Guard troops into Chicago as part of an immigration crackdown. Johnson used a Labor Day rally to denounce the plan, and Governor Pritzker joined the pushback. The same day, Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth approved deploying up to 600 military lawyers as temporary immigration judges within the Justice Department, blurring the line between civilian and military judicial functions. The White House also canceled union contracts covering roughly 450,000 federal employees, including Voice of America staff, prompting protests and a legal challenge from AFSCME. Meanwhile, a bipartisan effort led by Representatives Thomas Massie and Ro Khanna advanced a discharge petition to force disclosure of Epstein-related files — a thread that would run through the rest of the week. Senators Mike Lee and Greg Steube introduced a bill to formally rename the Pentagon the "Department of War," a preview of the executive action to come. And in Utah, a judge invalidated the state's congressional map as an unconstitutional dilution of Democratic voting power, ordering a new one by September 24.

By Tuesday, the courts had produced a mixed record. The Fifth Circuit blocked the administration from deporting Venezuelans under the Alien Enemies Act, ruling 2-1 that no actual "invasion" existed to justify invoking wartime powers — a rebuke of the administration's attempt to stretch a rarely used statute into routine immigration enforcement. The same day, DHS launched a $170 billion recruitment drive to hire 10,000 new ICE officers and 3,000 Border Patrol agents by year's end, lowering age requirements and offering signing bonuses, raising concerns about diminished screening and accountability. Attorney General Pam Bondi issued rulings narrowing asylum eligibility for gang-targeted families and domestic violence victims. Columbia University disciplined more than 100 students, including arrests, suspensions, and expulsions, following a pro-Palestine teach-in. An appeals court reinstated a Biden-appointed FTC commissioner Trump had tried to remove, defending regulatory independence, even as another appellate panel allowed the "Alligator Alcatraz" detention facility in Florida to keep operating pending appeal — a reminder that judicial pushback and judicial deference were occurring simultaneously, often on the same day. A Spokane Army veteran with no criminal record was arrested on federal conspiracy charges tied to an anti-ICE protest in June, and USCIS barred nongovernmental groups from registering new citizens to vote at naturalization ceremonies.

Wednesday was the week's most crowded day. A federal judge issued an emergency order stopping the deportation of up to 600 unaccompanied Guatemalan children — some already on planes — citing trafficking-protection law; all were returned to custody within a day, a stark example of judicial intervention arriving just in time. Washington, DC's attorney general sued the administration over its deployment of more than 2,200 National Guard troops in the city, arguing the action violated the Home Rule Act and constitutional limits on domestic military use — a fight distinct from, though thematically related to, the Chicago standoff. House Republicans voted to create a new subcommittee to reinvestigate the January 6 attack, led by Barry Loudermilk, drawing Democratic accusations that the effort was designed to rewrite the historical record; the vote came a day after Republicans blocked a plaque honoring officers who defended the Capitol. The Justice Department sued Boston and Mayor Michelle Wu over sanctuary policies. And the Epstein files fight took a sharper turn: a Justice Department official was secretly recorded suggesting that redactions in the files were being handled according to political affiliation, and reports emerged that Ghislaine Maxwell might be moved to a lower-security prison. The House Oversight Committee released roughly 33,000 pages of Epstein-related documents the same day, though most had already been public — a limited disclosure that did little to quiet the underlying tension. Elsewhere, a negotiated settlement preserved provisional ballot access for about 98,000 North Carolina voters facing ID disputes, the administration formally appealed both the tariff and FTC rulings to the Supreme Court, and the Justice Department signaled it might still deport Kilmar Abrego Garcia even if his asylum case succeeded.

Thursday brought some of the week's starkest moments. Trump reiterated that he believes he needs authoritarian-style, unchecked power to restore economic prosperity — an explicit preference for concentrated executive authority over ordinary constitutional constraint. The Justice Department opened a criminal investigation into Federal Reserve Governor Lisa Cook over alleged mortgage fraud, following a referral from the Federal Housing Finance Agency, raising questions about pressure on an institution designed to be insulated from the White House. FBI agents searched the home of former national security adviser John Bolton as part of a classified-documents investigation, seizing electronics and files without filing charges. Taken together, the Cook investigation and the Bolton raid illustrated a pattern of federal investigative power being turned toward officials seen as adversarial to the administration. Yet the same day, a federal judge ruled that the administration had unlawfully and retaliatorily terminated $2.6 billion in Harvard's research grants after the university refused to comply with White House demands, ordering the funding restored — one of the week's clearest judicial defenses of institutional independence. Northwestern University's president resigned amid a similar federal funding freeze. Representative LaMonica McIver was indicted over actions taken during an oversight visit to an ICE facility, a move that drew concern about criminalizing a lawmaker's oversight function. Trump also warned fellow Republicans that pushing for Epstein file disclosure would be seen as an act of hostility toward his administration — pressure aimed squarely at his own party. New CBO data showed his tax law offered little benefit to lower earners while cutting Medicaid and food assistance, alongside polling showing most Americans no longer believe hard work leads to advancement.

The week closed on Friday with its most symbolically loaded act: Trump signed an executive order restoring the pre-1947 name "Department of War" for the Pentagon, redirecting official communications and the department's website without seeking congressional approval for what remains, legally, an informal rebrand. The same day, Kennedy canceled $500 million in mRNA vaccine research funding, extending the week's earlier upheaval at the CDC into a direct cut to future public health capacity. Trump reaffirmed his intention to send federal law enforcement and National Guard troops into Chicago, citing crime and homelessness, over the continued objections of Johnson and Pritzker — a dispute that, by week's end, showed no sign of resolution. A Justice Department statement appeared to confirm earlier leaked comments about a possible arrangement affecting Ghislaine Maxwell's prison conditions, deepening unanswered questions about how the case was being handled. Trump formally petitioned the Supreme Court to overturn both the tariff ruling and the Fifth Circuit's block on Alien Enemies Act deportations, setting up major tests of executive authority in the term ahead. Smaller stories rounded out the week: a lawsuit challenging federal firearms registration requirements, continued judicial engagement with an active voting rights case, and Eric Adams's decision to remain in New York's mayoral race despite reported pressure from Trump to withdraw in favor of Andrew Cuomo.

Read as a whole, the week does not resolve into a single verdict. It records an administration moving simultaneously on several fronts — public health leadership, military nomenclature, domestic troop deployment, immigration enforcement, and investigations of perceived critics — while courts, in parallel, blocked deportations, restored university funding, protected agency independence, and limited the reach of wartime statutes. Congress, too, pulled in different directions at once, opening a new January 6 inquiry while facing bipartisan pressure to release Epstein-related files. What the week demonstrates most clearly is not the triumph of one side, but the ongoing test of whether institutional checks — judicial, congressional, and civic — can keep pace with the scope of executive action. That contest, unresolved, carried directly into the next week.
```
