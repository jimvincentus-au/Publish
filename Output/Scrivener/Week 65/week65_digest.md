Blockade, Blasphemy, and a Broken Congress
A week in which a naval blockade collapsed into a claimed peace, two lawmakers resigned in a single day, and the president's social media posts about Jesus overshadowed a war costing billions
```markdown
The week began, fittingly, with a joke that wasn't quite a joke. Trump told associates he would pardon any aide or official who came within 200 feet of the Oval Office once his term ended, a remark the White House waved off as humor while insisting his clemency power was "absolute." It landed against a backdrop of more than 1,800 pardons and commutations already issued, many for January 6 defendants, and set the tone for a week in which loyalty and legal exposure would repeatedly intersect. That same weekend, the administration fired immigration judges who had ruled against deportations, including some who had blocked the removal of pro-Palestinian university students, targeting jurists with immigrant-defense backgrounds and higher asylum grant rates. In Minneapolis, surveillance footage undercut an ICE account of a January shooting, revealing a 12-second encounter where officers had described a three-minute struggle; the agents involved were placed on leave. And in California, a widening crisis engulfed Rep. Eric Swalwell, the frontrunner in the state's governor's race, as multiple women accused him of sexual assault and misconduct, prompting a Manhattan DA investigation and public calls from Gavin Newsom, Hakeem Jeffries, and Adam Schiff for him to leave the race. Abroad, nuclear talks with Iran collapsed in Islamabad amid disputes over the Strait of Hormuz, frozen assets, and reparations, with Trump posting mocking content about Iran even as negotiations were still live and both governments offered irreconcilable accounts of a naval incident. Away from all of it, a civic march of more than a thousand people set out from Philadelphia toward Washington, a 160-mile echo of an earlier demonstration, framed by organizers as sustained resistance rather than a single protest.
By Monday the diplomatic collapse had become military policy. Trump ordered a full naval blockade of Iranian ports, following the breakdown of Vice President Vance's talks in Islamabad, threatening to destroy Iranian infrastructure and eliminate any approaching vessels; CENTCOM confirmed the blockade was operational. It was a unilateral use of force at one of the world's most sensitive chokepoints, ordered without congressional authorization. Domestically, the Swalwell story reached its first resolution: facing an imminent expulsion vote and the collapse of support from his own party's leadership, he resigned from Congress. Within hours, a fifth accuser came forward alleging drugging and rape, and both the Manhattan DA and the Department of Homeland Security opened investigations — the latter tied to a complaint from a known conspiracy theorist, raising its own questions about federal investigative machinery being turned toward political ends. Trump, meanwhile, opened a new front, publicly calling Pope Leo XIV "weak on crime" for criticizing the war, then posting an AI-generated image of himself with divine healing powers. He deleted it after backlash and falsely claimed it had depicted him as a doctor or Red Cross volunteer, declining to apologize. Separately, Netanyahu reportedly told his own cabinet that Vance had been briefing him daily on the confidential Islamabad talks, an arrangement that raised questions about how sensitive diplomatic information was being shared. And the week's first economic signal arrived: inflation hit 3.3 percent, more than triple February's rate, driven substantially by tariffs, with gasoline prices up over 21 percent.
Tuesday compounded the whiplash. Rep. Tony Gonzales, facing expulsion over an affair with a staffer who had later died by suicide, announced his own resignation — a second congressional departure within hours of Swalwell's. Two members of Congress leaving office in a single stretch reflected both the accumulation of institutional dysfunction and, in a narrower sense, the fact that expulsion mechanisms were actually working. The Swalwell scandal kept widening regardless: a fifth accuser, Lonna Drewes, came forward publicly in Beverly Hills with an account of being drugged and raped in 2018. Trump's feud with the Vatican escalated in parallel — he attacked the Pope in an Italian interview and separately called Italian Prime Minister Giorgia Meloni "unacceptable," reversing earlier praise after she defended the Pope. Elsewhere in the executive branch, Director of National Intelligence Tulsi Gabbard issued criminal referrals against the 2019 Ukraine-call whistleblower whose complaint had triggered Trump's first impeachment, and against the inspector general who had processed it — an apparent attempt to punish protected disclosures years after the fact. The Justice Department, meanwhile, moved to dismiss the seditious conspiracy convictions of Oath Keepers and Proud Boys leaders, extending a pattern that had already cleared more than 1,600 January 6 defendants through pardons and commutations. The administration also repealed the federal "endangerment finding" that underpins greenhouse gas regulation, celebrated at a climate-denial conference as a signature win, while a White House event featuring a gig worker delivering food to Trump was exposed as a staged promotional stunt for a "no tax on tips" policy. And former Attorney General Pam Bondi simply didn't show up for a scheduled House Oversight deposition on her handling of the Epstein files, with the Justice Department arguing that congressional subpoenas lapse once an official leaves office.
Wednesday brought the week's most direct institutional response to what had come before. Rep. Jamie Raskin and fifty co-sponsors introduced legislation to create a formal commission to assess Trump's fitness for office under the 25th Amendment, citing both his threats against Iran and his religious-imagery posts as evidence of erratic conduct — a rare formal invocation of a constitutional mechanism seldom used and almost never seriously proposed. The same day, OMB Director Russell Vought defended the administration's withholding of congressionally appropriated PEPFAR and global health funding, calling the choice ideologically motivated even after the Government Accountability Office had found the impoundment illegal; the funding freeze has been linked to an estimated 780,000 deaths in its first year. On the bench, Justice Ketanji Brown Jackson delivered a pointed public critique of her own court, describing the Supreme Court's use of emergency "shadow docket" orders favoring the administration as "scratch-paper musings" lacking real explanation — an unusual public airing of internal judicial strain. Reporting also surfaced a broader pattern of state legislatures moving against judges who had ruled on redistricting or voting access: an attempted impeachment in Kentucky was blocked by that state's supreme court, while similar retaliatory efforts were documented in West Virginia, North Carolina, Pennsylvania, Tennessee, and Utah. North Carolina's elections board voted along party lines to share voter registration data with DHS for citizenship cross-checks against a database known to be error-prone. And nationwide, Tax Day protests connected opposition to war spending, mass deportation, and private detention, while activists staged a sit-in at the offices of Senators Schumer and Gillibrand demanding limits on arms sales to Israel.
Thursday was the week's fullest day of institutional friction. House Democrats, led by Rep. Yassamin Ansari, filed six articles of impeachment against Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth over an unauthorized Iran strike, earlier boat strikes, and Signal-channel leaks. Raskin separately opened an investigation into Jared Kushner's financial ties to Saudi Arabia, and a new congressional task force was formed to examine Trump family self-dealing more broadly — three parallel accountability efforts converging in a single day. Congress also came close to reasserting its own war powers: a resolution to constrain military action against Iran failed in the House by a single vote, 213 to 214, while a Senate effort led by Bernie Sanders to block arms sales to Israel failed 40 to 59, though the margin reflected a growing bloc of Democratic dissent. Vought returned to testify before the Senate, this time denying that any funds had been illegally withheld — an assertion Senator Merkley accused him of fabricating — and separately refused to disclose the cost of the Iran war when asked directly. In the courts, Judge Richard Leon halted above-ground construction on Trump's planned White House ballroom project, ruling that national security concerns do not grant a blank check for otherwise unlawful activity; Trump responded with repeated public attacks calling him "Trump Hating." The feud with the Vatican deepened into open theological argument, as Vice President Vance and House Speaker Mike Johnson publicly challenged Pope Leo XIV's understanding of just war doctrine, while Defense Secretary Hegseth compared journalists to biblical Pharisees and restricted briefings to conservative outlets. Elsewhere, the administration withheld $73.5 million in federal funding from New York over a driver's license dispute, renewed threats to remove Federal Reserve Chair Jerome Powell, and canceled an $11 million Catholic Charities contract serving migrant children in what appeared to be retaliation for the Pope's criticism — three coercive funding actions against a state, an independent central banker, and a religious charity within a single day. Amid all of it, the House passed a bipartisan bill extending Temporary Protected Status for roughly 350,000 Haitians, 224 to 204, pushing back against the administration's termination effort.
The week closed on Friday with Trump declaring the Iran conflict over in a burst of thirteen posts claiming sweeping victory — that Iran had agreed to keep the Strait of Hormuz open, to surrender enriched uranium, and that Israel had been "prohibited" from bombing Lebanon. Iranian officials disputed the uranium claim specifically, and independent reporting indicated substantial disagreements remained unresolved. By week's end, the gap between the president's public account and the underlying facts was difficult to close. Elsewhere, accountability moved forward on narrower fronts: Minnesota prosecutors filed a felony assault charge against the ICE officer who had pointed a gun at highway occupants, the first criminal charge of its kind against a federal immigration agent, and acting ICE Director Todd Lyons announced his resignation amid mounting criticism of enforcement conduct. At a Florida detention facility known as "Alligator Alcatraz," attorneys reported that guards had cut off detainee phone access and used force in direct defiance of a judge's protective order. The Senate voted to reopen Minnesota's Boundary Waters wilderness to mining, clearing the way for a Chilean-owned subsidiary to pursue copper-sulfide extraction upstream from the protected area, reversing a two-decade moratorium. The House passed its Haitian TPS bill again, with eleven Republicans joining Democrats, as the Supreme Court prepared to hear related arguments later in the month. California's Supreme Court upheld the disbarment of John Eastman, the lawyer behind the fake-elector scheme to overturn the 2020 election, finding gross negligence and false statements without meaningful investigation. And on campuses nationwide, a coordinated Day of Action for Higher Education brought teach-ins and protests responding to federal funding threats against university independence.
Taken as a whole, the week measured the distance between what a government claims and what it can verify, and between institutions that still function and those bending under pressure. A blockade was ordered and then, in substance, abandoned, with victory declared before the other side agreed to the terms. Two sitting members of Congress left office in a single stretch under threat of expulsion, evidence that accountability mechanisms, however belatedly, still worked when misconduct became undeniable. Courts pushed back against a ballroom project and against retaliation targeting judges, even as the Justice Department worked to erase convictions tied to an attack on the Capitol and referred a protected whistleblower for prosecution. A sitting justice publicly criticized her own court's process. And a formal congressional effort to examine the president's fitness for office, alongside impeachment articles against his defense secretary, suggested that unease about the exercise of executive power had moved from commentary into legislative text. Whether these mechanisms could keep pace with the events generating them remained, at week's end, an open question.
```
