# The Comey Indictment Week: Prosecutors Replaced, Antifa Declared a Terrorist Enemy, and the UN Gets a 55-Minute Grievance Speech

*A week in which a president demanded prosecutions and got them, a comedian's suspension became a referendum on press freedom, and the machinery of law enforcement turned visibly toward political enemies*

![Header image](week36-digest-featured.jpg)

```markdown
The week of September 20 to 26 began with a president publicly demanding prosecutions of his political rivals and ended with those demands substantially fulfilled: an indicted former FBI director, and federal agencies newly authorized to investigate nonprofits and activists as a domestic terror network. In between, a comedian's suspension became a test of press independence, a presidential address to the United Nations drew ridicule for its inaccuracies, and courts, Congress, and career officials pushed back against executive power — with uneven results.

**The Weekend**

The weekend set the week's central pattern in motion. President Trump publicly and repeatedly called on Attorney General Pam Bondi to indict former FBI Director James Comey, New York Attorney General Letitia James, and Representative Adam Schiff, threatening to remove Bondi herself if she failed to act. When U.S. Attorney Erik Siebert reviewed the case against James and concluded the evidence was insufficient, he was forced out within days and replaced by Lindsey Halligan, a former personal attorney to Trump with no prior prosecutorial experience, whose mandate was explicit: deliver the charges the president wanted. This sequence — presidential demand, resistance, removal, replacement — would prove to be the week's defining chain of causation.

The same weekend produced a cluster of other assertions of executive authority. After Washington's mayor said city police would not cooperate with ICE, Trump threatened to federalize the district's law enforcement and floated declaring a national emergency to do so. He also announced plans to designate antifa, a diffuse movement with no formal leadership or membership, as a terrorist organization, calling for investigations into anyone who funds it. Abroad and at home, the administration moved unilaterally: a lethal strike on an alleged Venezuelan drug boat proceeded without congressional authorization or public evidence, National Guard troops were ordered into Memphis, and threats were leveled at both Venezuela and Afghanistan over Bagram Airbase. Domestic economic policy shifted just as abruptly, with a new $100,000 fee imposed on H-1B visa petitions amid confusion over its terms, while ICE conducted one of the largest workplace raids in recent memory at a Hyundai battery plant in Georgia, arresting 475 workers.

Media pressure also began that weekend and would not let up. FCC Chair Brendan Carr pushed ABC to suspend Jimmy Kimmel over remarks about Charlie Kirk's killing, a move that drew criticism even from Republican Senator Ted Cruz. MSNBC fired analyst Matthew Dowd and the Washington Post fired columnist Karen Attiah over commentary tied to Kirk's death, while the Pentagon tightened restrictions on journalists' access. Attorney General Bondi added her own pressure, saying employers should fire people who disparaged Kirk and that the department would pursue "hate speech," while a deputy floated RICO charges against pro-Palestinian protesters and Stephen Miller promised a government-wide campaign against an unproven organized conspiracy behind the killing. California, meanwhile, became the first state to bar law enforcement — including federal immigration agents — from masking during official duties, a local assertion of transparency that sat uneasily beside the president's public musing that he was "not so sure" protesters retained First Amendment protection at all.

**Monday**

By Monday, the antifa threat became policy. Trump signed an executive order directing federal agencies to investigate, disrupt, and dismantle activity attributed to antifa, notwithstanding the absence of any formal structure to designate and the lack of established legal authority for labeling domestic groups as terrorist organizations. Civil liberties advocates warned the order's language was broad enough to reach routine protest activity and the filming of ICE operations. That same day, Trump held a press conference urging pregnant women to avoid Tylenol and claiming vaccines cause autism, alongside a false claim about autism rates in Cuba; Health Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr. echoed suppressed-research claims. Medical experts, former President Obama, and the UK health secretary all condemned the assertions as unsupported and dangerous.

On the media front, Sinclair Broadcast Group produced a memorial special for Kirk that omitted his documented extremist statements while continuing to keep Kimmel off the air, eventually demanding he make a donation to the Kirk family and to Turning Point USA — an organization Sinclair itself supported financially — as a condition of his return. Elsewhere, nineteen detainees at a newly opened ICE facility inside Louisiana's Angola prison began a hunger strike over denied medical care, even as the administration recruited military JAG lawyers to serve as temporary immigration judges while experienced judges were being removed. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent announced U.S. readiness to support Argentina's currency ahead of that country's elections, lifting markets for an ally of the president, while in New York, the city's wealthiest donors began mobilizing hundreds of millions of dollars to defeat Zohran Mamdani despite his commanding primary victory.

**Tuesday**

Tuesday belonged to the United Nations. In a 55-minute address, Trump made a long series of falsifiable claims — describing a Serbia-Kosovo war that did not occur and which he claimed to have ended, misstating economic figures on grocery prices and inflation, and misrepresenting China's use of wind power — while also attacking London's mayor and the UN itself. Fox News hosts and the Senate majority leader nonetheless characterized the speech as truthful.

The same day, a coordinated pattern of voter-fraud prosecutions came into sharper view: secretaries of state and prosecutors in Alaska, Idaho, Iowa, Ohio, Louisiana, Texas, and Georgia pursued felony charges against voters, most of them noncitizens or people who had made administrative errors, despite data showing ineligible voting occurs at a rate of roughly one in a million ballots in Louisiana's own audit. Cases included an American Samoan who had simply followed election officials' instructions and an Iowa defendant later acquitted by a jury. Congress supplied its own friction: House Speaker Mike Johnson had, by this point, delayed swearing in Representative-elect Adelita Grijalva — who won her Arizona seat with 69 percent of the vote — far beyond any historical precedent, a delay her subsequent lawsuit alleges was intended to prevent her from signing a discharge petition to force release of Epstein-related files.

Courts offered a counterweight: a federal judge ordered $500 million in frozen federal grant funding restored to UCLA, one of more than 100 rulings against administration policies since January, according to Senator Schiff, while a separate lawsuit challenged the use of civil rights enforcement to pressure universities over diversity programs. The FCC's pressure on ABC over Kimmel continued as well, joined now by a direct threat from Trump to sue the network, calling Kimmel's reinstatement an illegal campaign contribution.

**Wednesday**

Wednesday extended the UN storyline into stranger territory. After an escalator stalled and technical glitches disrupted his appearance, Trump and press secretary Karoline Leavitt alleged deliberate sabotage and demanded arrests, even after a UN official attributed the escalator stoppage to someone in Trump's own delegation inadvertently tripping its safety mechanism.

More consequential developments followed. Reporting deepened on the Argentina bailout, indicating the rescue had been substantially driven by advocacy from hedge fund manager Rob Citrone, a personal friend of Bessent with major Argentine bond holdings who had purchased additional bonds shortly before the announcement — a conflict-of-interest question distinct from, but running parallel to, the week's law-enforcement narrative. Domestically, Trump canceled a meeting with Democratic leaders meant to avert a looming government shutdown, while the Office of Management and Budget directed agencies to prepare mass firings, rather than standard furloughs, of federal employees if funding lapsed.

The Kimmel standoff hardened further: sixty-six ABC affiliates owned by Nexstar and Sinclair declined to air his reinstated show after FCC Chairman Carr publicly urged affiliates to "take action," a call that carried particular weight given Nexstar's pending $6.2 billion acquisition of Tegna, which required FCC approval. Courts again pushed back in places, blocking the administration from conditioning emergency management grants on state cooperation with immigration enforcement as unconstitutional, even as a separate ruling declined to reinstate fired inspectors general despite acknowledging the removals likely violated the law. The administration also named Democratic election attorney Marc Elias for DOJ investigation over his past legal work against Trump, and the president threatened to deploy the National Guard to Portland based on a mischaracterized news report that mixed old and current protest footage.

**Thursday**

Thursday brought the week's central development to its conclusion. A federal grand jury indicted James Comey on charges of making false statements to Congress and obstruction, secured by Lindsey Halligan just before the five-year statute of limitations expired. Career prosecutors in the office had recommended against bringing charges for insufficient evidence, and subsequent reporting suggested the indictment may have relied on a statement Comey never actually made. The causal line from Saturday's demand to Thursday's indictment was, by this point, essentially unbroken.

The same day, Trump signed National Security Presidential Memorandum-7, directing the Justice Department, Treasury, and IRS to investigate organizations and individuals allegedly tied to political violence, explicitly naming groups funded by George Soros and Reid Hoffman. The ACLU and other legal observers warned the memorandum conflated protected speech with violence and could be used to suppress left-leaning civil society broadly — a pairing with the antifa designation that amounted to a formal reframing of dissent as a security threat.

Elsewhere, sixteen protesters were arrested in confrontations with a federal border squad deployed 2,000 miles from its normal jurisdiction near Chicago's Broadview ICE facility, and a Honduran man died in ICE custody in New York just one day after being detained — the second such death within a week. An ICE officer was filmed shoving a woman to the ground at a Manhattan immigration court after she pleaded for her detained husband's release; DHS called the act unacceptable and removed the officer pending investigation. Separately, Justice Clarence Thomas told a law school audience that settled precedent was not "gospel," suggesting the Court's conservative majority remained open to revisiting Obergefell and other substantive due process rulings.

**Friday**

The week closed on Friday with the president confirming the campaign was not finished. Trump told reporters he expected "others" among his perceived political enemies to face charges, calling them "corrupt radical left Democrats," while senior DOJ officials directed multiple U.S. attorneys' offices to investigate organizations funded by Soros, whom Trump has said should be jailed.

Outside the Broadview facility, federal officers used pepper spray, rubber bullets, and tear gas against protesters, arresting several; a CBS Chicago reporter covering the scene was also gassed and became ill for hours. In Iowa, ICE detained Ian Roberts, superintendent of the state's largest school district, during a traffic stop, citing a final removal order and lack of work authorization — a case that drew shock from local officials who noted the state had itself licensed him for the position.

ProPublica reported that Homeland Security Secretary Kristi Noem had expedited more than $11 million in FEMA disaster assistance for a Naples, Florida pier project after being contacted by a major financial donor, even as other communities' claims languished for months. The Pentagon, under Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth, reversed a prior review and confirmed that twenty soldiers involved in the 1890 killing of hundreds of Lakota people at Wounded Knee would retain their Medals of Honor. And in a smaller but telling footnote, the National Archives improperly released Democratic Representative Mikie Sherrill's sealed military records to an ally of her Republican gubernatorial opponent, an apparent privacy violation the agency called a mistake.

**In Sum**

Taken together, the week traced the movement of federal power along several converging paths at once: prosecutorial authority redirected toward named political opponents, protest and civil society reclassified under counterterrorism frameworks, broadcast regulation used to pressure editorial content, and public funds allocated in ways that benefited officials' personal and political associates. Courts and career officials supplied real friction — restoring UCLA's funding, blocking coercive grant conditions, acquitting a wrongly charged voter — but that friction proved partial, unable to prevent the ouster of a resistant prosecutor, the indictment that followed, or the seating delay of a duly elected representative. What the week recorded, in sum, was not a single dramatic rupture but the steady, documented accumulation of actions that narrowed the distance between the machinery of government and the personal grievances of the person directing it.
```
