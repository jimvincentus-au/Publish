Week 40: The Week Nothing Broke, and Nothing Held
Mass protest, a demolished East Wing, and a president seeking personal payment from his own Justice Department mark a week of small but steady democratic erosion.
Week 40 opened with the largest single day of protest in the second Trump term and closed with the government still shuttered, the East Wing of the White House reduced to rubble, and a president demanding personal payment from his own Justice Department. The seven days moved along many fronts at once, but a single pattern held them together. Power was tested in public, resisted in courtrooms, and mocked from the White House press office. Nothing broke outright. Everything bent a little further.
At the end of Week 39, the Democracy Clock stood at 7:41 p.m. It now reads 7:42 p.m., a shift of six-tenths of one minute. The movement is small by the standard of recent weeks, but it is not nothing. It reflects a week in which nearly every domain of power leaned further toward unaccountable control, while courts, states, and citizens kept up a steady, partial resistance. The clock did not leap because resistance held. It did not hold steady because the leaning did not stop.
The week began with near seven million people marching under the banner "No Kings," in cities and small towns across all fifty states. It was likely the largest coordinated demonstration of the Trump era. The White House did not engage with it; it mocked it. Official channels posted an AI-generated video of the president as a crowned monarch dumping waste on protesters below, alongside images showing Democratic leaders bowing before him in servile poses. House Speaker Mike Johnson called the rally a "Hate America" event full of paid agitators. Trump baselessly accused George Soros of funding it. In Texas, Governor Abbott deployed the National Guard before the protest even began, a preemptive show of force against people who had broken no law.
This was not simply an insult answered with an insult. It was a demonstration of how dissent is now handled by the people in charge. Millions gathering peacefully were treated not as citizens exercising a constitutional right but as an enemy formation to be ridiculed and intimidated. The message was aimed less at the protesters than at everyone watching: political speech at this scale would be met with contempt, not concession. Marc Benioff, the Salesforce chief, had endorsed a Guard deployment against San Francisco and quickly reversed himself after backlash, a small sign that some in corporate life still fear the public more than they fear the administration.
A second, closely related front involved the National Guard itself, deployed and threatened in city after city. In Portland, Judge Karin Immergut, a Trump appointee, twice blocked deployment, finding the administration's justification "untethered to the facts." In Chicago, Judge April Perry called the government's rationale "simply unreliable." These were not activist rulings from ideological opponents. They were findings by judges who examined the government's own evidence and rejected it.
The administration did not accept the rebuke. It appealed to the Ninth Circuit, which granted a temporary stay before agreeing to rehear the matter en banc, then asked the Supreme Court to intervene directly. Stephen Miller called Judge Immergut's ruling "legal insurrection." Trump personally attacked her by name. Meanwhile the president again floated invoking the Insurrection Act against American cities, a step that would turn routine urban policing into federal military occupation. The pattern across this domain was consistent: courts found facts, and the executive branch pushed past them.
Nowhere was the human cost of expanding enforcement more visible than inside ICE's operations. Weapons spending rose more than six-hundred percent over the prior year. More than one hundred seventy U.S. citizens were detained, some beaten, some tased, according to documented accounts. A pastor and a Marine veteran were shot with pepper balls during protests. A TikTok livestreamer documenting a raid was shot and later charged with assault. A man died fleeing agents on a Virginia highway, the third such death of its kind. New recruits, more than two hundred of them, were rushed into service despite disqualifying backgrounds, part of an effort to fill ten thousand new deportation officer positions quickly rather than carefully.
None of these were isolated errors. They were the visible edge of a system built to expand quickly and answer for itself slowly. Poor lighting in detention facilities caused documented harm to detainees' health. A migrant died during an aggressive raid on a cannabis farm. Citizens swept up in raids meant for others were held without charges for nearly a full day. Power expanded; oversight, where it existed at all, arrived late and thin.
Congress, for its part, offered almost nothing in the way of check or counterweight. The government shutdown became the longest in American history this week. Republican leadership showed no interest in negotiating, even as public support ran heavily in favor of extending healthcare subsidies at the center of the dispute. The Senate failed its eleventh vote on a funding bill. It also rejected a bill to guarantee pay for essential workers and troops going unpaid during the standoff. The Department of Homeland Security bought two Gulfstream jets for one hundred seventy-two million dollars in the middle of this same shutdown.
The administration used the funding freeze itself as a weapon. More than twenty-seven billion dollars in federal money was frozen or canceled for districts represented by Democrats, compared with under seven hundred fifty million for Republican districts. This was not incidental austerity. It was targeted pressure applied through the federal budget against political opponents, using a crisis the administration itself had helped prolong.
Inside this same paralysis, one member of Congress simply could not take her seat. Speaker Johnson refused to swear in Representative-elect Adelita Grijalva, despite her having won a legitimate election weeks earlier. The House stayed adjourned for seventy-seven of the previous eighty-nine days. The reason, according to the record, was to prevent her signature from completing a discharge petition that would force a floor vote on releasing files related to Jeffrey Epstein. Arizona's attorney general sued to compel her swearing-in, and the lawsuit eventually succeeded. But for this week, an elected representative's constituents went unrepresented so that a procedural maneuver could be blocked.
Redistricting fights unfolded on parallel tracks in three states. Utah's legislature passed a law stripping judicial review of redistricting and replacing it with a legislature-controlled test for partisan bias, then adopted a new map defying its own state supreme court's order to comply with a voter-approved anti-gerrymandering measure. Utah Republicans then filed a referendum to repeal that same voter-approved measure outright. In North Carolina, the state senate passed a map at the president's direct request, shifting the congressional delegation further toward Republican control and targeting the seat of a Black incumbent. Virginia responded by convening its own special session to consider a counter-gerrymander favoring Democrats. Litigation followed in multiple states, but none of it changed the trajectory. Each side escalated rather than yielded, and the redistricting fight spread rather than resolved.
The Justice Department's conduct this week showed a system organized increasingly around loyalty rather than law. Two federal prosecutors who declined to bring charges against Letitia James were fired. Their replacement, Lindsey Halligan, was criticized internally as unprepared, then was found to have leaked grand jury testimony to a reporter and used auto-deleting messages while discussing the case. James herself pleaded not guilty to the resulting mortgage fraud charges. House Republicans referred former CIA Director John Brennan for prosecution over nine-year-old testimony. James Comey's attorneys moved to have his own case dismissed, arguing the prosecutor pursuing it had been unlawfully appointed. Each thread pointed toward the same conclusion: prosecutorial power was being aimed at the president's adversaries, and internal resistance to that aim was being removed rather than heard.
Public money and public property were treated this week as instruments of personal legacy. Demolition of the White House East Wing proceeded without the review normally required, clearing space for a quarter-billion-dollar ballroom funded substantially by corporate donors. Treasury staff were told not to photograph the work. The commission meant to provide independent oversight of the project was placed under the chairmanship of a Trump loyalist. Elsewhere, the president commuted the sentence of a convicted ally, George Santos, after less than three months served, and pardoned Binance founder Changpeng Zhao, a case tied to a two-billion-dollar transaction that benefited a Trump family crypto venture. He also asked the Department of Justice, an agency he controls, to pay him roughly two hundred thirty million dollars in personal compensation for investigations into his own conduct. House Democrats opened an inquiry into the arrangement, calling it a scheme to enrich the president through a confidential process designed to delay disclosure.
Each of these acts, taken alone, might be explained away as an isolated decision. Taken together, they describe an office where the line between public duty and personal benefit had grown hard to find. Clemency rewarded loyalty. Construction rewarded donors. Litigation and prosecution power rewarded political utility. The presidency, this week, looked less like a public trust and more like a personal account with unusually large deposits.
The information environment narrowed in step with these other movements. The Pentagon replaced its traditional press corps, made up of established news organizations, with roughly sixty outlets drawn mostly from far-right and state-aligned media, after mainstream reporters refused new restrictions on their access. Trump sued the Wall Street Journal for ten billion dollars over its Epstein-related reporting. Melania Trump threatened a billion-dollar suit against journalist Michael Wolff on similar grounds. These were not quiet legal filings. They were public signals that scrutiny of the administration's past associations would carry a steep price, whether or not the suits ultimately succeeded.
Amid all this, resistance remained real, even if it rarely reversed the underlying trend. Federal judges in Oregon, Illinois, and Massachusetts each found against the administration's factual claims this week, on National Guard deployments and on the deportation of pro-Palestinian activists. The Court of Appeals for Veterans Claims unanimously rejected an attempt to pause benefit rulings during the shutdown. Illinois established a new commission to document federal agent misconduct after normal oversight channels had been weakened elsewhere. New York opened a public portal for residents to report ICE enforcement actions. California deployed its own National Guard, not against citizens but to help distribute food as shutdown-driven hunger spread. None of these actions undid the week's larger direction, but they kept the record intact, and they kept the possibility of accountability alive for whenever institutions with the power to act might choose to use it.
Week 40 will likely be remembered less for any single event than for its density. A mass protest met with monarchist mockery. Courts rebuked, and the executive pushed past them. An elected representative barred from her seat. Redistricting fights spreading state to state. A justice system turned toward loyalty. Public buildings and public funds treated as personal inheritance. Each thread, alone, might be called an exception. Together, across a single week, they described something closer to an operating method. The clock's small movement this week is the record of that method continuing to run, tested at every turn, and not yet stopped.
