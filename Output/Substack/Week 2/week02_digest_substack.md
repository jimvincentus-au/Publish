# This Week in Democracy: The Opening Barrage

*In his first full week back in office, Trump moved simultaneously against watchdogs, courts, immigrants, transgender Americans, and the federal workforce — testing how much can be done before anyone can stop it.*

![Header image](week02-digest-featured.jpg)

```markdown
The second week of the new administration did not so much begin as detonate. In the span of seven days, the federal government moved on immigration, the military, civil rights, federal oversight, foreign trade, and the machinery of its own bureaucracy — often several fronts at once, often before the previous day's actions had been fully absorbed. The defining feature of the week was not any single order but the sheer simultaneity of them: watchdogs were fired, prosecutors were purged, courts intervened, and the administration pressed forward anyway.

**The Weekend**

The weekend set the tone. Trump pardoned or commuted the sentences of roughly 1,500 people convicted in connection with the January 6 attack on the Capitol, including Oath Keepers leader Stewart Rhodes, who had been found guilty of seditious conspiracy. Some of the pardoned defendants publicly declined the clemency, and the Justice Department later took down the public database that had documented the underlying charges. Alongside the pardons came a cluster of first-day executive orders: a national emergency declared at the southern border, the cancellation of resettlement flights for 1,600 Afghan refugees already in the pipeline, the dismantling of diversity and affirmative-action programs, the disbanding of the Equal Employment Opportunity Commission, and new authority for immigration agents to enter schools and churches, spaces long treated as off-limits. By Sunday, ICE and partner agencies had launched coordinated raids in Chicago and other cities, arresting more than 950 people in a single weekend at some of those same sensitive locations. In North Carolina, a judge's attempt to discard 60,000 certified votes in an effort to overturn a state supreme court race drew immediate, organized pushback from civil society groups — an early sign that election results themselves would be contested terrain.

The weekend's most structurally significant move came quietly: the dismissal of at least seventeen inspectors general across federal agencies, carried out without the thirty-day congressional notice the law requires. The Council of Inspectors General warned the firings might be unlawful; objections came from both parties. Days later, Acting Attorney General James McHenry fired more than a dozen career Justice Department prosecutors who had worked on cases against Trump, citing distrust in their willingness to carry out the administration's agenda. These were not isolated personnel decisions — they were the opening moves in what would become a running story of independent oversight being cleared out from inside the executive branch.

**Monday**

By Monday, the focus shifted to the military. Trump signed a cluster of orders banning transgender service members, eliminating diversity offices at the Pentagon and the Department of Homeland Security, and reinstating — with back pay — the roughly 8,000 troops discharged for refusing the COVID-19 vaccine. These were three distinct policies aimed at three distinct groups, unified mainly by their departure from institutional continuity in favor of ideological and loyalty-based sorting.

The same day, a federal judge issued an administrative stay blocking the funding freeze the administration had announced days earlier, a freeze that threatened to disrupt healthcare, education, and disaster-relief programs nationwide. It was the first of several judicial interventions that would define the week. Also on Monday, according to reporting rather than any formal order, White House aide Stephen Miller was said to be pressuring Justice Department officials to compile lists of FBI agents who had worked on the January 6 and classified-documents investigations, with the bureau's acting director reportedly resisting full compliance.

Elsewhere, the administration moved to strip civil service protections from career federal employees under a new "Schedule Policy/Career" designation and offered a buyout to federal workers with pay through September. Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth revoked the security detail and clearance of retired Gen. Mark Milley, a past critic of Trump. And a diplomatic dispute with Colombia over deportation flights escalated within roughly a day into cancelled visas and 25 percent tariffs, before flights resumed.

**Tuesday**

Tuesday brought some of the week's most consequential — and most immediately felt — actions. An executive order cut off federal funding, insurance coverage, and institutional support for gender-affirming medical care for anyone under nineteen. Hospitals including NYU Langone and Denver Health paused treatment for young patients within hours. The administration also pursued an order attempting to end birthright citizenship, a guarantee written into the 14th Amendment; twenty-two state attorneys general sued, and a federal judge quickly blocked the order, calling it "blatantly unconstitutional." Trump also invoked the Alien Enemies Act to announce plans for a 30,000-person migrant detention facility at Guantánamo Bay, a military prison long associated with human rights abuses now being repurposed for civilian immigration detention. Meanwhile, the funding freeze that had been blocked a day earlier continued to cause disruption — including reports of Medicaid portal outages — prompting a second judicial restraining order.

**Wednesday**

By Wednesday, the Guantánamo plan moved from announcement to formal policy: a presidential memorandum directed the Secretaries of Defense and Homeland Security to expand the detention center to full capacity, and Trump signed the Laken Riley Act mandating detention for undocumented immigrants charged with theft-related offenses. The same day saw a wave of education orders — targeting what the administration called "radical indoctrination" in K-12 schools, reviving the 1776 Commission on patriotic education, expanding federal support for school choice, and directing federal agencies to investigate, and potentially move to deport, pro-Palestinian student activists under an anti-antisemitism framework.

In Washington, Robert F. Kennedy Jr. faced pointed questioning in Senate confirmation hearings over his history of vaccine misinformation and shifting positions on abortion, drawing opposition from thousands of physicians. And the funding freeze took another turn: the Office of Management and Budget formally rescinded its freeze memo, only for the White House press secretary to immediately clarify that the underlying executive orders "remain in full force and effect" — leaving the actual status of federal funding, and the government's own position on it, genuinely unclear.

**Thursday**

Thursday was the busiest single day of the week. Trump signed an order requiring independent regulatory agencies — including the SEC and FTC — to submit their policy priorities and budgets for White House approval, undoing decades of structural independence that Congress had built into those agencies. He also signed a further set of orders restricting transgender rights: banning transgender athletes from women's sports categories, transferring transgender women held in federal prisons to men's facilities while cutting their access to gender-affirming treatment, and declaring English the official language of the United States.

On trade, the administration imposed 25 percent tariffs on Canada and Mexico and 10 percent on China, triggering a stock market drop and threats of retaliation from Ottawa, before carving out exemptions for USMCA-covered goods and autos within days. Elon Musk's role in federal operations expanded sharply: Trump signed an order broadening Musk's oversight of a centralized federal payment system, and a senior career Treasury official resigned after associates of Musk demanded access to sensitive payment infrastructure — a demand connected to an earlier whistleblower lawsuit alleging that federal employee data was being collected for a Musk associate under the guise of official communications.

The day also brought a batch of deregulatory and industrial orders — reviving the coal industry, expanding tree-cutting on public lands, boosting deep-sea mining, creating a sovereign wealth fund and a strategic bitcoin reserve, and pausing enforcement of the Foreign Corrupt Practices Act. Amid all of this, a federal judge issued a formal injunction — stronger than the earlier temporary blocks — preventing the administration from impounding federal funds owed to states. And in his own confirmation hearing, FBI director nominee Kash Patel declined to rule out politically motivated investigations of Trump's opponents.

**Friday**

The week closed on Friday with three threads converging. Trump signed a sweeping deregulation order requiring agencies to repeal at least ten existing regulations for every new one issued, with a mandate that net new regulatory costs for the fiscal year be significantly less than zero. The FBI purge, previously reported anonymously, became visible: at least five senior officials in major field offices were ordered to resign or were terminated, part of a broader campaign against career agents tied to the January 6 and Trump-related investigations, drawing a warning from the FBI Agents Association about the resulting loss of institutional capacity. The National Labor Relations Board was left without the quorum needed to issue significant rulings after Trump fired its general counsel and chair.

Reporting also revealed that donors with tax matters pending before the IRS had paid off more than $130,000 in campaign debt for Billy Long, Trump's nominee to lead the agency, shortly after his nomination was announced — and that a young engineer connected to Musk's companies had gained direct access to Treasury payment systems, following the Treasury official's resignation earlier in the week. In a smaller but telling move, DHS dissolved an internal advisory board that had reviewed local police immigration partnerships for civil rights concerns, removing oversight just as those partnerships were expanding.

**The Pattern**

Taken as a whole, the week established a pattern that would recur throughout the young administration: rapid, wide-ranging assertions of executive authority, followed almost immediately by legal challenge, and in several cases by outright judicial rejection — on the funding freeze, on birthright citizenship, on the impoundment of state funds. Courts emerged as the most consistent institutional check on the week's actions, intervening not once but repeatedly, sometimes within days of an order's signing. At the same time, the administration's response to those checks was not always to comply cleanly; the funding freeze was rescinded on paper while its substance was reasserted in the same breath, a maneuver that blurred the line between compliance and defiance.

Inside the executive branch, the machinery of independent oversight — inspectors general, career prosecutors, FBI leadership, an independent labor board — was cleared out in overlapping waves, while regulatory agencies built for independence were placed under direct White House control. For immigrant communities, transgender Americans, and federal civil servants alike, the week's policies were not abstract: hospitals halted treatment, arrests spiked into the thousands, and career officials lost their positions within days of an inauguration. Whether measured by volume, speed, or the breadth of institutions touched, it was a week that tested, in real time, how far executive power could be extended before the other branches of government were able to respond.
```
