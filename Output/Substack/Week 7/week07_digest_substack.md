# This Week in Democracy: The Machinery Turns

*A week of funding threats, deportations in defiance of courts, and a presidency that treated federal power as personal leverage*

![Header image](week07-digest-featured.jpg)

```markdown
The first week of March opened with a burst of executive activity so dense it was hard, at the time, to see its shape. By the time the week closed seven days later, the pattern was clearer: federal money, immigration law, and executive orders were being used as instruments of pressure against universities, law firms, immigrants, and independent officials, while courts intervened repeatedly but only partially to check it.

The weekend set the tone. The administration issued an executive order threatening to withhold funding from universities it judged to have "hostile environments," a designation that froze $2.2 billion at Harvard and pushed Columbia into accepting sweeping administrative concessions in exchange for restored funding. Warning letters went out to 60 universities; San Jose State was targeted before an investigation into it had even concluded. That same weekend, the Department of Homeland Security eliminated three of its own oversight offices — the ones responsible for civil rights, detention conditions, and citizenship services — laying off most of their staff and later limiting complaints to an English-only online portal. Immigration enforcement turned personal and specific: Columbia graduate Mahmoud Khalil was arrested without a warrant over pro-Palestinian activism, Rümeysa Öztürk was detained over an op-ed she had written, and Yunseo Chung had her green card revoked. A father with no criminal record was deported and separated from his 12-year-old son. Four U.S. citizen children, including one being treated for brain cancer, were deported alongside their undocumented parents.

Elsewhere that weekend, Trump fired Democratic FTC Commissioner Rebecca Slaughter, asserting a unilateral removal power over an independent agency despite decades-old statutory protections; she sued immediately. An executive order stripped collective bargaining rights from roughly two-thirds of federal employees. Other orders reached into cultural and civic terrain: dismantling Voice of America and the U.S. Agency for Global Media, directing the Smithsonian to remove content deemed "anti-American," ordering the removal of slavery and Native American removal exhibits from national parks, and declaring English the official language in a move that revoked federal language-access protections. Trump also attacked federal judge James Boasberg over an adverse deportation ruling, calling him a "radical left lunatic" and urging impeachment — an escalation echoed separately by Rep. Eli Crane and Elon Musk regarding judges who ruled against Musk's cost-cutting initiative, and pointed enough to draw a rare public rebuke from Chief Justice Roberts. An executive order aimed at mail-in voting sought proof-of-citizenship requirements and the elimination of ballot-receipt grace periods, drawing immediate legal challenges. Amid all this, resistance was visible too: hundreds confronted Vice President Vance in Vermont, Boston's mayor defended sanctuary policy before Congress, and a new hotline network mobilized volunteers to track ICE activity.

By Monday, the focus shifted to trade. The administration imposed 25 percent tariffs on Canada and Mexico and raised tariffs on China to 20 percent. Retaliation was immediate and substantial: Canada announced C$30 billion in counter-tariffs, China imposed 15 percent tariffs on U.S. farm products along with export restrictions, and Mexico's president promised her own response. The same day, the American Bar Association condemned the intimidation of judges, even as the Justice Department opened a review of a politically sensitive election-fraud conviction and Rep. Mike Turner was removed from the House Intelligence Committee for contradicting the administration's account of Russian cybersecurity threats. Health and law enforcement independence also came under strain: HHS Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr. claimed it was "very very difficult" for measles to kill a healthy person and promoted cod liver oil as treatment, this during an active outbreak in which children with no underlying conditions had already died in Texas. In New York, senior FBI official James Dennehy was forced to retire after resisting a purge of agents who had investigated the January 6 Capitol assault, following the earlier removal of eight other FBI officials. The Associated Press, meanwhile, amended its First Amendment lawsuit over its exclusion from the White House press pool after refusing to rename the Gulf of Mexico. And an order expanding logging across 280 million acres of national forest directed agencies to bypass standard Endangered Species Act review.

Tuesday belonged to Trump's first joint address to Congress. The guest list was built around hardline immigration and transgender policy themes, and the speech included a claim that Musk's Department of Government Efficiency initiative had uncovered "hundreds of billions" in fraud — a figure later shown to be false, with documented savings under $9 billion and some cited examples fabricated outright. Inside the chamber, Democratic Rep. Al Green was forcibly removed by the sergeant-at-arms after repeatedly interrupting the speech to shout that Trump had "no mandate" to cut Medicaid, a protest and ejection that would return to the story two days later. Away from the Capitol, Rep. Richard Hudson, who leads House Republicans' campaign arm, directed members to stop holding town halls in order to avoid drawing attention to public backlash over government cuts — a quieter but telling retreat from constituent accountability. And State Street, the investment firm behind the Fearless Girl statue, quietly dropped its requirement that companies in its index funds maintain gender-diverse boards, part of a broader corporate pullback from diversity commitments under political pressure.

Wednesday brought the week's clearest judicial pushback. The Supreme Court, in a 5–4 decision, rejected the administration's attempt to continue freezing nearly $2 billion in foreign aid. The same day, a federal court ruled that Trump's firing of National Labor Relations Board member Gwynne Wilcox had been illegal, ordering her reinstatement and stating plainly that the president is "not a king." A third check followed from the Merit Systems Protection Board, which ordered the USDA to temporarily reinstate nearly 6,000 fired probationary employees, finding reasonable grounds that the terminations had violated federal law. Even as courts pushed back, pressure on universities widened rather than narrowed: the Justice Department opened a Title VII civil rights investigation into the University of California system over alleged failures to address antisemitic harassment, extending federal scrutiny from private universities to a major public system, and Barnard College announced the expulsion of several student protesters following the pattern set at Columbia. In Alabama, a federal court held a hearing on the state attorney general's threats to prosecute groups that help women travel out of state for abortions — a novel test of interstate reproductive rights. And reports surfaced that DOGE was planning to cut up to half the IRS workforce and as many as 83,000 jobs at Veterans Affairs, reducing staffing to 2019 levels despite rising demand for services.

Thursday's developments carried a distinctly personal dimension. Trump signed an executive order creating a Strategic Bitcoin Reserve and Digital Asset Stockpile just ahead of a "crypto summit," a move that legitimized and boosted the value of bitcoin holdings, including his own, while exposing public funds to risk for unclear public benefit. The same day, he signed an order suspending security clearances and terminating federal contracts tied to Perkins Coie, a law firm that had represented Hillary Clinton and other Democratic clients, citing its past Steele dossier work and its diversity hiring practices — a continuation of a pattern that had begun the prior weekend with a similar order against Jenner & Block. A separate memo directed agencies to enforce bond requirements on plaintiffs seeking injunctions against the government, a change legal experts warned would raise the financial cost of challenging executive action in court.

Yet judicial and legislative checks continued in parallel: a federal judge ruled that Trump lacked authority to freeze congressionally appropriated funds, issuing a preliminary injunction, while the House voted to formally censure Al Green for his outburst two days earlier, with ten Democrats joining Republicans. Elsewhere, the Justice Department dismissed a Biden-era environmental lawsuit against a Louisiana chemical plant accused of emitting carcinogens near a majority-Black community, framing the dismissal as an end to "environmental justice" enforcement, and a congressional investigation into oil industry disinformation was allowed to lapse under Republican control.

The week closed on Friday with the formal culmination of its central throughline. The Justice Department, joined by HHS, the Education Department, and the General Services Administration, announced the immediate cancellation of $400 million in federal grants and contracts to Columbia University — described as the first phase of a broader campaign. An executive order narrowed eligibility for Public Service Loan Forgiveness, directing the Education Department to exclude organizations judged to have a "substantial illegal purpose," a category that could include those aiding immigration violations or civil disobedience. The State Department announced plans to use artificial intelligence to screen the social media activity of foreign students, aiming to revoke visas from those perceived as sympathetic to Hamas, a program that could affect tens of thousands of visa holders. Despite multiple court orders blocking the practice, transgender women not named in existing lawsuits continued to be transferred to men's federal facilities. A German permanent resident of nearly two decades was detained and allegedly mistreated by border officials at Boston's airport, prompting a formal inquiry from the German government. And, in a rare non-adversarial note, Trump signed an order establishing a White House task force, which he would chair himself, to coordinate federal preparations for the 2026 World Cup.

Taken as a whole, the week showed an administration comfortable using the tools available to it — appropriated funds, enforcement discretion, executive orders, and personnel authority — against institutions and individuals it viewed as adversarial, while courts, an independent labor board, and a merit board offered resistance that was real but incomplete. Universities lost hundreds of millions of dollars over their handling of protest and speech. A green-card holder and several students faced detention or deportation for political expression. A father was separated from his child; American children were deported with their parents. Judges faced calls for impeachment over their rulings, and a sitting member of Congress was removed from the floor and later censured for a floor protest. Courts blocked a foreign-aid freeze and ordered federal employees reinstated, but tariffs, a bitcoin reserve tied to the president's own holdings, and mounting pressure on universities moved forward largely on schedule. The week's clearest counterweight came not from any single institution but from the layered response of courts, an independent board, a legal association, and ordinary people organizing hotlines and showing up at town halls — a reminder that accountability, in this period, was being tested and defended at once, often within the same day.
```
