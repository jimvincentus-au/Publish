A Killing in Minneapolis, a Government's Response
The shooting of a US citizen by an ICE agent set off a week of federal escalation, resignations, lawsuits, and threats of martial law
```markdown
This was the week a shooting in Minneapolis became a national reckoning with federal power. Renee Nicole Good, a US citizen, was killed by an ICE agent during a massive immigration enforcement surge, and the seven days that followed traced a single, widening crisis: an administration defending the shooter, a Justice Department declining to investigate, career officials resigning in protest, states suing to stop the operation, and a president openly weighing whether to send in the military. Alongside it, two other fronts opened — a criminal investigation into the Federal Reserve chairman and a unilateral seizure of Venezuelan oil revenue — while courts, in scattered but real rulings, pushed back against pieces of the expanding executive claim to power.
The weekend began with the enforcement operation that killed Good. Roughly 2,000 federal agents had been deployed into Minneapolis-St. Paul under "Operation Metro Surge," and what started as workplace raids spread into residential neighborhoods before an ICE agent shot Good, an incident captured on video. Within hours the FBI took over the investigation, cutting Minnesota's Bureau of Criminal Apprehension out of the case despite an earlier federal court ruling favoring congressional oversight of ICE facilities. The White House called the shooting self-defense, and President Trump endorsed Vice President Vance's unsupported claim that Good had been a paid agitator — a charge the video evidence did not support. Trump then announced he was withholding $129 billion in federal funding from Minnesota over unrelated child-care fraud allegations, a move widely understood as retaliation against a state now at the center of a federal shooting controversy. Protesters did not wait for answers: more than 1,000 demonstrations under the banner "ICE Out For Good Weekend of Action" spread across the country, with confrontations and arrests concentrated in Minneapolis. In a separate but overlapping development, the administration also moved to seize control of Venezuela's oil revenue following the ouster of Nicolás Maduro, declaring a national emergency over the proceeds without consulting Congress.
By Monday the Good case had metastasized into an institutional crisis inside the government itself. Four leaders of the Justice Department's Civil Rights Division and six federal prosecutors in Minnesota resigned after DOJ declined to open a civil-rights investigation into the shooting, instead directing scrutiny toward Good's activist history. Reporting surfaced that federal agents had left the crime scene, that no body-camera footage had been released, and that a selectively edited video had been leaked to a friendly outlet before any official release — a pattern critics said was designed to control the narrative rather than establish facts. Minnesota Attorney General Keith Ellison filed suit against the Department of Homeland Security, alleging excessive force, warrantless arrests, and targeting of courts, churches, and schools, and seeking to halt the surge outright. The same day, the administration opened a second front entirely apart from Minneapolis: the US Attorney's office in Washington launched a criminal investigation into Federal Reserve Chair Jerome Powell over building renovation costs and issued a subpoena to the Fed, following Trump's earlier threat to pursue Powell criminally over interest rate policy. Congress, meanwhile, pressed on three separate tracks — passing the Epstein Files Transparency Act amid DOJ non-compliance, advancing a Senate bill to limit further Venezuela military action, and investigating whether pardoned January 6 defendants had been hired at ICE. Senator Mark Kelly filed suit against Defense Secretary Hegseth after being censured and threatened with reduced rank for urging troops to refuse unlawful orders.
Tuesday's developments were quieter but no less telling. Bill and Hillary Clinton refused to comply with House Oversight subpoenas in the Epstein probe, prompting a contempt threat from Chairman Comer. A federal judge ordered the restoration of whistleblower attorney Mark Zaid's security clearance, finding its earlier revocation to be improper political retaliation. The Supreme Court heard oral arguments in two major cases — one testing the legality of Trump's global tariffs under emergency powers law, the other concerning state bans on transgender athletes in girls' sports — with the conservative majority appearing skeptical of the challengers in both. The Powell investigation drew an unusual international response: central bank leaders around the world issued a joint statement backing Powell and warning that the administration's pressure campaign endangered the Federal Reserve's independence and, by extension, global financial stability. Minnesota's legal challenge to the ICE surge also expanded, with new claims focused on the operation's impact on the state's Somali community and language describing the situation as a "federal invasion."
Wednesday was the week's heaviest day. A newly released Justice Department memo revealed that the administration had approved the military raid that captured Maduro without resolving its legality under international law, asserting that domestic authorization alone was sufficient — a striking claim to unilateral war-making power. That same day, the FBI executed a search warrant at the home of Washington Post reporter Hannah Natanson, seizing devices in connection with an investigation into a government contractor, a move press freedom advocates called an aggressive escalation against journalists. DOJ leadership formally stated there was "no basis" for a civil-rights investigation into the Good shooting, and more senior officials in the Civil Rights Division resigned in response — a second and distinct wave of departures from the ones a day earlier. Separately, Senator Elissa Slotkin and Representatives Jason Crow, Maggie Goodlander, and Chrissy Houlahan confirmed they were under federal investigation over their participation in a video urging troops to refuse illegal orders — speech Trump had already labeled "seditious." Representative Robin Kelly, backed by 70 members of Congress, filed articles of impeachment against DHS Secretary Kristi Noem over the Minneapolis operation. And even as the administration pursued the seized Venezuelan oil revenue — completing its first sale of the crude — the Trump Organization announced new $3 billion and $7 billion real-estate deals with a Saudi developer, reviving longstanding conflict-of-interest concerns. Trump himself commented that Good had been "very, very disrespectful to law enforcement," a remark that landed the same day federal agents used tear gas and flash-bangs against protesters and a federal officer shot a second man during an enforcement operation in Minneapolis.
Thursday brought the week's clearest legislative defeat and its most alarming rhetorical escalation. A Senate resolution requiring congressional approval for further military action in Venezuela failed on a 50-50 tie-breaking vote, with Vice President Vance casting the deciding vote against it after two senators reversed their earlier support following administration assurances — leaving the expansive war-powers claim from the Maduro memo effectively unchecked. A federal appeals court reversed the order that had freed Mahmoud Khalil, finding the lower court lacked jurisdiction, and reopened the possibility of his re-detention while the administration continued pursuing his deportation over his political activism. The ACLU filed a class-action lawsuit against DHS and Secretary Noem alleging that ICE agents in Minnesota had disproportionately targeted Somali and Latino communities through unlawful stops and arrests. Then came the second shooting: a federal officer shot a man in the leg during a traffic stop in north Minneapolis, reigniting protests in a city already reeling from the Good killing. In response, Trump threatened to invoke the Insurrection Act if Minnesota officials could not control the unrest, and Noem publicly confirmed she had discussed the option with him, asserting he had constitutional authority to deploy troops domestically.
Friday did not resolve the standoff; it deepened it. Trump repeated the Insurrection Act threat, describing protesters as attacking federal law enforcement, while Governor Tim Walz urged peaceful demonstration and characterized the federal response as organized brutality. Congressional Democrats held a field hearing in St. Paul titled "Kidnapped and Disappeared" to document the surge's impact, and Illinois's governor formed a state accountability commission to monitor federal immigration officers operating in the state. Courts delivered a cluster of partial checks: a judge barred federal agents from using force against nonviolent observers of immigration enforcement in Minnesota, a separate federal judge ruled the Justice Department was not entitled to sensitive California voter data, and another judge indicated he would issue a protective order for noncitizen academics facing retaliatory immigration action. California, for its part, enacted a law permitting the state arrest of federal agents who violate state law, sharpening the standoff between state and federal authority. Reporting also indicated that ICE had detained more than 2,400 people in Minnesota during the surge, including some with legal status, while a Texas ICE detainee's death was newly under investigation as a possible homicide. In a smaller but symbolically pointed move, the Pentagon formalized a policy stripping Stars and Stripes of its independent civilian editor and ombudsman, drawing criticism from press freedom advocates and Senate Democrats.
By the end of the week, the throughline was unmistakable: a single act of lethal force by a federal agent had exposed how far an administration would go to shield its own personnel from scrutiny, and how many other institutions — the courts, the press, the Federal Reserve, the military's internal speech protections, even a decades-old military newspaper — were being tested at the same time. Career prosecutors quit rather than certify a decision they viewed as indefensible. States sued, organized commissions, and, in California's case, asserted a novel power to arrest federal agents. Judges granted narrow but real relief in several cases even as a war-powers resolution failed and an appeals court reopened the door to a deportation case. None of these checks stopped the underlying escalation — by Friday, the president was still threatening to invoke the Insurrection Act against an American state — but neither did the government's pressure go unanswered. The week stands as a record of an executive branch testing the outer edges of accountability, and of institutions, unevenly and incompletely, testing back.
```
