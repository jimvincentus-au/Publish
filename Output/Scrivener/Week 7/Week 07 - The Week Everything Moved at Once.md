Week 7: The Week Everything Moved at Once
Nearly thirty protections weakened, a handful strengthened, and the clock barely moved because the currents canceled each other out.
The record for this week closes on a paradox worth sitting with. The Democracy Clock barely moved. Yet the seven days it measures may be among the most consequential of the term so far, a week in which the machinery of government was tested in nearly every direction at once. This is the first entry in this account where the clock's stillness does not mean calm. It means the system absorbed so much pressure, in so many places, that no single measure could capture it cleanly. The week deserves to be read for what it reveals about how power moves when it moves everywhere at once.
At the close of the previous week, the Democracy Clock read 7:32 p.m. It closed this week at the same time, a net shift of six hundredths of a minute. The number is almost too small to state. But it is not the story. Nearly thirty separate democratic protections weakened this week, some by wide margins. A smaller number strengthened, mostly through court rulings that checked executive overreach. The near-zero net movement was not stability. It was cancellation. Dozens of currents pulled in different directions at once, leaving the surface still while the depths churned.
Consider first what happened to people without full legal protection. Immigration and Customs Enforcement spent the week treating deportation as a tool of political control rather than law enforcement. Mahmoud Khalil, a green-card holder, was seized without a warrant for his role in campus activism. Yunseo Chung, a Columbia student, had her status stripped after anti-war protest. Rümeysa Öztürk was detained over an op-ed. Farm-worker organizer Alfredo Juarez Zeferino was picked up and ultimately left the country. None of these people were accused of crimes. In each case, the offense was speech.
The pattern deepened with Kilmar Ábrego García, deported in direct violation of a standing 2019 court order. Here the government did not merely stretch immigration law; it defied a judicial command outright. Days later, the administration invoked the Alien Enemies Act of 1798, a wartime statute, to justify mass deportations tied to alleged gang membership. Federal judges would go on to reject this use of the law as an abuse of emergency authority. But the invocation itself signaled something clear. Extraordinary powers, once reserved for extraordinary circumstances, were being reached for as ordinary tools.
By week's end, the State Department had announced plans to use artificial intelligence to scan the social media accounts of foreign students, revoking visas based on perceived sympathies rather than conduct. Four U.S. citizen children, one battling brain cancer, were deported alongside undocumented parents. Taken together, these events did not read as isolated incidents. They read as a single, sustained argument: that legal status, even citizenship itself, could be treated as provisional when political loyalty was in question.
The judiciary, meanwhile, came under direct attack from the people meant to respect its rulings. Trump publicly called for the impeachment of Judge James Boasberg after an adverse ruling on deportation flights, a demand serious enough to draw a rare public rebuke from Chief Justice Roberts. Elon Musk echoed the call. Representative Eli Crane filed formal impeachment articles against a judge who had ruled against DOGE. None of these judges had done anything but rule against the government in ordinary litigation.
The American Bar Association felt compelled to issue a public statement defending judicial independence, an unusual step for an organization that typically stays above the partisan fray. Separately, federal judges quietly proposed removing their own security details from Justice Department control, fearing that protection itself could become a lever of pressure. These were not the actions of an institution operating normally. They were the actions of an institution bracing for a fight.
Universities absorbed a comparable wave of pressure, this time delivered through the language of budgets rather than the language of impeachment. Columbia University lost four hundred million dollars in federal funding tied to accusations of tolerating hostile campus environments, and within weeks began making policy concessions to recover it. The University of Pennsylvania had one hundred seventy-five million dollars suspended over its handling of transgender athletes. The Department of Justice opened a Title VII investigation into the University of California system. A federal prosecutor threatened to blacklist law schools that kept diversity programs from federal hiring.
Sixty universities received formal warning letters over their handling of pro-Palestinian protest. The pressure produced quick compliance. Barnard College expelled student protesters. Swarthmore suspended others. What makes this development significant is not any single funding cut, but the mechanism it revealed: federal money, once granted, could be treated as conditional on ideological alignment. Institutions built on independence chose to bend rather than lose it.
Inside the executive branch itself, internal oversight was torn down with unusual speed. The Department of Homeland Security eliminated three watchdog offices responsible for civil rights and detention oversight in a single stroke, a change that would later be shown to cut investigation rates from twenty percent to three. The Department of Health and Human Services ended public participation in its rulemaking process entirely. A completed civil rights report documenting problems with immigration enforcement was quietly buried rather than released.
The Department of Veterans Affairs shut down its only office tracking racial disparities in veteran disability claims. More than two thousand public datasets vanished from federal repositories, taking with them the raw material that researchers and journalists use to hold agencies accountable. None of these changes required new legislation. They required only administrative will, and administrative will was present in abundance.
Two firings this week tested a specific constitutional question with real teeth: does a president have the power to remove independent regulators without cause. Rebecca Slaughter was dismissed from the Federal Trade Commission over a policy disagreement. Gwynne Wilcox was removed from the National Labor Relations Board on similar grounds. Here the courts pushed back, at least in part. A federal court ruled Wilcox's firing unlawful and ordered her reinstated, one of the week's few structural corrections rather than erosions. The doctrine protecting independent agencies from at-will presidential removal, built over decades, held for one week longer. Whether it holds through appeal remains an open question the record leaves for later chapters.
The civil service absorbed a different kind of blow. An executive order stripped collective bargaining rights from more than a million federal workers in a single stroke. Mass firings proceeded across the Department of Education, the Department of Agriculture, and the Department of Veterans Affairs, part of a workforce reduction that would eventually reach the Internal Revenue Service as well. Here too a check appeared. The Merit Systems Protection Board found reasonable grounds that the firings violated federal law and ordered nearly six thousand USDA workers temporarily reinstated. The ruling did not undo the broader pattern, but it put on the record that an independent board still believed the terminations were probably illegal.
Financial entanglement between the presidency and private interest surfaced again and again. Trump signed an executive order creating a federal bitcoin and digital asset reserve, a policy move that directly benefited crypto holdings tied to his own network. Days after receiving a large campaign donation from an affected company, he publicly opposed a Medicare rule the company disliked. Separately, a fraud prosecution against a crypto executive was halted shortly after that executive made a large purchase of Trump-branded digital tokens. Any one of these episodes might be explained away. Together, they describe a pattern in which policy and personal enrichment moved in close, visible proximity.
Independent journalism absorbed one of the week's sharpest single blows. An executive order dismantled Voice of America and the U.S. Agency for Global Media, ending eighty years of continuous international broadcasting and placing thirteen hundred staff on leave in a single action. Separately, the president suggested during a public address that law enforcement should scrutinize named domestic news organizations for their coverage. Voice of America staff sued to block the shutdown, and the litigation would continue well past this week. But the immediate effect was silence where broadcast had run without pause for generations.
A final development, smaller in scale but unmistakable in intent, touched the nation's historical record. An executive order directed the Smithsonian Institution to remove content deemed improper or divisive, aimed in particular at exhibits on African American history. National parks quietly removed displays addressing slavery and the removal of Native peoples. Separately, it was reported that the president had requested the original Declaration of Independence be moved from the National Archives to the Oval Office. None of these actions changed law. They changed what visitors would see, and what future visitors would be told happened.
Two developments this week ran the other way, and they deserve to be named plainly rather than folded into the larger tide. The Supreme Court rejected the administration's attempt to continue freezing nearly two billion dollars in congressionally approved foreign aid. A federal judge separately ruled that the president lacked authority to impound funds Congress had already appropriated. These were not dramatic reversals of the week's broader direction, but they were real. They showed that the judiciary, even under direct political attack, kept issuing rulings the executive branch did not want, and for now, kept complying with them.
No single scheduled action stands out from this record as requiring forward notice. The litigation opened this week, on Voice of America, on the NLRB firing, on university funding freezes, will resolve in courts over months rather than days.
What this week leaves behind is not a dramatic turn but a demonstration. Pressure was applied to courts, universities, watchdog offices, the civil service, the press, and the historical record, all within seven days. Most of it met little resistance beyond scattered rulings and a handful of lawsuits. The clock's stillness this week was not evidence of balance. It was evidence that erosion and correction can occur at nearly the same volume without either canceling the other's significance. The record shows a government testing, in parallel, how far its reach could extend before something pushed back. In most places, not much did.
