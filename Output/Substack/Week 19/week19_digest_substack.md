# Pardons, Purges, and Pushback: A Week of Executive Overreach and Judicial Resistance

*Harvard fights back, courts block tariffs and deportations, and a wave of pardons rewards political loyalty*

![Header image](week19-digest-featured.jpg)

```markdown
This was a week defined by two competing forces: an executive branch testing how far institutional pressure could be pushed, and a judiciary that pushed back at nearly every turn, even when its rulings didn't always hold. By week's end, Harvard remained locked in open legal war with the administration, a cluster of pardons had raised fresh questions about who gets mercy and why, and a Supreme Court decision had stripped legal protection from hundreds of thousands of migrants, even as lower courts kept finding that other enforcement actions had gone too far.

The weekend set the tone. An executive order dismantling diversity, equity, and inclusion programs reached into West Point, where student clubs were disbanded and materials removed, part of a broader pattern that also saw a critically ill child's humanitarian parole revoked and a Mexican singer's visa canceled. In Alabama, a U.S.-born citizen named Leonardo Garcia Venegas was detained for hours while filming an ICE operation, despite presenting valid identification—an early sign that aggressive enforcement tactics were not reliably distinguishing citizens from targets. And in Massachusetts, the administration made its opening move against Harvard, revoking the university's certification to enroll international students. Harvard sued immediately, and within a day Judge Allison Burroughs had issued a temporary restraining order blocking the action. Elsewhere, Trump announced a 50% tariff on European Union goods effective June 1, then extended the deadline to July 9 after the European Commission asked for more time—part of a now-familiar pattern of trade brinkmanship conducted through personal announcement rather than institutional process. The weekend also saw the Indivisible movement organize what it called the largest day of protest of Trump's second term, spanning more than 1,300 cities, while federal judges began quietly discussing whether they needed their own independent security force, wary that the Justice-Department-controlled U.S. Marshals Service might be pressured to withdraw protection from judges who ruled against the administration.

By Monday, the week's second major thread emerged: a cluster of pardons that critics said rewarded loyalty and access rather than legal merit. Trump pardoned former Virginia sheriff Scott Jenkins, convicted of bribery, and Paul Walczak, a nursing-home executive convicted of tax crimes whose mother had attended a high-priced Mar-a-Lago fundraiser. The same day, the administration's proposed federal budget revealed its priorities in stark terms, sharply increasing funding for ICE and the Pentagon while proposing steep cuts to SNAP and Medicaid that would shift costs onto states and threaten benefits for millions. NPR filed a First Amendment lawsuit challenging an executive order that barred federal funds for NPR and PBS, arguing the order retaliated against the network for unfavorable coverage. Federal judges, meanwhile, continued rejecting the administration's attempts to sidestep due process in deportation cases, finding that DHS had mischaracterized prior court orders in its efforts to remove protections for deportees bound for South Sudan and Djibouti. Separately, Republican-led efforts in several states moved to raise the threshold for ballot initiatives and referendums, narrowing one of the few tools voters have to bypass unresponsive legislatures.

Tuesday brought an escalation on nearly every front. Federal agencies were ordered to sever all ties with Harvard, canceling roughly $100 million in contracts and grants and pushing the funding fight well beyond the original enrollment dispute even as courts kept intervening. The same day, Trump signed his 152nd executive order since returning to office, a pace unmatched by any recent predecessor at this point in a term and a marker of a governing style built on unilateral action rather than legislative negotiation. The pardon pattern continued as well, with clemency for Todd and Julie Chrisley, the reality television couple convicted of defrauding banks of more than $30 million, following advocacy from their daughter. Elsewhere, Boeing admitted in legal filings that its employees had deliberately misled the FAA about risks in the 737 MAX's flight-control system, contributing to two fatal crashes, while reporting also tied the company to close financial and lobbying relationships with the administration. A federal judge delivered one clear check on executive overreach, striking down Trump's order targeting the law firm WilmerHale over its association with Robert Mueller and ruling that the order violated the First Amendment. But that same day, Trump used a Memorial Day social media post to call judges ideologically opposed to the country and accuse them of protecting criminals, extending a running campaign against judicial legitimacy. A flurry of other rulings touched tariffs and DOGE authority, including one requiring Elon Musk to face a lawsuit over the initiative's constitutional footing, reflecting how broadly litigation against the administration had spread.

Wednesday deepened the Harvard confrontation and delivered the week's most consequential legal ruling on tariffs. Backed by a letter from more than 200 college presidents opposing federal overreach, Harvard formally sued the administration and refused to comply with a list of demands tied to its funding and enrollment status. In Congress, the House passed the sprawling "One Big Beautiful" tax-and-spending bill, which extends Trump-era tax cuts, funds mass deportations, and cuts safety-net programs, drawing public backlash even as it moved to the Senate. On tariffs, two federal courts ruled that Trump had exceeded his authority by using emergency powers to impose sweeping duties without congressional approval—a significant assertion that trade policy belongs to Congress. But the win was immediately complicated: the administration's appeal produced a temporary stay, keeping the tariffs in effect despite the ruling against them. Immigration enforcement escalated further as Homeland Security Secretary Kristi Noem and deputy chief of staff Stephen Miller ordered ICE to triple its daily arrest target to 3,000 people, raising concerns about due process and detention capacity given the earlier wrongful detention of a U.S. citizen. In response to the mounting pressure across these fronts, grassroots organizing continued to widen, with new or expanding campaigns including Tesla Takedown, the 50501 movement, a general strike effort, and a boycott campaign aimed at Fox News. Trump also pardoned former Chicago gang leader Larry Hoover, commuting his life sentence, and issued two dozen additional pardons—a contrast critics drew against the administration's harsher stance toward undocumented immigrants without criminal records. Separately, Elon Musk officially ended his formal role atop the Department of Government Efficiency, though Trump indicated he would remain informally involved.

Thursday's developments ranged across the courts and the states. The Supreme Court issued a unanimous 8-0 ruling narrowing environmental review requirements under the National Environmental Policy Act, a decision favoring a Utah oil railway project and, more broadly, fossil fuel infrastructure expansion for years to come. In a very different arena, Idaho and Montana followed Utah's lead in banning LGBTQ+ Pride flags at government buildings, part of a wave of 31 similar bills introduced across 17 states. Twenty-two young plaintiffs, ages 7 to 25, filed suit against the administration's fossil-fuel-promoting executive orders, arguing they violated constitutional rights to life and liberty. The tariff fight took another turn as a federal appeals court granted a temporary stay on the earlier ruling against Trump's tariffs, keeping them in force while the administration pursued further appeals, potentially all the way to the Supreme Court. And in the Harvard case, Judge Burroughs issued an injunction expanding protection for the university's ability to enroll international students, preserving the status quo while litigation continued. In Oklahoma, parents organized under the name "WOKE" used a state opt-out law to resist a new social studies curriculum embedding 2020 election conspiracy theories and Christian nationalist themes, while a separate lawsuit challenged how the curriculum had been approved.

Friday closed the week with some of its most consequential rulings. In a 7-2 decision, the Supreme Court allowed the administration to end humanitarian parole and temporary protected status for hundreds of thousands of Cuban, Haitian, Nicaraguan, and Venezuelan migrants, reversing a lower court's block and exposing many to expedited removal even as related litigation continued. The State Department, meanwhile, notified Congress of plans to build an "Office of Remigration," restructuring the Bureau of Population, Refugees, and Migration around deportation tracking and cutting more than 300 offices and thousands of positions in the process. The Harvard fight escalated again as the administration directed U.S. consulates worldwide to conduct social media screening for all visa applicants tied to the university, citing concerns about antisemitism and extending scrutiny to students, faculty, and other visitors alike. Amid these federal actions, Virginia's legislature advanced a constitutional amendment to codify abortion rights, a measure that must pass again next year before reaching voters. A federal judge in Boston halted a deportation flight to South Sudan after finding the administration had violated a prior order guaranteeing detainees the right to challenge removal to third countries, leaving affected men stranded in Djibouti. And in an unusual coda, an AI-generated voice impersonating White House chief of staff Susie Wiles was used by an unknown actor to contact her associates requesting pardons and cash transfers, prompting an FBI investigation into the security breach.

Taken as a whole, the week illustrated a widening gap between what courts were willing to block and what ultimately stayed in force. Judges ruled against the administration on tariffs, on due process in deportations, on retaliation against a law firm, and repeatedly in Harvard's favor, yet appeals and a receptive Supreme Court preserved much of the underlying policy anyway. Pardons flowed toward the well-connected even as enforcement intensified against migrants with no such access, and a growing infrastructure of grassroots protest and litigation tried to keep pace with the scope of executive action. The week did not resolve any of these tensions; it sharpened them, leaving courts, universities, protesters, and Congress each holding a piece of an unfinished argument over where presidential power ends.
```
