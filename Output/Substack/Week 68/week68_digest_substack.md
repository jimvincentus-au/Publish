# The Week the Voting Rights Act Was Gutted, and States Raced to Cash In

*A Supreme Court ruling triggered a nationwide redistricting scramble, a war's true costs kept surfacing, and journalists became targets*

![Header image](week68-digest-featured.jpg)

```markdown
This was the week the Supreme Court rewrote the practical meaning of the Voting Rights Act, and the country found out within hours what that meant. It was also a week in which a war the administration insisted had ended kept producing casualties, costs, and contradictions; in which federal law enforcement turned repeatedly toward journalists rather than away from them; and in which the human toll of immigration enforcement became harder to look past. Courts pushed back in places, unevenly, even as other institutions bent quickly to new pressure. Taken together, the seven days from May 2 through May 8 showed how fast the machinery of government can move once a single legal restraint is removed.

**The Weekend**

The weekend opened with the Supreme Court's ruling in *Callais v. Louisiana*, which required plaintiffs to prove discriminatory intent rather than discriminatory effect in redistricting cases—a standard that, in practice, eliminated Section 2 of the Voting Rights Act as a workable tool against racial gerrymandering. The response was immediate. Alabama sought expedited review of a map already found unlawful. Indiana moved to dismiss a pending challenge. Courts in Mississippi and Florida reopened settled matters. Louisiana suspended an active congressional primary outright. The ACLU, the NAACP, and Louisiana voters filed suit within the same weekend, but the pace of state action was already outrunning the pace of legal response—a dynamic that would define the entire week.

The same weekend brought a second jolt: the Justice Department, under Acting Attorney General Todd Blanche, indicted former FBI Director James Comey over a deleted Instagram post spelling "86 47" in seashells, a post Comey had already apologized for. The timing—days after a violent incident at the White House Correspondents' Dinner, and alongside a Republican senator's public acknowledgment that the phrase wasn't criminal—fed concerns that federal prosecutorial power was being aimed at a prominent critic rather than a genuine threat.

Trump closed out the weekend with a rapid-fire sequence of social media posts: fabricated images placing himself as a fifth face on Mount Rushmore, a golden self-portrait, a comparison of a reflecting pool's condition to the Obama years—interspersed with a racially charged attack on House Democratic Leader Hakeem Jeffries calling for his impeachment. Separately, the administration argued that the conflict with Iran had "terminated," a claim meant to sidestep War Powers Resolution obligations, even as a naval blockade continued and Trump issued a 24-hour ultimatum threatening major air strikes. Senator Tammy Duckworth publicly disputed the "ended" framing, pointing to ongoing missile exchanges in the Strait of Hormuz. The war's costs were already visible in daily life: Spirit Airlines shut down entirely, eliminating roughly 17,000 jobs and citing fuel costs tied to the conflict; gas prices jumped more than thirty cents in a week; farmers reported being priced out of fertilizer.

**Monday**

The administration's attention turned to a different kind of project—converting a public golf course in Washington into a private course and expanding a "National Garden of American Heroes" monument park, without congressional approval. Democracy Forward sued, and a judge ordered the administration to begin producing internal communications about the plan. The same day, the Supreme Court, through Justice Alito, issued an administrative stay preserving mail and telehealth access to the abortion medication mifepristone after the Fifth Circuit had moved to restrict it—one of several moments this week where a court intervened against an executive or lower-court action, keeping the picture from being one of uniform institutional collapse.

Monday also brought a grimmer pattern into focus: multiple documented cases of medical neglect in ICE detention—an untreated ovarian cyst, appendicitis, a chest lump, chronic cardiovascular disease—alongside reporting that deaths in ICE custody had risen more than fivefold since medical reimbursements were cut. A federal judge, Melissa DuBose, said DHS had endangered her safety by publishing a false claim that she had knowingly released a murder suspect, and indicated she was weighing contempt proceedings. Smaller but telling incidents rounded out the day: a clash between ICE agents and protesters outside a Brooklyn hospital, renewed and baseless claims from Trump that a Texas municipal election had been "rigged," and antisemitic vandalism at synagogues and homes in Queens.

**Tuesday**

Tuesday belonged largely to Tennessee. Governor Bill Lee called a special legislative session aimed squarely at eliminating Memphis's only Democratic, majority-Black congressional district, and Republican leadership rejected Democratic requests for extended public comment or advance disclosure of the new map, compressing what would normally take weeks into days.

The same day, the Justice Department sued 30 states and the District of Columbia to compel release of sensitive voter data, including driver's license and Social Security numbers—part of a broader data campaign that multiple federal courts had already rejected. A U.S. attorney separately subpoenaed personal contact information for Fulton County's 2020 election workers, directing responses to his own office rather than a grand jury; Fulton County moved to quash the subpoena as politically motivated. In Indiana, voters ousted five of seven state senators who had resisted a Trump-aligned push to redraw maps, following a roughly $7 million ad campaign—a result suggesting that primary pressure alone could enforce redistricting compliance. Tom Homan, Trump's border advisor, promised an escalation of ICE deployment into resistant jurisdictions, declaring that "mass deportations are coming." Quietly, a DHS funding bill advanced through reconciliation carrying $1 billion for a White House ballroom project alongside billions for ICE and CBP.

**Wednesday**

The Supreme Court let its own *Callais* ruling take effect ahead of its standard 32-day waiting period, over a sharp dissent from Justice Jackson, clearing the way for Louisiana Republicans to redraw maps before the general election—a procedural acceleration that drew accusations the Court was actively managing the ruling's practical rollout rather than simply issuing it.

The same day, the FBI opened a criminal "insider threat" investigation into leaks made to journalist Sarah Fitzpatrick over her reporting on FBI Director Kash Patel's conduct—an unusual application of that tool to a reporter rather than a classified-information case. Hours later, the FBI raided the office of Virginia Senate President Pro Tempore L. Louise Lucas, an 82-year-old Black legislator who had led the state's redistricting reform efforts; Fox News' apparent advance presence at the scene suggested the raid had been tipped to friendly media beforehand.

Elsewhere, HHS confirmed the FDA had blocked publication of peer-reviewed, taxpayer-funded studies finding certain vaccines safe, and the *Washington Post* reported that the government had pressured satellite imagery firms to withhold damage assessments from the Middle East, forcing journalists to rely on Iranian and European sources to verify the war's toll. Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick gave roughly 400 answers in a closed-door, unsworn session about his continued contact with Jeffrey Epstein after Epstein's 2008 conviction, including a 2012 visit to Epstein's island he called "inexplicable." The Justice Department also asked the Supreme Court to substitute itself as the defendant in E. Jean Carroll's $83.3 million defamation verdict against Trump—a maneuver that would result in dismissal, since the government cannot be sued for defamation—while DOJ closed San Francisco's major immigration court, relocating 120,000 pending cases to an already understaffed facility.

**Thursday**

Thursday brought the week's clearest and fastest-moving demonstration of what the *Callais* ruling had unleashed. Tennessee's Republican supermajority repealed a 1972 law banning mid-decade redistricting, passed a new map splitting Memphis across three Republican-leaning districts, and Governor Lee signed it into law the same day. State troopers cleared the House gallery of protesters, and Representative Justin Jones burned a Confederate flag in the Capitol rotunda in protest. The NAACP's Tennessee State Conference filed an emergency lawsuit within three hours of the bill's signing. Alabama's attorney general moved the same day to reinstate a map already found racially gerrymandered, while the governor urged defiance of a federal injunction—part of a wave that already included Ohio, Missouri, North Carolina, Texas, and Florida, with South Carolina and Louisiana still in progress.

Separately, the Southern Poverty Law Center was indicted on fraud charges that legal experts called weak, tied to standard informant-payment practices used in monitoring extremist groups. The human costs of immigration enforcement sharpened further: a federal judge ordered the release of the detained parents of an 18-year-old U.S. citizen with terminal cancer, who died a day after their reunion in Mexico; ProPublica documented at least 79 children injured by tear gas and pepper spray during enforcement operations; and reporting showed a 50 percent rise in children entering foster care due to parental detention.

On the press-freedom front, Kash Patel filed a $250 million lawsuit against *The Atlantic* and reporter Sarah Fitzpatrick, while the FBI separately investigated a *New York Times* reporter over coverage of his girlfriend's security detail, and Patel ordered polygraph tests of more than two dozen staff to find leakers. A confidential CIA assessment, meanwhile, found that Iran could sustain the ongoing blockade for three to four months and retained roughly 75 percent of its missile launchers—directly contradicting public claims by Trump and Defense Secretary Hegseth of an imminent Iranian collapse. Gas prices hit $4.56 a gallon, with 81 percent of Americans reporting financial strain, even as Shell posted a 24 percent jump in profits and Whirlpool described a "recession-level industry decline." Trump also publicly attacked Pope Leo XIV as "WEAK on Crime" over the pope's criticism of U.S. Iran and immigration policy.

**Friday**

Friday's rulings cut in different directions. Virginia's Supreme Court, in a 4–3 partisan decision, struck down a redistricting map nearly 3 million Virginians had approved by referendum just weeks earlier to counter Republican gerrymandering elsewhere, ruling on procedural grounds that the amendment process had begun before early voting concluded; the decision restored maps favoring Republicans, and Trump celebrated it as a "huge win." The same day, the Court of International Trade ruled Trump's global "Liberation Day" tariffs illegal, ordering a permanent injunction and refunds with interest—even as Trump simultaneously threatened "much higher" tariffs against the European Union if a trade deal wasn't reached by July 4.

Vice President Vance said the administration would threaten to withhold federal health funding from states resisting fraud-audit compliance, while CMS froze new hospice and home-health enrollments for six months, despite legal experts noting HHS lacks authority to withhold Medicaid funds broadly. Courts also pushed back elsewhere: a federal judge blocked sanctions against a UN rapporteur for criticizing Israel on First Amendment grounds, South Carolina's Supreme Court overturned Alex Murdaugh's convictions for jury interference, and a pardoned January 6 rioter was sentenced to prison for an unrelated burglary.

The week's press-freedom thread continued when Trump, during a Lincoln Memorial inspection of reflecting-pool repairs, called ABC reporter Rachel Scott's question about gas prices "stupid" and used a derogatory slur to describe her to Interior Secretary Doug Burgum, who laughed. A 22-foot golden Trump statue was unveiled at a Miami golf course amid a cryptocurrency financing dispute, a Gold Star mothers' luncheon was held at a Mar-a-Lago-style White House replica, and contaminated East Wing demolition debris was dumped at a public golf course without environmental review. Consumer sentiment, meanwhile, hit a record low of 48.2, and the administration projected a $2.1 trillion deficit for the fiscal year. California, separately, reached a $12.75 million settlement with General Motors over illegal sale of drivers' location data and pursued enforcement against State Farm over wildfire insurance claims—a reminder that some forms of institutional accountability continued regardless of federal dynamics.

By the end of the week, the throughlines were unmistakable. A single Supreme Court ruling had, within days, been converted by state legislatures into the elimination of decades-old minority representation, executed faster than courts, advocacy groups, or the public could respond in kind. A war described as over kept generating casualties, costs, and intelligence contradictions that officials struggled to reconcile with their own public statements. Federal law enforcement, meanwhile, turned its investigative and legal tools not only toward political opponents engaged in lawful voting-rights work but toward the journalists covering the officials who wielded that power. And running beneath all of it was a steady accumulation of harm to ordinary people—detainees denied medical care, children exposed to chemical agents, families separated, consumers absorbing rising costs while corporate profits climbed—that did not make headlines on their own but, taken together, described the week's real cost. Courts intervened in places, unevenly, preserving some rights and vacating others; legislatures moved with a speed that outpaced deliberation; and the public record of the week stands less as a single event than as a demonstration of how quickly the removal of one legal constraint can cascade through a system built to depend on it.
```
