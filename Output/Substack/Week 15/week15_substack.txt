The week did not open with a single rupture. It gathered instead, in small and repeated acts, until the pattern became unmistakable. A judge arrested. A university sued. A club opened for sale. A curriculum rewritten. Each item alone might pass as ordinary governance. Together they described something else: a government using its full weight to reward loyalty, punish resistance, and quietly widen the space in which it could act without answering to anyone.

At the close of Week 14, the Democracy Clock stood at 7:40 p.m. It closed Week 15 at the same time. The internal ledger moved just 0.3 minutes, too little for the public face to shift. Nothing this week broke sharply from what came before. The same machinery kept turning, in the same direction, at roughly the same pace. Courts still checked some abuses. Congress still declined to check most others. What shifted was less the rate of erosion than its breadth. Nearly every domain of governance, from immigration to media to civil service to campus life, absorbed a small new dose of the same medicine.

Immigration enforcement this week moved past the border and into homes, schools, and mailboxes. Families were deported to Honduras with citizen children in tow, often with little access to lawyers. ICE targeted unaccompanied children and the relatives who sponsored them. An Irish green-card holder was detained over an old record. A Justice Department order pushed migrants into a newly declared military buffer zone, where crossing became a matter for military-adjacent prosecution rather than ordinary immigration law. The president himself dismissed due process as an obstacle to speed.

This reach did not stop at noncitizens. The Postal Inspection Service began feeding mail data to ICE, a quiet expansion of surveillance into ordinary correspondence. In Oklahoma City, agents raided the wrong house, a citizen family's home, and left with their belongings. Above all of it hung a new executive order aimed at sanctuary jurisdictions, threatening federal funds against cities and states that declined to cooperate. Immigration had become the proving ground for a broader claim: federal power, it now argued, could reach into local governance, family life, and even the mail, whenever the executive judged it useful.

Courts remained the most reliable brake on this expansion, and the record shows real friction. A federal judge in Texas permanently blocked use of the Alien Enemies Act against a covered class of Venezuelans. Another enjoined Border Patrol from suspicionless stops. A summary judgment favored Perkins Coie against a retaliatory executive order, and an appeals court restored protections against DOGE access to Social Security data. Justice Ketanji Brown Jackson publicly rebuked attacks on judicial independence as a threat to the rule of law itself.

That rebuke was necessary because the attacks were not abstract. The FBI arrested a sitting Milwaukee County judge on obstruction charges tied to an immigration case. A member of Congress, Clay Higgins, called for more judges to be arrested. The president used a rally to denounce judges who ruled against him. Even as courts kept blocking the administration's furthest reaches, the administration kept working to make judges afraid to rule that way again. Two currents ran side by side: rulings that held, and rhetoric built to make future rulings harder to sustain.

Universities absorbed a similar squeeze from two directions at once. From Washington, executive orders targeted campus diversity programs and accreditation; Harvard faced funding threats serious enough to prompt a lawsuit, and by week's end the administration moved to strip its tax-exempt status outright. A separate investigation opened into Harvard Law Review over alleged racial discrimination. From within, campuses turned on their own protesters. Swarthmore suspended nine students tied to a pro-Palestinian encampment. Yale revoked recognition of a student group. FBI agents raided the homes of activists in Michigan. The pressure came from both directions at once, and universities showed themselves willing partners in narrowing the space available for dissent.

Independent journalism faced its own version of this two-front assault: constrain the old channels, build new loyal ones. The Associated Press lost White House and pool access. The Justice Department rescinded protections that had limited subpoenas against reporters, a change that hands the state more leverage over sources and leaks. NPR and PBS lost federal funding by executive order, even as the FCC opened investigations into both. Radio Free Europe and similar broadcasters had their funding contested in court, restored only through judicial order. Meanwhile the White House launched its own outlet, "White House Wire," built to publish favorable coverage on official servers, and held separate briefings for friendly influencers. The goal was not simply to silence critics. It was to replace them.

Access to power itself became strikingly explicit this week, more so than mere lobbying or donor privilege. Donald Trump Jr. opened a private club, Executive Branch, charging half a million dollars for proximity to cabinet officials. The president offered dinners and White House access to the largest holders of his personal $TRUMP token, prompting senators to demand an ethics inquiry. At the same moment, the administration quietly halted investigations and lawsuits against eighty-nine corporations, many tied to inaugural donations, and the Justice Department paused Foreign Corrupt Practices Act prosecutions while closing its cryptocurrency enforcement unit. Access was for sale, and enforcement against the wealthy and connected was retreating at nearly the same hour.

Beneath these headline moments, the machinery of the civil service kept being remade into something more pliable. DOGE personnel gained classified-network access and worked under nondisclosure agreements on FAA communications tied to Starlink, a Musk-linked interest. The Peace Corps was restructured after a DOGE review. Nearly four hundred contributors to the National Climate Assessment were dismissed outright. The Justice Department's voting rights section lost its leaders, and its Civil Rights Division lost most of its lawyers through departure and reassignment. None of this required new legislation. It required only reassignment, attrition, and the quiet redirection of institutional purpose away from independence and toward obedience.

Congress, for its part, mostly declined to resist any of it. House Republicans changed floor rules specifically to block a resolution of inquiry into Defense Secretary Hegseth's use of Signal. Senate and House Republicans alike blocked efforts to challenge the president's tariff emergency, leaving trade policy almost entirely in executive hands. Five bills became law during the first hundred days, a thin legislative output that left the administration's larger agenda moving instead through executive order and litigation. Where opposition appeared, it was often symbolic: a twelve-hour sit-in by Hakeem Jeffries and Cory Booker, marathon floor speeches planned by Senate Democrats, an arrest of Bishop William Barber during a Capitol protest. These were genuine acts of dissent. They did not change outcomes.

Public memory and civic identity came under a more openly ideological hand this week as well. The Trump administration fired Biden appointees from the U.S. Holocaust Memorial Museum board, including Doug Emhoff. In Oklahoma, state education officials advanced social-studies standards embedding debunked claims about the 2020 election alongside stronger Christian framing, pushed through by a process critics called deliberately rushed. The president established a new Religious Liberty Commission and hosted a National Prayer Day event built around his own political persona. Each act, alone, might be dismissed as culture-war noise. Together they described an effort to place the state's thumb more firmly on what children are taught and which religious identity carries official favor.

Even the language of accountability began to blur under the week's pressure. A cabinet meeting was used to repeat disputed claims about migrant children and the economy, delivered from a setting citizens are trained to trust. The president blamed his predecessor for a downturn his own tariffs had helped produce. DOGE announced spending cuts whose independently projected savings turned out to be far smaller than claimed. None of these were dramatic lies in the classic sense. They were the ordinary distortions of a government grown comfortable using its platforms for persuasion rather than disclosure.

Against all of this, resistance persisted, and it is worth naming plainly. Maine's frozen child-nutrition funds were restored through settlement. A federal court forced restoration of Radio Free Europe's appropriated funds. The CFPB's staffing survived an appeals court's intervention. These were not trivial wins; they showed that where legal process still had teeth, it could still bite. But the broader pattern of the week was one in which such wins arrived as exceptions inside a widening field of executive assertion, not as the rule.

No scheduled ruling or dated event in this week's record demands a forward-looking note beyond what has already been described. Litigation over the Alien Enemies Act, TPS revocation, and the sanctuary-jurisdiction order all remain live, their outcomes still to be written.

Seen whole, Week 15 did not mark a turn in the story of this term so much as a widening of its base. The instruments were already familiar: emergency powers stretched past their intent, agencies hollowed from within, courts pressured even as they held, media squeezed while loyal alternatives rose in its place. What distinguished this week was how many domains absorbed the same treatment at once, and how openly the exchange of access for loyalty was conducted, in a memecoin dinner, in a half-million-dollar club, in dropped investigations timed to donor rolls. The clock's stillness this week does not mean nothing happened. It means the erosion had grown steady enough to no longer need acceleration. The ordinary work of government and the extraordinary concentration of power had begun to look, day to day, very much the same.
