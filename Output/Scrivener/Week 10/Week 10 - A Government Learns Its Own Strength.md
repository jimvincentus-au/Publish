Week 10: A Government Learns Its Own Strength
Law firms, judges, universities, and federal workers face coordinated pressure while the clock barely moves, hiding a week of broad erosion.
Week 10 opens with a government learning its own strength. Where earlier weeks tested boundaries one at a time, this week tested several at once. Law firms, judges, universities, students, federal workers, and the historical record itself all came under pressure in the same seven days. No single act defined the period. Its meaning came from the accumulation.
At the close of Week 9, the Democracy Clock stood at 7:33 p.m. It closes Week 10 at the same public time, though the underlying measure moved forward by a sliver, roughly a fifth of a minute. The clock barely stirred, and this stillness conceals more than it shows. Nearly every trait tracked this period moved toward erosion. The public reading held steady only because the movement, though broad, came in small steps rather than one great rupture. This was a week of consolidation, not collapse. It was, in its way, more instructive for being so.
The clearest thread running through the week was law used as a tool rather than a limit. On March 22, the White House issued executive orders targeting four major law firms—Perkins Coie, Covington & Burling, Jenner & Block, and WilmerHale—for representing the president's opponents. Two firms, Paul Weiss and Skadden, chose settlement, agreeing to pro-bono commitments and policy concessions to avoid the government's wrath. Trump then warned other firms to "behave themselves," making explicit what the orders had implied. But not every firm folded. Jenner & Block and WilmerHale sued, arguing the orders were retaliatory and unconstitutional. The split matters: it shows coercion works against firms without the resources or resolve to fight, but fails, at least in court, against those willing to try. Power flowed to the executive wherever firms gave way. It was contested wherever they didn't.
This was not an isolated tactic. Sixteen political opponents had their security clearances revoked by memorandum, with no stated legal process. Devon Archer, a convicted fraudster whose testimony had served administration interests, received a full pardon. Days later, Carlos Watson and Trevor Milton—the latter a two-million-dollar campaign donor—received clemency as well. Pardon power moved toward those who had been useful or generous, not toward those wronged by the system. Two traits tracking this pattern moved sharply upward: law as weapon, and corruption investigations blocked or retaliated against. The pattern was consistent, if diffuse, rather than one dramatic act.
The judiciary faced open hostility from multiple directions. Judge James Boasberg opened an inquiry into whether the administration had violated his order halting deportation flights, an inquiry made necessary because deportations had proceeded regardless. Trump responded not with compliance but with calls for the judge's impeachment. Representative Van Orden filed an impeachment resolution against a different judge who had ruled against DOGE's access to Treasury records. House Speaker Mike Johnson went further, floating the elimination of federal district courts altogether. Secretary Hegseth mocked a judge who had blocked the administration's transgender military ban, suggesting on social media that she report for duty. Chief Justice Roberts, in a rare public statement, rejected impeachment as a response to unfavorable rulings—a quiet but pointed rebuke. The judiciary held its ground this week, but it held that ground under direct and repeated attack, from the presidency, from Congress, and from the cabinet, all at once.
Deportation policy proceeded on similarly strained footing. The administration removed 137 Venezuelan migrants under the Alien Enemies Act despite Boasberg's order to halt the flights. Errors surfaced quickly: women sent to an all-male facility, a Nicaraguan migrant misidentified as Venezuelan. These were not isolated clerical slips; they pointed to a program running with almost no safeguards for identity or gender placement. Compounding the unease, Trump publicly denied signing the underlying proclamation, despite his own signature appearing in the Federal Register. The denial raises a question that outlasts the week itself: who actually controls major acts of executive power, and can accountability attach to a president who disclaims his own documented decisions.
Immigration enforcement turned, this week, toward political speech specifically. ICE detained Columbia student Yunseo Chung, a green-card holder, after she joined pro-Palestinian protests; a court blocked her detention. Tufts doctoral student Rumeysa Ozturk was detained for co-writing an op-ed on Palestinian rights and moved across state lines in apparent violation of a court's notice requirement. A University of Alabama student, Alireza Doroudi, was detained without stated cause. The State Department, in parallel, issued a directive mandating social media vetting of visa applicants for perceived support of "terrorist activity." Each case, alone, might read as an enforcement irregularity. Together, they describe a policy: political speech, voiced by a noncitizen, becomes grounds for removal. Universities responded to related funding pressure by narrowing their own protections. Columbia overhauled its protest rules and hired an internal security force after the administration withheld $400 million in federal funds. The University of Michigan closed its diversity program under similar pressure. Neither university needed to be ordered outright—the threat of lost funding did the work.
Protest around Tesla dealerships drew its own distinct response. Peaceful demonstrators organizing under the banner "Tesla Takedown" prompted federal domestic-terrorism charges from the FBI and Attorney General Bondi, who separately warned Representative Jasmine Crockett to "tread carefully" after she voiced support for the protests. Trump floated twenty-year prison sentences and suggested Tesla vandals be imprisoned in El Salvador, rhetoric striking given his own pardons of violent January 6 defendants. Elon Musk publicly accused protest organizer Valerie Costa of crimes before his 220 million followers, triggering harassment against her. Separately, Trump supporters drove a vehicle into protesters and used a taser against another—acts of private violence in a climate the state's own words had helped shape. Property protest was framed as terrorism. Physical violence against protesters drew little official concern by comparison.
The week's most consequential structural act may have been the executive order stripping collective bargaining rights from federal workers across more than thirty agencies, over a million employees affected in a single stroke. This was paired with an announced 10,000 to 20,000 job cuts at Health and Human Services, roughly 11,000 terminations at the IRS, plans to eliminate FEMA, and notice to Congress that USAID would fully shut down by July 1. None of these actions required new legislation. Each proceeded through executive reorganization, testing whether the president can dissolve agencies Congress created. Peter Marks, the FDA's top vaccine regulator, was forced out for refusing to endorse misinformation during an active measles outbreak—a single personnel decision whose consequences for public health will outlast the week itself. Taken together, this cluster is not incremental erosion. It is active dismantling of institutional capacity: tax enforcement, disaster response, foreign aid, and disease surveillance all diminished in the same seven days.
Private wealth kept its grip on state authority. Elon Musk spent over twenty million dollars on a single Wisconsin Supreme Court race, offering cash payments and bonuses tied to petition signatures, with explicit reference to controlling redistricting through 2041. SpaceX, meanwhile, received expanded federal contracts across Defense, Commerce, and other agencies. DOGE, Musk's cost-cutting initiative, ran unauthorized data searches at the Social Security Administration despite a federal restraining order limiting such access; the agency then named a former DOGE staffer as Chief Information Officer one day after certifying compliance with that same order. Wisconsin's attorney general sued to block Musk's cash payments to voters, a rare instance of state authority pushing back against this merger of private fortune and government reach. The lawsuit did not settle the underlying question, but it did establish that someone, at least, was asking it.
A parallel campaign unfolded over public memory. An executive order directed the Smithsonian to strip out "improper, divisive, or anti-American ideology" and ordered the restoration of Confederate monuments removed in the past five years. Federal websites quietly deleted pages documenting the histories of Black veterans, transgender civil rights figures, and pioneering women, some restored only after public pressure exposed the deletions. A list of over two hundred words, including "racism" and "women," was flagged for removal from government websites and school curricula. This was not censorship by omission; it was censorship by directive, aimed at the historical record itself, with the vice president handed curatorial authority over federal museums nationwide.
Against this backdrop, the Signal chat scandal exposed a different failure: not deliberate control of information, but reckless indifference to it. Senior national security officials, including Secretary Hegseth, discussed classified Yemen strike plans on an unsecured commercial messaging app that inadvertently included a journalist. Officials then denied that classified information had been shared, a claim later contradicted by published transcripts. Rather than address the breach, Hegseth and National Security Adviser Waltz turned on the journalist, Jeffrey Goldberg, calling him "discredited" and worse. A later report found officials' personal contact credentials, including those tied to the Signal chat, exposed online—a second failure layered atop the first. Deputy Chief of Staff Stephen Miller appeared to authorize a military strike without documented presidential approval, a chain-of-command question that remains unresolved. Here the pattern was not concealment through erasure, as with the Smithsonian order, but concealment through denial and deflection, blame redirected toward the person who exposed the failure.
A new voting-rights order added a further structural claim. Trump signed an executive order requiring documentary proof of citizenship for federal voter registration and conditioning federal election funding on state compliance. Legal scholars and the Democratic National Committee challenged the order at once, noting that it claimed presidential authority over ground the Constitution reserves to states and Congress. The order rested on a disputed factual claim about noncitizen voting. Its practical effect, if carried out, would fall on eligible citizens who lack ready access to citizenship documents, a burden that tends to concentrate among the poor, the elderly, and the transient.
Not every development this week ran one direction. Federal judges blocked enforcement of the transgender military ban and required hearings before further Alien Enemies Act removals. Jenner & Block and WilmerHale chose litigation over settlement. Two fired FTC commissioners sued over their removal, arguing it violated statutory protections. A watchdog group sued over apparent Signal recordkeeping violations. None of this reversed the week's structural drift, but together, these moves show that the channels for contesting executive power—courts, lawsuits, formal complaint—had not yet closed, even as pressure against them grew sharper from several directions at once.
Public resistance also persisted in visible, organized form. Rallies led by Bernie Sanders and Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez drew roughly 86,000 people across three states. One in five Americans, according to polling, planned permanent boycotts of companies aligned with the administration's agenda. Tesla dealership protests continued nationwide despite the threats leveled at their organizers. None of this changed the week's policy outcomes, but it did show that public mobilization remained active, even as the state's response to it grew sharper.
Several pending matters carry directly into the weeks ahead. The lawsuits filed by Jenner & Block and WilmerHale remain unresolved, as do those filed by the fired FTC commissioners. Judge Boasberg's inquiry into deportation-flight noncompliance continues, alongside the requirement that hearings precede further Alien Enemies Act removals. The voter-registration order faces active legal challenge. Congress has not yet acted on proposed legislation to strip courts of nationwide injunctive power, though House Republicans have signaled intent to pursue it.
Taken as a whole, Week 10 marks less a single crisis than a widening of terrain already claimed. The tactics visible here—executive orders against private firms, funding threats against universities and states, impeachment calls against judges, erasure of inconvenient history—were not new inventions. They were refinements of methods tested in earlier weeks, applied now with more confidence and less hesitation. Institutional resistance persisted, in courts, in lawsuits, in a Chief Justice's public words. But persistence is not reversal. The clock's still face hides the breadth of what moved beneath it. Erosion, this week, did not arrive as a single blow. It arrived as a pattern, repeated until it began to look ordinary.
