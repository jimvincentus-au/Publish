The Week Sedition Talk Met the Courts
A president called for lawmakers' execution, the DOJ's case against Comey and James collapsed, Georgia's election case died, and a Russian-drafted peace plan leaked into public view
```markdown
The week of November 22–28 opened with a presidential order to prosecute named political enemies and closed with courts blocking gerrymandered maps in two states, but what happened in between tested, in concrete terms, how far executive power could reach into the machinery of dissent, law enforcement, and the electoral map itself. By the time the holiday weekend ended, a president had called publicly for the execution of sitting senators, the Justice Department's marquee prosecutions had collapsed in court, and a leaked transcript had shown an American envoy coaching Russian officials on how to manage the president himself.
**The Weekend**
The weekend began with Trump posting a direct order to Attorney General Pam Bondi demanding prosecution of James Comey, Letitia James, and Adam Schiff — an extraordinary breach of the norm that the Justice Department operates independent of the president's personal grievances. The same weekend, the DOJ indicted former national security adviser John Bolton on national defense information charges, with prosecutors suggesting more charges could follow. Separately, after nearly 100 House Republicans prepared to defy Trump and vote to release the Epstein files, he abruptly reversed his opposition and called for their release himself — a concession friendly media recast as strategy, but one that nonetheless exposed real limits on his control over his own party.
Then the rhetoric sharpened into something more dangerous. After six Democratic veteran-lawmakers released a video reminding U.S. troops that they are not obligated to follow unlawful orders, Trump branded their conduct "seditious behavior" punishable by death, repeating the threat in multiple posts across the weekend and naming Senator Mark Kelly specifically. Kelly noted that the threat carried a particular edge, given his wife Gabby Giffords' history as a survivor of political violence. It was, by any plain reading, a sitting president calling for the execution of sitting members of Congress over protected speech.
That threat did not stay rhetorical for long, and other fronts moved in parallel. Immigration enforcement expanded with its own friction: ICE deported a college student in apparent defiance of a federal court's emergency order, removed a trans woman in violation of a standing torture-risk order, raided a South Korean-owned factory in Georgia in an episode serious enough to prompt a diplomatic apology, and DHS began preparing a large-scale operation aimed at New Orleans. A new X transparency feature revealed that numerous high-follower "patriotic American" accounts were actually run from Russia, Nigeria, and India, exposing a layer of foreign-operated infrastructure inside domestic political discourse. Reporting on Trump's own finances added a quieter thread: a prospective Trump Organization deal in Saudi Arabia, more than $80 million in personal bond purchases tied to policy-favored sectors, and a presidential library foundation that had raised $50 million, largely from litigation settlements with media companies.
**Monday**
Courts began to answer the weekend's opening salvo. Judge Cameron McGowan Currie dismissed the indictments against Comey and James outright, ruling that Trump's appointment of Lindsey Halligan as interim U.S. attorney violated federal law and the Constitution's Appointments Clause — a direct judicial rebuke of an attempt to install a loyalist prosecutor around the Senate confirmation process. The DOJ vowed to appeal, and Trump allies moved quickly to blame Bondi rather than the underlying legal defect. That same day, the president's execution rhetoric acquired an institutional arm: Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth announced a Pentagon investigation into Senator Kelly for "potentially unlawful conduct," with officials signaling he could be recalled to active duty and face court-martial — a use of military disciplinary machinery against a sitting senator over a video reiterating settled law.
Monday also brought reporting that quantified something less visible than a single ruling or threat: roughly 5,500 departures from the Justice Department under Bondi, with the civil rights division alone losing 70 percent of its staff, as investigative priorities shifted away from corruption and voting rights and toward the president's political targets. Elsewhere, multiple House Republicans reportedly told journalists they were weighing resignation over White House treatment and Speaker Johnson's failure to defend the chamber's independence — even as Trump insisted publicly that the party had "never been so united." In a notable break from months of threats to withhold federal funding from New York, Trump held a cordial Oval Office meeting with Mayor-elect Zohran Mamdani, reversing his posture without explanation and drawing backlash from his own base. The administration also ordered a review of roughly 233,000 Biden-era refugees and ended Temporary Protected Status for nearly 4,000 Myanmar nationals fleeing an active civil war.
**Tuesday**
The Kelly investigation widened further: the FBI's counterterrorism division contacted House and Senate sergeants-at-arms seeking interviews with all six lawmakers behind the military-obedience video, which the lawmakers characterized as intimidation directed from the top. In three days, a video restating existing military law had moved from presidential threat, to Pentagon inquiry, to a federal counterterrorism investigation of sitting members of Congress. Congress held its own oversight hearing the same day, with Democrats documenting alleged civil rights abuses in ICE's Los Angeles operations — citizens arrested without cause, allegations of racial profiling — and announcing a new dashboard to track verified misconduct. Federal courts, meanwhile, pushed back on parts of the enforcement apparatus: one judge blocked DHS's elimination of bond hearings for certain immigrants, another required warrants and documented cause for arrests in Colorado, and detained immigrants filed a contempt motion against DHS and Secretary Kristi Noem over detention conditions.
The most consequential story of the week's middle days, though, arrived through a leak. Bloomberg published transcripts showing special envoy Steve Witkoff advising a senior Russian official on how to flatter and persuade Trump into accepting a Russian-drafted peace framework involving Ukrainian territorial concessions. A companion leak showed the White House pushing false claims to friendly media that a "peace deal" had already been finalized. This was a distinct and more serious matter than the general confusion swirling around who had actually authored the peace proposal — it was documented evidence of an American envoy coordinating messaging with a foreign adversary's government to manage the president's own decision-making.
The same day, corporate America's divisions became visible: the "We Ain't Buying It" boycott campaign launched a five-day action against Amazon, Target, and Home Depot timed to Black Friday, while shareholders at Apple and Costco rejected anti-DEI proposals by wide margins even as McDonald's, Meta, Target, and Walmart rolled back their diversity programs — two distinct corporate responses to the same political pressure. Separately, Rep. Eric Swalwell sued FHFA Director Bill Pulte over alleged unauthorized access to political opponents' mortgage records, and twenty states plus Washington, D.C. sued to block HUD changes to homelessness funding targeting organizations that serve transgender people.
**Wednesday**
Wednesday brought the collapse of the last remaining criminal accountability mechanism for the 2020 election. Fulton County prosecutor Pete Skandalakis dismissed the Georgia racketeering case against Trump and his co-defendants, citing the case's complexity and estimating that a trial could not occur before 2029. This was a different kind of ending than the Comey and James dismissals — not a ruling that the prosecution had been unlawfully structured, but a discretionary judgment that the case could no longer proceed. Either way, no criminal case tied to efforts to overturn the 2020 election now remained active anywhere in the country.
The same day, the 11th Circuit affirmed a $1 million sanction against Trump and attorney Alina Habba over a meritless racketeering suit against Hillary Clinton, the DNC, and James Comey, finding a documented "pattern" of abusive litigation. Trump also opened a new front, launching a nationwide push for mid-decade congressional redistricting and threatening primary challenges against Indiana state senators resisting the plan; one unnamed legislator told reporters he feared violence for speaking out. In Georgia, the DOJ pursued demands for Fulton County election records tied to unfounded 2020 fraud claims while the state Republican Party unveiled plans to eliminate no-excuse absentee voting, automatic registration, and drop boxes ahead of 2026. Older news resurfaced too: FCC Chair Brendan Carr's earlier pressure on Disney to suspend Jimmy Kimmel over comments about Charlie Kirk's murder, and a pattern of gendered attacks on female journalists, including Trump calling a New York Times reporter "ugly, both inside and out" after coverage of his declining stamina.
**Thursday**
Thanksgiving Day carried the week's most consequential legal story into deeper context: extensive reporting traced the history of Section 2 of the Voting Rights Act, from its 1982 amendment through Shelby County and Milligan, to the Supreme Court's October arguments in Callais v. Louisiana, where a "temporal argument" now questions whether majority-minority districts remain constitutionally permissible at all. Successful Section 2 cases from Alabama, Mississippi, North Dakota, Tennessee, and Washington illustrated what could be lost if the provision is narrowed or struck down — the primary remaining federal tool against racially discriminatory voting maps. A related thread showed the Supreme Court's earlier order permitting ethnicity as a "relevant factor" in immigration stops feeding a wave of aggressive detentions of Hispanic Americans, some of whom had already proven citizenship. A federal appeals court, by contrast, blocked the administration from expanding rapid deportation authority to immigrants believed to have lived in the U.S. for under two years, finding the expansion would violate due process.
Other reporting documented over 600 children placed in federal immigration shelters in 2025 — more than the prior four years combined — along with spouses of citizens arrested during immigration interviews and a North Carolina sweep that caught mostly non-criminal individuals, while ICE activity in Chicago disrupted FEMA flood relief work. Late Thanksgiving night, Trump posted a message using a slur against Governor Tim Walz, falsely blamed Biden for Afghanistan evacuation vetting failures despite his own administration's record, and threatened to denaturalize and deport immigrants deemed incompatible with "Western Civilization." Separately, the White House finalized a deal with Northwestern University requiring a $75 million fine and an overhaul of campus demonstration policies in exchange for restored federal funding and the closure of a federal investigation.
**Friday**
Friday arrived with the week's most sweeping single-day expansion of enforcement power, triggered by a shooting near the White House committed by an Afghan national. USCIS halted all Afghan immigration processing and then suspended all asylum decisions nationwide; the State Department suspended Afghan visa issuance; DHS expanded review to cover all Biden-era asylum approvals; and the administration reversed its policy barring ICE arrests inside churches. Separately, and with no direct connection to the shooting, DHS terminated Temporary Protected Status for Haiti amid an active humanitarian crisis there. Collectively, these were measures applied broadly in response to the actions of one individual.
The same day, Trump announced he would pardon former Honduran President Juan Orlando Hernández, convicted of running a large-scale cocaine-trafficking conspiracy into the United States, calling his 45-year sentence "harsh and unfair" despite prosecutors having described him as operator of a "cocaine superhighway." Trump also declared he would void roughly 92 percent of Biden's executive orders based on unsubstantiated claims that Biden had not personally authorized an autopen signature process, and threatened Biden with perjury charges if he disputed it — notwithstanding Trump's own use of an autopen.
Against this, courts intervened again: a federal panel blocked Texas from using its newly gerrymandered congressional map in 2026, finding it likely an illegal racial gerrymander, while a Utah judge separately struck down that state's map for violating a voter-approved anti-gerrymandering amendment — two distinct rulings on separate legal grounds, both curbing the same nationwide redistricting push that had produced threats against Indiana legislators days earlier. The White House also launched an official "Media Bias Tracker," publicly naming the Washington Post, CBS News, and CNN in an "Offender Hall of Shame," continuing a week of gendered attacks on female reporters. And as the holiday weekend peaked, the boycott campaign shifted an estimated $109 billion in spending from major retailers to small businesses, Starbucks Workers United escalated its strike to roughly 2,500 baristas across 120 stores during the height of the season, and Costco sued the administration over tariff authority.
**Assessment**
Read end to end, the week showed two forces moving at once rather than one overwhelming the other. A president called for the execution of lawmakers, and within days the Pentagon and FBI turned that rhetoric into open investigations of a sitting senator and his colleagues. A special envoy was shown coaching a foreign adversary on how to manage the American president. Mass reviews and suspensions swept across entire nationalities and humanitarian statuses in response to a single violent act. Yet the same week saw a federal judge dismiss politically motivated indictments for violating the Constitution's own appointment procedures, appellate courts block gerrymandered maps in two states, another court sanction the president for abusive litigation, and a Georgia prosecutor close the last remaining criminal case tied to the effort to overturn a presidential election — not through vindication, but through the ordinary friction of complexity and time. Neither the executive's reach nor the judiciary's resistance fully prevailed; each constrained the other, unevenly, while corporate boardrooms split over diversity commitments, consumers organized boycotts measured in the billions, and reports of quiet fracture inside the president's own party surfaced alongside his loudest public claims of unity. It was a week in which the distance between what a president says and what institutions allow narrowed in some places and widened dangerously in others.
```
