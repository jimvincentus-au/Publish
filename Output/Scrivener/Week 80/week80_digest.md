A History Rewritten, a Cabinet Stalled, and a War Without a Clock
In one week: the Smithsonian came under direct siege, tariffs were reimposed by decree three times over, a war powers vote failed again, and Senate Republicans balked at an attorney general nominee tied to a personal immunity deal
```markdown
This week, spanning July 25 through July 31, brought together four distinct but overlapping stories: a sustained push to reshape how American history is preserved and told, a cabinet confirmation that collapsed over the president's personal financial protections, a war-powers debate that Congress lost for the second time in two weeks, and a false story about a New York City mayor that spread through major news outlets faster than anyone corrected it. Underneath these headline fights, quieter developments — a State Department map with every African country misplaced, a $22 million order for luxury vehicles, a 19-year high in Treasury yields — sketched the texture of an administration operating at full speed across many fronts at once.
The weekend opened with a coordinated set of actions touching historical memory. A Trump-appointed preservation board voted to weaken public and tribal review requirements for federal projects at historic sites, clearing the way for a disputed triumphal arch near Arlington and a mineral-extraction effort at Chaco Canyon. At nearly the same time, the administration ordered warning signs installed at the Smithsonian's National Museum of American History and began repainting the Eisenhower Executive Office Building without the customary review process. Individually, each move might have passed as a bureaucratic footnote. Together, they signaled an effort to narrow outside input — from historians, tribal nations, and Congress — over how the government tells the story of the country's past.
The same weekend produced a second test of institutional limits: three days after the Supreme Court struck down the administration's emergency tariffs as unconstitutional, Trump reimposed them, first under Section 122 of trade law and then under Section 301 — authorities that do not require congressional approval. Legal experts noted that these statutes, unlike the one the Court had just voided, could make the tariffs effectively permanent. It was the third attempt in short order to keep the same policy alive through a different legal door, raising a basic question about whether a Supreme Court ruling could meaningfully constrain the executive's approach to trade.
A third weekend story began quietly and would grow for days. The New York Post published a front-page claim that thousands of 9/11 victims' families wanted New York Mayor Mamdani barred from a memorial ceremony, sourced to an online petition. The petition's own organizer later admitted the family-member count was an unsubstantiated guess. Also over the weekend, four House Republicans crossed party lines to support a symbolic resolution demanding troop withdrawal from Iran — a day after backing a $95 billion war-spending package — while the Pentagon quietly revised its Iran-war casualty database, introducing a new operational category that appeared designed to reset the 60-day clock that normally forces a war-powers reckoning with Congress.
By Monday, several of these threads deepened. ICE, which had briefly paused most vehicle stops after two fatal shootings in Texas and Maine, resumed aggressive enforcement within days on Trump's order, despite one of the officers involved having a documented history of domestic abuse allegations that should have disqualified him from hiring. Separately, Taylor Farms — a food company linked to a multi-state cyclospora outbreak — contacted the White House seeking a delay in the government's public health announcement, a request a former FDA commissioner said fell well outside normal practice. At a Michigan rally, Trump reduced the justification for the Iran war to a single unexamined claim, falsely stated he had attended the G20, and misrepresented the source of ammunition shortages, while posting AI-generated images hinting at a future political run. Meanwhile, the 9/11 petition story moved further into the mainstream: CBS News New York and Fox Business both amplified it, with Fox Business citing a figure — 15,000 — with no supporting count. In Arizona, federal contractors felled centuries-old cottonwood trees for a border wall extension despite earlier assurances to preserve nesting habitat, prompting activists to occupy the last standing tree.
Tuesday was the week's most consequential single day. Senators John Cornyn and Thom Tillis announced they would withhold support for Attorney General nominee Todd Blanche unless the administration provided written confirmation that a $1.8 billion "anti-weaponization" fund — tied to a personal IRS settlement involving Trump — would be eliminated. The Senate Judiciary Committee scheduled, then began wavering on, a confirmation vote. The same day exposed a stark asymmetry in how election irregularities were treated: a New Jersey software error involving fewer than 400 noncitizen votes triggered an immediate federal investigation, subpoena threats, and calls to end automatic voter registration, while news surfaced that California had rejected nearly 150,000 mail ballots in its June primary with no federal inquiry and little media attention. Also on Tuesday, the FCC received petitions to strip license renewals from eight ABC-owned stations following Trump's criticism of the network, prompting a bipartisan group of former FCC chairs to warn that the proceeding was unconstitutional. Trump pressured Health Secretary Kennedy to cut the childhood vaccine schedule further, despite a standing federal court order against such changes. And as gas prices, mortgage rates, and war costs strained household budgets nationwide, Trump announced the White House had ordered 250 Cadillac Escalades at a cost exceeding $22 million. The day closed with reports that ICE was preparing action against roughly 300,000 Haitian Temporary Protected Status holders, arresting people with expired visas at airports — including U.S. citizens' spouses — and that the Office of Refugee Resettlement had shared 460,000 leads with ICE, resulting in more than 12,000 arrests, some involving children and their sponsors.
On Wednesday, the fight over historical memory returned to Congress. A House committee member had coordinated with Christian nationalist activists on questions for a Smithsonian oversight hearing, during which the director of the National Museum of American History was accused of promoting "woke ideology" for examining difficult chapters of the country's past. At a separate hearing chaired by Senator Rand Paul, Dr. Anthony Fauci invoked his Fifth Amendment right rather than answer questions about COVID-19's origins, describing the proceeding as an attempt to embarrass and imprison him; Paul said he would pursue a contempt of Congress vote. In a party-line vote, the Senate confirmed Jay Clayton as director of national intelligence, despite his having sidestepped questions about the 2020 election and having previously subpoenaed New York Times journalists before withdrawing the subpoenas. Reporting also revealed that the administration had directed Secret Service agents to secretly monitor former FBI Director James Comey's phone after his "86 47" social media post, with agents instructed to report details on their questioning of him directly to the president. Quietly, a deadline passed: Trump did not file for Supreme Court rehearing of the ruling upholding birthright citizenship, despite earlier vowing to do so "immediately," leaving the protection intact for now.
Thursday brought the Blanche standoff to a head. The Senate Judiciary Committee abandoned plans to advance his nomination after Trump declined to formally narrow the immunity arrangement senators had demanded be terminated in writing. Rather than yield, Trump reversed course again, vowing to revive the fund through legislation or executive action — and then floated an extraordinary alternative: "temporarily" withdrawing Blanche's nomination and resubmitting it once Cornyn and Tillis had left office, keeping Blanche as acting attorney general indefinitely in the meantime. The same day, the coordinated pressure on Fauci continued, with multiple Republican senators accusing him of covering up vaccine information and demanding apologies, while a Democratic senator warned the hearings were scapegoating one official for a broader institutional failure. In a rare moment of unanimity, the Senate passed a non-binding resolution opposing any pardon or clemency for Ghislaine Maxwell. Elsewhere, a State Department presentation at an international AIDS conference in Brazil displayed an AI-generated map with every African country misplaced — Nigeria shown inland, Mozambique relocated — bearing an OpenAI watermark. And as the 30-year Treasury yield hit a 19-year high, with national debt approaching $40 trillion and annual interest payments surpassing $1 trillion, neither party offered a serious plan to address it.
Friday closed the week with the Senate's second failed attempt in two weeks to constrain the administration's war-making authority. Following fresh airstrikes launched in response to an Iranian missile attack, the Senate voted 49-50 to reject a resolution requiring troop withdrawal absent explicit congressional authorization — a narrow but consequential defeat that left the earlier casualty-database reclassification's practical effect unchallenged. On voting rights, the administration petitioned the Supreme Court to enforce a mail-voting order requiring citizenship lists, even after 23 states and the First Circuit had already blocked it, while the Kansas Court of Appeals separately preserved a mail-ballot grace period historically responsible for saving tens of thousands of votes. It also emerged that the FBI had requested and received the IP address of a South Carolina voter as part of a fraud investigation, a move a former senior Justice Department official called atypical. Trump demanded the Senate cancel its August recess to pass the long-stalled SAVE America Act. A budget filing revealed that at least one detained hunger striker in ICE custody had been subjected to forced feeding, hydration, catheterization, or blood draws, with an ACLU attorney noting many detainees lack legal representation to contest such orders. And reporting deepened around Representative Max Miller, detailing allegations that he had physically abused his ex-wife and infant daughter, with a restraining order filed and a former girlfriend's earlier allegations resurfacing alongside calls for his resignation.
Viewed as a whole, the week showed several of the same pressures converging from different directions. Efforts to control historical narrative moved from rulemaking to congressional hearings shaped by outside activists. A cabinet confirmation stalled not over policy disagreement but over a president's refusal to relinquish a personal legal shield, forcing an unusual standoff with members of his own party. Congress twice declined to reassert its constitutional authority over war, even as the executive branch adjusted its own recordkeeping in ways that could delay that reckoning further. And a single unverified petition, amplified without correction across several major news organizations, showed how quickly an unsubstantiated claim about a sitting official can outrun any effort to correct it. None of these stories, individually, settled anything. Together, they mark a week in which existing checks — judicial, legislative, and journalistic — were repeatedly tested, and in several instances, did not hold.
```
