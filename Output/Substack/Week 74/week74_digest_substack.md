# A Birthday Party, an Arch, and a War Nobody Can Explain

*The president turned the White House lawn into a stage, Congress lost its surveillance law and nearly its intelligence oversight, and a hastily signed Iran deal came apart in public within days.*

![Header image](week74-digest-featured.jpg)

```markdown
This was the week the White House became a stage. Donald Trump turned his 80th birthday into a corporate-sponsored spectacle on the South Lawn, Congress let a surveillance law lapse without renewing it, and a hastily announced deal with Iran unraveled in full public view within days. Beneath the noise, courts kept intervening against the administration's excesses — sometimes successfully, sometimes only after visible delay and resistance. By week's end, an untested loyalist had taken charge of American intelligence, a mosque president had been freed from what a judge called retaliatory detention, and a small Georgia town had beaten back a federal detention center simply by refusing to accept it.

**The Weekend**

The weekend began with the UFC event, and it was no sideshow. Trump hosted a fight card on the White House lawn to mark his birthday, backed by at least $60 million in combined federal agency and UFC funding, cryptocurrency bonuses distributed through his family's World Liberty Financial venture, and his own stockholding in UFC's parent company. Legal efforts to stop the event failed in court, and when a fighter made a racist, sexist remark about Michelle Obama during the broadcast, Trump said nothing. Army ceremonial units more commonly reserved for visiting heads of state opened a related press conference at the Lincoln Memorial, blurring the line between military pageantry and personal promotion. The event's financing, political convenience, and symbolism would prove not to be a one-off; later reporting connected it to plans for a July 4 rally and to Trump's continued use of a foreign government's jet — part of a broader pattern of turning the presidency into an instrument of self-glorification.

Even as the party unfolded, courts pushed back elsewhere. A federal judge's order to strip Trump's name from the Kennedy Center's facade survived an emergency appeal, and workers quietly removed the signage overnight under tarps. The Trump-appointed board that runs the center voted to keep fighting the ruling anyway — a pattern of grudging, incomplete compliance that would recur throughout the week. Separately, Judge Leonie Brinkema blocked a $1.8 billion fund that had emerged from Trump's own legal settlement with the IRS, taxpayer money that could have been used to compensate his allies and January 6 defendants. A second court ordered the restoration of historical exhibits on slavery, civil rights, Indigenous history, and climate change that had been stripped from national parks the previous year, giving the administration three weeks to comply. Each ruling represented a check on an administration eager to rewrite institutions, records, and financial arrangements in its own image.

Immigration enforcement hardened over the same stretch. Trump signed a $70 billion Department of Homeland Security funding law locking in years of expanded ICE and CBP operations through 2029, stripped of the accountability measures — warrant requirements, body cameras — that Democrats had sought. In Ohio, FBI agents raided the Cleveland office of a voter registration group and visited affiliated individuals' homes, seizing devices as part of a broader push to investigate voter fraud ahead of the midterms. At midnight, Section 702 of the Foreign Intelligence Surveillance Act lapsed for the first time in eighteen years, after Congress failed to renew it amid Democratic distrust of Trump's installation of a politically loyal acting director of national intelligence — a distrust that would prove well-founded by Friday. New Jersey's attorney general sued to force health inspections at Delaney Hall, an ICE-contracted facility in Newark, while the governor toured the site and protesters were arrested in solidarity outside. And the New York Times reported that the administration had seriously considered suspending habeas corpus to speed deportations after the Supreme Court affirmed migrants' right to challenge removal in court — a contemplated step, not an executed one, but a serious one nonetheless.

**Monday**

The week's second major storyline emerged Monday, when Trump announced via social media that a deal with Iran was complete and that the U.S. naval blockade of the Strait of Hormuz would be lifted. The agreement's text was withheld even as Iran's foreign ministry began describing terms — on sanctions relief and Hormuz transit fees — that sharply contradicted the administration's account. The same day, Governor Gavin Newsom announced that Trump had directed Justice Department investigations into him and his wife, describing a year of federal scrutiny and grand jury activity aimed at a potential 2028 rival. The Justice Department also charged five men with plotting to attack the UFC event with explosive drones and snipers, a plot uncovered after a tip from one suspect's mother — a rare instance of the week's security apparatus functioning as intended, though its aftermath would raise separate concerns. Meanwhile, Shasta County, California, passed a ballot measure ending mail-in and early voting and mandating hand-counted ballots, prompting the state's attorney general to announce plans to sue. And Representative Jamie Raskin accused FBI Director Kash Patel of directing more than $1 million in bonuses to loyalist agents, calling it a possible personal slush fund.

**Tuesday**

Tuesday brought the week's densest concentration of institutional strain. New reporting detailed how Stephen Miller had proposed suspending habeas corpus outright, and how Miller and Vice President Vance had separately pushed for invoking the Insurrection Act to suppress protests after ICE agents killed two civilians, Renee Good and Alex Pretti, in Minnesota. The administration was also reported to be reclassifying long-term immigrant residents to deny them bond hearings, in apparent defiance of court rulings against the practice. In a starkly asymmetrical response to the Minnesota killings, federal prosecutors charged fifteen protesters with conspiracy for opposing the crackdown — even as the agents involved faced no charges, and even as eighteen of thirty-six earlier cases against protesters had already been dropped, one after a judge found it rested on a false affidavit. FBI Director Patel then posted details from a court-sealed investigation into the UFC attack plot on social media, prompting allegations that he had violated disclosure rules; Trump, asked about the plot at the G7 summit, appeared unaware of it. Trump also threatened Senator Lindsey Graham over his skepticism of the Iran deal, warning he would be "in big trouble" — a threat that drew pushback from Graham and other Republicans asserting Congress's right to review any nuclear agreement. The Kennedy Center board, for its part, missed the court-ordered deadline to remove Trump's name by twelve hours, then certified compliance while the facade sat under a tarp. And reporting confirmed that a $14 million no-bid Reflecting Pool renovation and a promised $200 million East Wing ballroom project, now grown to roughly $600 million, were both running far over their advertised costs.

**Wednesday**

On Wednesday, Trump signed the Iran memorandum of understanding at the Palace of Versailles, having abruptly reversed an earlier plan to have Vance sign it instead. The backlash was immediate: Republican senators including Bill Cassidy, Ted Cruz, and Thom Tillis criticized reported concessions, including a $300 billion fund for Iran and immediate sanctions relief granted without preconditions. Elsewhere, the Justice Department refused to file a court-required declaration certifying that the blocked $1.8 billion fund had actually been halted, citing separation-of-powers concerns — continued resistance to a judicial order rather than compliance with it. The department also settled claims for Michael Flynn and Carter Page at $1.25 million each, using the same judgment-fund mechanism that January 6 defendants convicted of assaulting police were separately pursuing for their own payouts, prompting Senator Adam Schiff to introduce legislation barring such payments. Congressional staff visited the federal prison camp housing Ghislaine Maxwell to investigate allegations of preferential treatment, only to have Bureau of Prisons leadership repeatedly shut down their questioning. And new reporting showed that ICE detention deaths had more than doubled since Trump's second term began, while the agency was found to be purchasing immigrant tax records from a data broker in an apparent workaround of a court order restricting such access.

**Thursday**

Thursday delivered the Iran deal's final unraveling for the week. The administration released the fourteen-point memorandum text, only for Vance's public claim — that sanctions relief was contingent on Iranian behavioral change — to be directly contradicted by the document's actual language, which provided for immediate relief. In a notable counterpoint, Georgia's Republican House Speaker announced the legislature would not redraw the state's congressional map despite Trump's pressure to exploit a Supreme Court ruling weakening Voting Rights Act protections, citing the need for a deliberate process — a rare instance of a Republican-led body declining to use an opening for partisan advantage. At the intelligence community's headquarters, Bill Pulte arrived a day ahead of his official start as acting Director of National Intelligence, requesting a full employee list to plan cuts of potentially hundreds of jobs and asking about bringing classified briefings home. The Justice Department's Office of Legal Counsel issued a memo arguing that federal disability law does not require community-based care alternatives to institutionalization, explicitly acknowledging a break from thirty years of settled interpretation. And the administration's denaturalization campaign accelerated sharply, with 29 cases filed in under two months against a total of 166 filed between 2008 and mid-2026, and plans for at least 250 more by October.

**Friday**

The week closed on Friday with Pulte formally sworn in as acting Director of National Intelligence, after Trump cancelled the Senate confirmation hearing of his intended nominee, Jay Clayton, by social media post. Pulte arrived with no intelligence background and a documented history of pursuing politically motivated referrals against Trump's critics. The same day, a federal judge ordered the release of Salah Sarsour, a Wisconsin mosque president and green card holder, finding that immigration officials had likely detained him in retaliation for his Palestinian rights advocacy after Secretary of State Marco Rubio personally signed a memo targeting him — a ruling that found a legal resident's detention had violated his First Amendment rights. In North Carolina, the legislature continued advancing a package of bills granting partisan control over election administration, criminalizing homelessness, and diluting Black voter power, despite sustained public opposition and the governor's objections. In Georgia, DHS reversed course and cancelled plans for a 10,000-bed detention center in a converted warehouse after a small town sued and organized against it. Federal prosecutors indicted fifteen more Minneapolis protesters after infiltrating their encrypted communications, even as the FBI seized a voting rights group's devices and subpoenaed a national voting organization. Trump's fabricated claim that Italian Prime Minister Giorgia Meloni had "begged" him for a photo at the G7 prompted a public denunciation from Meloni and the cancellation of a planned visit by Italy's foreign minister. And officials delayed release of a report on voting machine vulnerabilities even as Pulte was directed to investigate claims of "rigged elections."

Taken as a whole, the week showed an executive branch testing the outer edges of its authority on multiple fronts at once — over intelligence leadership, foreign policy, detention, and dissent — while courts, career officials, and even some Republican lawmakers and local governments pushed back, not always successfully and rarely without delay. The Iran memorandum's public collapse, the surveillance lapse, and the installation of an inexperienced loyalist atop American intelligence were not isolated events but connected symptoms of an administration moving fast and disclosing little. Judicial rulings on the Kennedy Center, the anti-weaponization fund, national park exhibits, and a wrongly detained mosque president showed that institutional checks still functioned — but compliance came late, grudgingly, or under visible duress. For a system built on the assumption that power answers eventually to law, this was a week that tested how long "eventually" can stretch before it stops meaning anything at all.
```
