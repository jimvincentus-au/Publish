# This Week in Democracy: Millions March, a Cabinet Purge, and a President in the Courtroom

*The largest single-day protest in American history collided with a government shutdown, a fired attorney general, a Pentagon purge, and an executive assault on mail-in voting.*

![Header image](week63-digest-featured.jpg)

```markdown
This week began with the largest single-day protest in American history and ended with the president's attorney general fired days before a subpoenaed deposition. Between those two poles ran a familiar but intensifying pattern: a government shutdown grinding into its seventh week, courts repeatedly blocking executive actions, an escalating threat of war against Iran, and a president who attacked the Supreme Court publicly before appearing in its courtroom in person. It was a week in which the machinery of dissent and the machinery of institutional control both operated at full capacity, often in direct view of each other.

The weekend opened with numbers rarely seen in American civic life. On March 28, more than 8 million people took part in over 3,300 coordinated demonstrations across all fifty states and more than fifteen countries, under the banner "No Kings 3." Organizers and outside observers described it as surpassing the 2017 Women's March as the largest single-day protest in the nation's history. Nearly half the gatherings occurred in Republican-leaning areas, suggesting the discontent was not confined to traditional opposition strongholds. The White House response was dismissive, attributing the turnout to "leftist funding networks" rather than engaging with its substance. Yet even as most of the day passed peacefully, it did not end that way everywhere: near a federal detention facility in Los Angeles, police used pepper rounds and bean bags to disperse a crowd, and a Department of Homeland Security agent shot an 18-year-old student journalist with a less-lethal projectile, permanently blinding him in one eye — an incident his attorney said violated a standing federal injunction protecting journalists at protests.

Underneath the protests, the Department of Homeland Security shutdown was already in its sixth week, the longest partial government shutdown in American history. House Republicans, driven by a Freedom Caucus faction resistant to funding without accompanying immigration-enforcement reforms, had rejected a bipartisan Senate deal that would have funded most of the department. TSA workers went unpaid, absences and resignations climbed, and Trump responded not through negotiation but through unilateral executive orders and memoranda directing DHS to redirect funds to cover salaries — moves of uncertain legal footing that nonetheless kept the appearance of function without resolving the underlying impasse. Elsewhere over the weekend, a federal judge restored detained immigrants' access to confidential legal calls at a Florida facility, nine activists were convicted on terrorism charges in a Texas ICE-protest case despite the trial judge noting no organizational basis for that label, and the FBI director moved to release a decade-old file involving a Democratic congressman and gubernatorial candidate despite his prior cooperation and exoneration. Overseas, the war with Iran entered its fourth week without congressional authorization, straining the Strait of Hormuz and rattling oil markets.

By Monday, the administration's ambitions turned toward election administration itself. Trump signed an executive order directing DHS and the Social Security Administration to build a national voter eligibility list, instructing the Postal Service to send mail ballots only to voters on that list, and threatening to withhold federal funds from states that did not comply. Civil rights groups sued within hours, arguing the order intruded on constitutional authority reserved to the states. The same day, Trump publicly called the Supreme Court "stupid" and "dumb," two days ahead of oral arguments in the birthright citizenship case his administration was defending — an attempt to shape public perception of an outcome not yet decided. Iran policy hardened further, with Trump threatening to destroy the country's power plants, oil wells, and desalination facilities if the Strait of Hormuz remained closed; legal experts noted that strikes on civilian infrastructure, particularly desalination plants, could constitute war crimes, a question the White House press secretary declined to answer directly. Domestically, a federal judge blocked construction of Trump's $400 million White House ballroom project, ruling he lacked authority to proceed without congressional approval — a ruling Trump attacked on social media even as the Justice Department appealed. A separate judge found the administration's funding cutoff to NPR and PBS unconstitutional viewpoint discrimination. The Endangered Species Committee, convened for the first time in three decades, unanimously exempted Gulf of Mexico drilling from environmental review on national-security grounds tied to the Iran conflict, and the Pentagon quietly sat on dozens of wind-farm project reviews as Treasury yields climbed toward levels last seen during the 2008 crisis.

Tuesday brought the formal signing of the mail-in voting order, with additional detail: the Attorney General was directed to investigate election officials over mail-ballot distribution, and lawsuits followed almost immediately from state officials and voting-rights groups. The Endangered Species Committee finalized its Gulf drilling exemption. Three former FBI agents, two of whom had worked on the Jack Smith investigation, filed a class-action suit alleging they had been summarily fired without due process in retaliation for perceived disloyalty. The Justice Department separately sued Minnesota over transgender athletes' participation in school sports, while the Supreme Court ruled 8–1 that Colorado's conversion-therapy ban for minors was unconstitutional, casting doubt on similar bans in over twenty other states. In upstate New York, a medical examiner ruled the February death of a blind Rohingya refugee — left in a Buffalo parking lot on a freezing night by border patrol agents — a homicide, prompting a state investigation. Quietly, the Justice Department's Office of Legal Counsel issued an opinion arguing that the Presidential Records Act was unconstitutional and that Trump need not comply with it, a claim with implications for the preservation of his own administration's history.

Wednesday's defining moment came at the Supreme Court itself, where Trump became the first sitting president to attend oral arguments in person, sitting in the front row with his attorney general and commerce secretary for the birthright citizenship case he had derided two days earlier. He left after about ninety minutes, before the ACLU's argument, but not before several justices — including Kagan, Kavanaugh, and Gorsuch — voiced skepticism toward the government's position. The same day, the Justice Department removed a career prosecutor who had resisted pressure to bring insufficiently supported charges against former CIA Director John Brennan, a move that would prove to be a preview of larger changes at the department's top. Florida and Mississippi signed proof-of-citizenship voter-registration laws, joining South Dakota and Utah in a growing state-level trend. And Trump posted, falsely, that Iran's president had requested a ceasefire — a claim Iran's Foreign Ministry denied within hours.

Thursday was the week's sharpest institutional rupture. Trump fired Attorney General Pam Bondi by social media post, reportedly over her handling of the Epstein files and insufficient prosecution of political adversaries, timed just before her subpoenaed deposition on those files was to occur. He replaced her with Todd Blanche, his former personal defense attorney, who promptly stated that the president has the "right" to direct Justice Department investigations. Epstein survivors, family members, and lawmakers from both parties demanded that accountability efforts continue regardless of who held the office. On the same day, in a separate institution governed by different rules and different chains of command, Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth fired the Army's Chief of Staff, General Randy George, after George refused an order to strike two women and two Black officers from a promotion list; two additional senior Army officers, including the head of the Chaplain Corps, were removed as well. The Joint Chiefs' leadership publicly honored George's service — a rare and pointed institutional rebuke delivered without direct confrontation. These were not the same event wearing two faces: one was the removal of the nation's chief law enforcement officer ahead of testimony on a politically sensitive matter, replaced by a personal loyalist; the other was the ouster of a senior military officer for refusing to execute a discriminatory personnel order. That they landed on the same day gave the week its most visible marker of institutional pressure converging from two directions at once. The day also brought new tariffs — 100% on patented pharmaceuticals, 50% on metals — with reduced rates offered to companies willing to onshore production or negotiate pricing, effectively creating a tiered system that favored firms with leverage to negotiate directly with the executive branch. A Trump-aligned planning commission approved the ballroom project just two days after a federal judge had blocked it, and at an Easter reception, Trump directed his budget director to end federal daycare funding, telling states to absorb the cost given wartime military spending.

The week closed on Friday with a budget request that made explicit what many of the week's threads had implied. The administration's proposed fiscal year 2027 budget nearly doubled 2024 defense spending, requesting $1.5 trillion, funded in part through 10% cuts to non-defense programs including healthcare, housing, and education, alongside $152 million to reopen Alcatraz as an active prison. A separate sequestration order mandated automatic cuts across non-exempt federal accounts. The DHS shutdown, now past forty-nine days, persisted as the House again declined to vote on the Senate's bipartisan compromise; Republicans instead pursued funding ICE through budget reconciliation to bypass a Democratic filibuster, while Trump signed yet another order directing full DHS pay through redirected funds. Reporting also revealed that DHS had been deploying BORTAC and BORSTAR — tactical units trained for high-risk warrants and cartel operations — in routine civilian immigration enforcement, involving extensive force and at least one fatal shooting of an unarmed civilian. Not every development ran in the administration's favor: a federal court struck down the seven-day advance-notice requirement that had constrained congressional oversight visits to ICE facilities, restoring lawmakers' ability to conduct surprise inspections. Overseas, the United States bombed a bridge in Iran's Karaj region, and Trump posted about seizing Iranian oil while threatening to bombard the country "back to the Stone Ages" — language delivered even as a U.S. search-and-rescue operation for a missing service member was still underway. Civil society's response continued to build: Washington state enacted its first income tax on millionaires, New York added wealth-tax provisions to its budget, California organizers advanced a billionaire wealth-tax ballot initiative, and labor and education groups announced a May Day "Workers Over Billionaires" day of action. Lawmakers from both parties pressed for Bondi's subpoenaed testimony to proceed despite her firing, while a DOJ whistleblower's attorneys alleged that the department's inspector general had failed to investigate serious misconduct amid what they described as unprecedented violations of court orders.

Taken as a whole, the week traced a familiar arc pulled taut in both directions at once. Millions of Americans mobilized in numbers without precedent, expressing broad and geographically diffuse unease with the concentration of power in the executive branch. At the same moment, that same branch moved to seize control of election administration, remove officials who would not bend to political direction, threaten force against a foreign civilian population, and reallocate the federal budget toward military priorities at the expense of domestic programs. Courts intervened repeatedly — on the ballroom, on public broadcasting, on detention-facility access, on congressional oversight — providing a check that held in specific instances even as the president publicly challenged the judiciary's legitimacy days before appearing in its own courtroom. Whether these countervailing forces would settle into a durable equilibrium or continue to strain against each other remained, at week's end, an open question resting on institutions still functioning, if unevenly, under considerable pressure.
```
