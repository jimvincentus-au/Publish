Week 8: Institutions Hollowed, Membership Redrawn
Executive orders, DOGE austerity, and immigration crackdowns quietly rewire who governs, who belongs, and which histories and services survive inside the American state.
By Week 8 of Trump’s second term, the pattern was no longer a series of isolated shocks but a visible architecture. Power was moving along three main channels: a presidency that treated law as a flexible tool, a parallel apparatus in Elon Musk’s Department of Government Efficiency that reached into every agency, and an immigration and security system turned inward against dissenters and the vulnerable. Around them, courts, civil servants, and citizens pushed back, but always from a position of reaction. The Democracy Clock stayed at 7:52 p.m. in public terms, yet the underlying movement—0.2 minutes closer to midnight—captured how much of this week’s damage was structural rather than spectacular, and how much resistance was needed just to hold the visible time steady. That resistance was real. It was also outpaced.
At the close of Week 7, the Clock already read 7:52 p.m., a democracy under strain but not yet in formal collapse. By the end of Week 8, the public time was unchanged, though the internal measure ticked forward by a fraction of a minute. That small shift masked a heavy load. Executive power without effective oversight edged higher as the White House declared a border emergency, ended birthright citizenship by decree, and used executive orders to dismantle agencies and target specific law firms. Law as a weapon, not a limit, advanced as immigration statutes, anti‑corruption rules, and prosecutorial discretion were bent toward punishing enemies and shielding allies. At the same time, courts ordered thousands of purged civil servants reinstated, blocked cuts to teacher training, and forced Musk’s DOGE to open its records. The net effect was a week in which authoritarian drift deepened, but institutional resistance kept the hands of the Clock from jumping more visibly forward. The strain on those guardrails grew.
The first major development was the consolidation of executive power through orders that reached far beyond ordinary policy change. Trump’s March 14 order to “continue the reduction of the federal bureaucracy” instructed multiple entities—from the Agency for Global Media to the Minority Business Development Agency—to strip away non‑statutory functions and report back to the White House. This was not a budget trim; it was a directive to decide, inside the executive branch, which congressionally created missions would effectively live or die. In parallel, another order defunded the US Agency for Global Media outright, threatening the jobs and even the safety of thousands of journalists and staff whose work had long projected independent reporting into authoritarian environments. These moves shifted power from dispersed, semi‑independent institutions to a single center, and they did so in ways that would be hard to reverse quickly, because they hollowed out people, programs, and global relationships rather than just lines on a ledger. The architecture of the state itself was being pared back.
The same week, the presidency reached down into the legal profession itself. An executive order aimed at the law firm Paul Weiss directed agencies to review and potentially suspend security clearances for its lawyers, cut off goods and services, and reconsider contracts with its clients. The stated rationale invoked national interest and alleged discrimination, but the firm’s history of work against Trump associates made the target plain. A similar order days earlier had gone after Perkins Coie, prompting a federal judge to liken it to a bill of attainder and to block most of its effects. Together, these orders showed a presidency willing to use national security language to punish specific legal adversaries. That is a structural change. It tells every firm and lawyer that representing the wrong client or cause can bring not just political blowback but the machinery of the state. The chill on independent legal advocacy is the point.
Immigration and citizenship became the second great channel of power. Over the course of the week, the administration moved to end birthright citizenship, suspend the refugee resettlement program, and declare a national emergency at the southern border. The birthright change, announced as a completed act and then backed by emergency appeals to the Supreme Court, went to the core of the Fourteenth Amendment’s promise that anyone born on US soil is a citizen. Ending that by executive order did more than alter immigration policy; it stratified membership in the polity by ancestry and status, and it did so in a way that, if upheld, would be hard to unwind. The refugee suspension and border emergency, meanwhile, normalized the use of extraordinary powers for what had long been treated as chronic policy questions. Funds could be shifted, enforcement expanded, and oversight sidestepped under the banner of crisis. Emergency powers were becoming a routine governing tool.
Within that new legal landscape, the case of Mahmoud Khalil became a focal point. Khalil, a Palestinian activist and Columbia graduate with a green card, was arrested by plainclothes ICE agents at his university housing and moved to detention in Louisiana. The government invoked a rarely used foreign‑policy provision, claiming his presence could harm US interests, and tied his activism to Hamas without charging him with any crime. At the same time, the Secretary of State announced plans to revoke visas and green cards for those “deemed” to support Hamas, and DHS revoked another Columbia student’s visa on similar grounds. These actions turned immigration law into a direct instrument against political speech. They told non‑citizens—and, by extension, many citizens—that certain views could cost them their right to live in the country, regardless of legal status or lack of criminal conduct. Dissent was recast as disloyalty.
The machinery of enforcement followed that logic. ICE detained a naturalized citizen in Virginia despite his valid documents, swept up record numbers of immigrants in February, and filled detention centers to capacity, including through new agreements that let local police act as immigration agents. The administration resumed family detention at privately run centers in Texas, reversing a prior shift toward surveillance alternatives. It deported families to Panama and Costa Rica, where they were held in poor conditions with limited access to counsel, and it removed the non‑citizen parents of a ten‑year‑old US citizen recovering from brain cancer as they traveled for medical care. Plans surfaced to invoke the Alien Enemies Act, a wartime authority last associated with Japanese internment, to allow arrests and deportations without normal due process. In each case, the law remained on the books, but its application moved away from individualized risk and toward broad categories of origin and ideology. Citizenship itself became more contingent.
Against this, the courts drew some lines. A federal judge in Manhattan barred Khalil’s deportation while his case was heard, and another order in the same matter demanded that the government justify its use of the foreign‑policy clause. These were temporary shields, not final victories, but they showed that judicial review still had teeth. Yet even here, the balance of power was shifting. The administration pressed the Supreme Court to lift injunctions blocking its birthright order, arguing that nationwide blocks hampered executive function. And Trump, in a speech at the Justice Department, praised Judge Aileen Cannon for dismissing his classified‑documents case against precedent and mused that criticizing judges should perhaps be illegal. The message to the bench was clear: rulings that favor the president will be rewarded with public acclaim, while scrutiny of the judiciary itself is suspect. Judicial independence was being tested in real time.
The third major development lay inside the government’s own workforce. Under direction from the White House and Musk’s DOGE, the Office of Personnel Management had ordered mass firings of probationary employees across multiple agencies weeks earlier. In Week 8, the scale and intent of that purge came into sharper focus. The Department of Education announced that more than 1,300 staff—roughly half its workforce—were being laid off, with civil‑rights enforcement offices especially hard hit. TSA workers lost union protections. HHS offered buyouts to tens of thousands of employees in the middle of a measles outbreak. The Veterans Administration prepared to cut 80,000 jobs, many held by veterans themselves. The Presidential Management Fellowship, a key pipeline for young talent into federal service, was shuttered. These were not isolated cuts; they were a coordinated hollowing out of the neutral civil service in favor of a leaner, more pliable apparatus. Capacity was being traded for control.
Here, too, the courts intervened. Judge William Alsup in California and Judge James Bredar in Maryland issued orders requiring agencies to offer jobs back to tens of thousands of fired probationary workers and blocking further firings at numerous departments. Both judges described the government’s performance‑based justifications as false and suggested an illegal scheme to purge staff without proper review. Their rulings did more than restore paychecks. They asserted that even probationary employees, often seen as expendable, are protected by law against mass political firings. They also limited OPM’s ability to advise agencies on terminations, constraining the very machinery the White House had used to reshape the bureaucracy. The legal floor for civil‑service protection held, at least for now.
Yet even as some workers returned, the broader trend continued. Treasury’s secretary spoke openly of shrinking and privatizing federal services, framing a shift from public to private spending as the administration’s economic answer. DOGE announced the termination of 793 federal leases, including offices for the IRS and Social Security, in the name of saving $500 million, regardless of operational strain. The Department of Education’s layoffs went forward despite lawsuits from more than twenty state attorneys general arguing that the cuts were unconstitutional and incapacitated the agency’s congressionally mandated duties. The Social Security Administration changed its overpayment policy to allow 100 percent benefit withholding, and only after public outcry did it back away from a plan to end phone service for millions of claimants. The pattern was clear: courts could block the most egregious moves, but the baseline of a smaller, more politicized state was being set. That baseline will be hard to rebuild.
A fourth development was the fusion of economic policy with elite interests and disinformation. Trump signed an order creating a US Crypto Reserve, using taxpayer money to support the cryptocurrency industry, even as the SEC dropped lawsuits against major exchanges and moved to replace a commissioner known for her skepticism of crypto. At the Justice Department, enforcement of the Foreign Corrupt Practices Act was paused for six months, and anti‑kleptocracy units that had targeted Russian oligarchs were disbanded. Separately, reporting described the administration’s tolerance of money laundering. These steps collectively signaled that certain forms of financial misconduct—especially those linked to favored sectors or foreign elites—would face less scrutiny, while other areas of law enforcement intensified. Wealth was buying not just speech, but law.
Musk’s DOGE sat at the center of this new economic‑political nexus. The EPA was told that any spending over $50,000 required DOGE approval, effectively giving a private‑aligned office veto power over environmental operations. DOGE’s website initially boasted of $105 billion in taxpayer savings, but an independent “Musk Watch” tracker found that only $8.6 billion could be verified, with the rest inflated by counting potential rather than actual contract amounts. In response, DOGE removed detailed data that had allowed cross‑checking, and Musk attacked the tracker rather than its methodology. At the same time, Popular Information documented how DOGE’s cuts to USAID contracts could lead to increased deaths from malaria and polio abroad. The combination of opaque numbers, aggressive branding, and real human costs illustrated how economic data was being massaged to support a political story of efficiency while masking the depth of the cuts. The numbers told one story; the outcomes told another.
Trade policy amplified the sense of chaos as strategy. The administration imposed 25 percent tariffs on all imported steel and aluminum, prompting immediate retaliation from the European Union and Canada, which announced billions in counter‑tariffs on US goods. Trump threatened to double tariffs on Canadian metals to 50 percent, then walked that back after Ontario dropped an electricity surcharge, only to proceed with the broader 25 percent measure. He also threatened a 200 percent tariff on EU wines and champagnes in response to planned EU whiskey tariffs, a move that intersected with his own winery interests. Markets reacted sharply: major indices suffered their worst early‑term declines since 2009, and analysts warned of higher inflation and slower growth. The administration’s own rhetoric framed this as a necessary “de‑tox period,” suggesting that public economic pain was an acceptable price for a future freed from “globalists.” Risk was shifted onto the public while profits and discretion stayed close to power.
Information and memory formed the fifth axis of change. The defunding of USAGM, with its networks like Voice of America and Radio Free Europe, removed a long‑standing US platform for independent news in authoritarian environments. At home, the information environment was strained in other ways. Musk’s social platform X suffered extended outages attributed to a DDoS attack, and allies like Michael Flynn floated conspiracies about the CIA or philanthropists being behind it. DOGE’s website changes made it harder to verify budget claims. USAID staff received an email instructing them to shred or burn agency records, in direct tension with federal preservation laws. A White House fact sheet quietly removed references to two California national monuments established to protect sacred lands, raising fears that their protected status might be revoked without open debate. Each of these actions chipped away at the infrastructure that allows citizens and future investigators to know what their government has done. What cannot be controlled is at risk of being erased.
At the same time, official disinformation continued to flow from the top. Trump again called the 2020 election “rigged and crooked” and demanded imprisonment of unnamed perpetrators, keeping alive a narrative that undermines trust in elections and paints political opponents as criminals. The Secretary of Health and Human Services, Robert F. Kennedy Jr., used a Fox News appearance to spread false claims about vaccine dangers during an active measles outbreak, even as his department canceled key vaccine advisory meetings and redirected resources toward unproven treatments. Elon Musk described Social Security as “the biggest Ponzi scheme of all time” and urged cuts to its workforce. These statements, coming from positions of authority, did not just mislead; they prepared the ground for policy changes that would weaken public health and social insurance while casting those changes as necessary corrections. The line between propaganda and policy grew thinner.
Universities and civil society became contested ground in this information struggle. The administration canceled $400 million in federal funding to Columbia University, citing failures to protect students from antisemitic harassment, and the Education Department warned sixty campuses of potential enforcement actions. Columbia, under pressure, expelled or suspended students and revoked diplomas for participants in a 2024 pro‑Palestinian demonstration. At the same time, a Columbia student’s visa was revoked, and Khalil’s case loomed over campus. These actions sent a clear signal: institutions that did not align their disciplinary and speech policies with federal expectations risked losing money and facing legal scrutiny. Universities, long spaces for dissent and debate, were being drawn into a system where their survival depended on managing protest in ways acceptable to the state. Academic freedom now carried fiscal risk.
Yet civil society did not fall silent. Thousands of researchers and supporters joined “Stand Up for Science” protests across the country, while hundreds of demonstrations on International Women’s Day challenged administration policies. Senator Bernie Sanders toured under the banner “Fighting Oligarchy,” drawing large crowds and linking domestic inequality to democratic erosion. Protesters targeted Tesla dealerships and charging stations, objecting to Musk’s role in government and the economic fallout of DOGE’s cuts. In North Carolina, activists organized rallies at the state legislature, planned to pack the gallery, and scheduled an “empty chair” town hall for a senator who would not appear. The Democratic Party announced “People’s Town Halls” in Republican‑held districts where incumbents avoided constituents. And in New York, Jewish Voice for Peace led an occupation of Trump Tower to demand Khalil’s release, resulting in nearly 100 arrests. These actions did not change the formal balance of power, but they showed that public resistance remained organized and visible. The streets still spoke.
Internationally, the week underscored a broader retreat from cooperative norms. The US formally exited the Paris Climate Agreement and withdrew from a global fund to compensate developing countries for climate harms. It extended permits for liquefied natural gas projects, promoted fossil fuel use in Africa, and cut support for renewable energy transitions abroad. In Europe, the administration threatened not to defend NATO allies unless they increased defense spending, flirted with withdrawal from the alliance, and endorsed aspects of Russia’s war aims. Military aid and intelligence to Ukraine were halted, then resumed only after Kyiv agreed to a 30‑day ceasefire on US terms. The US vetoed a G7 effort to crack down on Russia’s shadow oil fleet and pushed to strip references to sanctions and the war from joint statements. These moves weakened alliances, emboldened adversaries, and signaled that US commitments were now transactional and contingent. The same logic of power without durable constraint was playing out beyond US borders.
What to watch from this week’s record lies in the cases and processes already in motion. The Supreme Court’s response to the administration’s emergency appeals on birthright citizenship will determine whether the Fourteenth Amendment’s guarantee remains intact or is reinterpreted through executive initiative. Lawsuits by Democratic attorneys general against the Education Department’s cuts, and by states and NGOs against EPA grant cancellations, will test how far a president can go in dismantling agencies and reversing climate policy without new statutes. Court‑ordered discovery into DOGE’s operations, and FOIA coverage for that office, may reveal how decisions about cuts, leases, and spending vetoes were actually made. And the Khalil litigation will clarify whether foreign‑policy clauses and wartime authorities like the Alien Enemies Act can be used to deport activists for their speech. Each of these fights will shape how entrenched this week’s changes become.
In the larger arc of the term, Week 8 matters because it shows how quickly a democracy can be reshaped without a single dramatic break. Birthright citizenship is ended not by amendment but by order and litigation. A global media agency is defunded in a day. A department like Education is halved by email. Immigration law, once a technical field, becomes a direct instrument against protest. At the same time, judges in Boston, California, Maryland, and New York issue orders that keep people in the country, keep teachers in training, and keep civil servants at their desks. Journalists and watchdogs expose inflated savings and hidden cuts. Protesters fill streets and lobbies. The Democracy Clock at the end of Week 8 still reads 7:52 p.m., but that stability is not a sign of safety. It reflects how much institutional resistance is now required just to keep the visible time from racing ahead, and how much of the damage is being done in ways that will be hard to reverse when the hands finally move again. The work of repair is already running behind the pace of change.
