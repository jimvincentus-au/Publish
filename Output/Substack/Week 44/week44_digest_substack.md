# The Week Federal Agents Came to Charlotte, and the President Called for Executing Members of Congress

*A mass immigration raid on a mid-size city, a forced transparency law on Jeffrey Epstein, and a presidential outburst calling for the death of sitting lawmakers defined an extraordinary seven days*

![Header image](week44-digest-featured.jpg)

```markdown
The week of November 15–21 opened with federal agents descending on Charlotte, North Carolina, and closed with the president of the United States publicly calling for the execution of sitting members of Congress. In between, sustained pressure forced a reversal on the release of Jeffrey Epstein's files, federal judges found serious misconduct in a politically charged prosecution, and a marquee Republican ally announced she was leaving Congress altogether. It was a week in which the machinery of government, the courts, and ordinary citizens repeatedly collided with the outer edges of executive power.

The weekend began with "Operation Charlotte's Web," a Department of Homeland Security enforcement surge launched after local police in Charlotte declined to hold people without judicial warrants. In under five hours, agents arrested 81 people. Willy Aceituno, a naturalized U.S. citizen, was detained despite presenting citizenship documents, and agents smashed a car window during the operation. Hundreds of residents turned out to protest that afternoon, and the Charlotte-Mecklenburg police department publicly stated it had no role in the raid — an early sign of friction between local authorities and a federal operation deployed in a Democratic-led inland city.

The same weekend, a second story took shape that would run through the entire week: the fight over Jeffrey Epstein's files. Trump directed Attorney General Pam Bondi to open an investigation into Epstein-linked figures, including Bill Clinton, Larry Summers, Reid Hoffman, and JPMorgan Chase — a move critics said could later be used to withhold documents under an "ongoing investigation" exemption. He also threatened a lawsuit against the BBC over an edited clip of his January 6 speech, despite the network's apology. At the same time, Trump turned on members of his own party who supported releasing the Epstein files, calling Marjorie Taylor Greene a "traitor" and mocking Rep. Thomas Massie's recent marriage. Some supporters responded by burning MAGA merchandise. Facing an unstoppable discharge petition that would force a vote regardless of his wishes, Trump abruptly reversed course by Sunday night, posting that House Republicans should vote to release the files.

By Monday, the entanglement of presidential power with personal financial interest was harder to ignore. Trump pardoned Changpeng Zhao, the Binance founder convicted of money laundering for groups including Hamas and Al Qaeda, shortly after Binance struck a $2 billion deal with Trump's own crypto firm. He also pardoned white-collar fraudster Charles Scott. That same day, Trump announced his own cryptocurrency venture and moved to end a regulatory clampdown on the industry — regulatory relief arriving in the same news cycle as personal profit.

Monday also brought the first major judicial rebuke of the week. A magistrate judge found a "disturbing pattern" of misconduct in the prosecution of former FBI Director James Comey, ruling that Trump-appointed prosecutor Lindsey Halligan had made false statements of law to the grand jury that indicted him. The judge ordered grand jury materials turned over to the defense, describing the case bluntly as "indict first and investigate later." Separately, the Justice Department sued Michigan for unredacted voter rolls, including Social Security numbers, part of a pattern extending to Pennsylvania and Arizona, while a Michigan Republican leader simultaneously asked DOJ to send federal election monitors to the state. The State Department, meanwhile, circulated an internal memo proposing to suspend 38 universities, including Harvard and Yale, from a federal research partnership over diversity hiring practices.

Tuesday delivered one of the week's rare moments of bipartisan institutional success alongside one of its more troubling diplomatic spectacles. The House passed the Epstein Files Transparency Act 427–1, with only Rep. Clay Higgins voting no, mandating DOJ disclosure of unclassified Epstein-related records within 30 days. The Senate signaled it would pass the bill immediately by unanimous consent. On the same day, Trump hosted Saudi Crown Prince Mohammed bin Salman at the White House for the first time since journalist Jamal Khashoggi's 2018 murder. Asked about the killing, Trump said "things happen" and claimed the crown prince knew nothing about it — directly contradicting U.S. intelligence findings. The visit came with an announced F-35 fighter jet sale, made over Pentagon objections, a $1 trillion Saudi investment commitment, and a Trump Organization real estate deal in the kingdom moving forward in parallel.

Tuesday also brought a second major court ruling: a three-judge federal panel struck down Texas's newly drawn congressional map as racially discriminatory, ordering the state to use its 2021 map for 2026. Texas immediately appealed to the Supreme Court. In Charlotte, the immigration enforcement operation widened further — CBP agents attempted to raid an after-school program serving immigrant children, and the local school district recorded more than 30,000 absences, triple the normal rate, as families kept children home, including a blind and autistic student. In Dearborn, Michigan, right-wing influencers staged a rally that included the desecration of a Qur'an and slurs directed at city council members, an apparent effort to provoke confrontation for online content. And during the Saudi visit, Trump lashed out at reporters questioning him about Epstein and his family's business ties, calling for the FCC to revoke ABC's broadcast license and telling a Bloomberg reporter to be "quiet, piggy."

Wednesday carried the Epstein transparency effort to its legislative conclusion as the Senate passed the bill by unanimous consent, sending it to Trump's desk despite reported internal administration opposition — one official was quoted saying, "We lost this one badly." The Comey case, meanwhile, produced a startling admission: during a hearing on whether the prosecution was vindictive, Lindsey Halligan acknowledged that the full grand jury never reviewed the final indictment against Comey, and another prosecutor revealed that a memo recommending against charging him had been concealed on orders from the deputy attorney general's office. In Charlotte, enforcement agents raided a Latino church during services, sending congregants fleeing into the woods, while U.S. citizen Gerardo Ortiz was detained twice within 24 hours despite no criminal history — part of a surge that had produced more than 250 arrests statewide.

Also on Wednesday, six Democratic lawmakers with military or intelligence backgrounds — Senators Mark Kelly and Elissa Slotkin, and Representatives Chrissy Houlahan, Chris Deluzio, Jason Crow, and Maggie Goodlander — released a video reminding U.S. service members of their duty under the Uniform Code of Military Justice to refuse patently illegal orders, a principle rooted in Nuremberg precedent. It was a statement of settled law. The president's response the next day would treat it as an act of treason.

On Thursday, Trump signed the Epstein Files Transparency Act into law, establishing a 30-day deadline for the DOJ to release unclassified records — though the law's exemption for materials tied to "ongoing investigations" left lawmakers warning it could still be used to withhold documents. But the signing was overshadowed almost entirely by Trump's response to the veterans' video. He posted repeatedly on Truth Social, branding the six lawmakers traitors guilty of sedition "punishable by DEATH," and reposted messages calling for them to be hanged. House Democratic leaders contacted Capitol Police to arrange protection for the members named. The White House later claimed the president did not actually want them executed. It was, without qualification, a sitting president calling for the killing of sitting members of Congress over an act of protected speech restating existing military law.

The same day, a federal judge ruled that the administration lacked authority to unilaterally deploy the D.C. National Guard for routine policing, though the ruling was stayed until December 11 pending appeal, leaving troops in place for the time being. The administration also announced up to 34 new offshore oil and gas lease auctions off California, Alaska, and Florida, and proposed rules narrowing habitat protections under the Endangered Species Act, over bipartisan state objections.

Friday brought the resignation of Marjorie Taylor Greene, who announced she would leave Congress effective January 5, 2026, saying she refused to be a "battered wife" after Trump's attacks and withdrawal of his endorsement, and citing death threats she attributed to his rhetoric. Trump called her departure "great news for the country." The same day, in a striking contrast, Trump met cordially with New York City Mayor-elect Zohran Mamdani, praising him as a "rational person" and discussing cooperation on housing costs — months after calling him a "communist lunatic" and threatening to strip his citizenship and cut city funding. Also on Friday, 79-year-old Judge Jerry Smith issued a 104-page dissent in the Texas redistricting case invoking George Soros seventeen times, despite Soros having no role in the litigation — an unusual breach of judicial norms. New polling showed Trump's approval falling to 41%, a career low among several demographics, as unemployment rose to 4.4%, its highest level since 2021, and even 42% of Republicans said they blamed the president for economic conditions. Separately, Trump gave Venezuelan President Nicolás Maduro a one-week ultimatum to leave power; the deadline passed without his departure.

Taken as a whole, the week showed both the reach and the limits of executive authority operating under sustained pressure. Federal agents entered an inland American city and detained citizens without cause; a president used pardons and deregulation in ways that intersected with his own financial holdings; and, in the week's defining moment, that same president called publicly for the execution of elected lawmakers who had merely stated settled military law. Yet courts intervened repeatedly — on the Comey prosecution, on Texas's map, on the National Guard's deployment — and Congress, across party lines, forced a transparency law the White House did not want. Institutions bent under strain this week. They did not, in every instance, break.
```
