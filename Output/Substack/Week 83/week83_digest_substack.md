# This Week in Democracy: A President Who Answers to No One

*From an Attorney General who won't promise independence to a Supreme Court that pauses its own ruling, the guardrails keep bending the same direction*

```markdown
This was a week in which the machinery meant to check presidential power kept declining to use it — even as other parts of that same machinery pushed back hard enough to show the difference was a choice, not an inevitability.

**The Weekend**

The week opened with Attorney General Todd Blanche telling a national television audience that no attorney general should promise to act independently of the president. The remark came just days after his narrow confirmation, which followed the dismissal of his predecessor for insufficient action against Trump's political opponents. It set the tone for a week in which the country's central law-enforcement and regulatory bodies were repeatedly asked, directly or through their actions, whether they served the presidency or the public — and answered in ways that varied sharply depending on which institution was doing the answering.

That same weekend brought a wave of reporting on the immigration court system, where due process appeared to be eroding on several fronts at once. ICE agents made arrests inside courthouses despite the system's long-recognized chilling effect on attendance. "Mega master" hearings, sometimes scheduled with as little as fifteen days' notice, produced more than 53,000 in-absentia removal orders in June alone. More than 100 sitting immigration judges have been replaced by new appointees marketed as "deportation judges," who now grant relief in fewer than 2 percent of the cases they hear. A separate Texas Tribune analysis found children held well beyond the legally mandated 20-day detention limit. Taken together, these findings described a system built increasingly to produce removals rather than adjudicate them.

Financial self-dealing surfaced on multiple fronts over the same weekend. General Dynamics was found to have kept $533 million for an artillery factory that never became functional, with no accountability attached. House Democrats opened an insider-trading investigation after an Energy Fuels executive purchased company stock shortly before a monument-shrinkage decision that benefited the firm. And federal regulators approved a bank charter for World Liberty Financial, the Trump family's cryptocurrency venture, which has already funneled more than $1.6 billion to the family. The president, meanwhile, staged a photo-op crediting himself for a drop in drug prices actually driven by a Biden-era law, and separately claimed inflation was falling and that the United States "totally controls" the Strait of Hormuz — both claims contradicted by available data.

**Monday**

The judiciary offered a more mixed picture. The Supreme Court declined to rehear Trump's appeal in the E. Jean Carroll case, leaving intact the $5 million jury verdict finding him liable for sexual abuse and defamation — a quiet but firm assertion that a jury's finding against a sitting president cannot simply be undone by petition. A federal judge separately blocked the FBI and General Services Administration's plan to relocate FBI headquarters to the Reagan Building, ruling that the $555 million reprogramming exceeded statutory authority and permanently enjoining the agencies from proceeding.

That same day, ABC filed a federal lawsuit against the FCC and its chair, alleging that the agency's demand for early license renewal on eight stations amounted to retaliation for coverage the president disapproved of, including the network's handling of late-night host Jimmy Kimmel. Also on Monday, a federal prosecutor sued over her termination, alleging retaliation for prior enforcement work, while the Justice Department appealed a judge's dismissal of charges against Kilmar Ábrego García — charges the judge himself had called "vindictive." In the Oval Office, Trump threatened to bomb Oman if it interfered with reopening the Strait of Hormuz, said he intended to declare the strait U.S. territory, and dismissed reports of poor conditions aboard the USS Abraham Lincoln.

**Tuesday**

Tuesday brought a legislative response to the weekend's self-dealing revelations. Senator Elizabeth Warren and nine colleagues introduced a bill to bar presidents, vice presidents, and senior officials from owning or controlling a bank — a direct answer to the World Liberty Financial charter. The Pentagon separately ordered thirty major universities, including Harvard, MIT, and Johns Hopkins, to sever foreign academic partnerships within two weeks or risk losing federal funding. The Justice Department announced it would deploy 1,000 election monitors to polling places for the midterms, citing unspecified voter-fraud concerns — a deployment far larger than the agency's historical practice. And Disney formally joined ABC's suit against the FCC, filing a complaint citing more than 600 document requests and describing a chilling effect on programming decisions; it was the first time in more than fifty years that a major broadcaster had faced this kind of early license review.

**Wednesday**

Wednesday offered one of the week's clearer instances of judicial pushback. The Fourth Circuit Court of Appeals affirmed a preliminary injunction limiting immigration enforcement at Quaker, Sikh, and Baptist houses of worship, finding the administration's 2025 enforcement policy likely violated the Religious Freedom Restoration Act. The same day, reporting surfaced describing Christian nationalists with influence in the administration advocating repeal of the Nineteenth Amendment in favor of household voting that would defer to men — a proposal aimed at over a century of constitutional suffrage protection. The week's pattern of attacks on the press also escalated: White House communications director Steve Cheung used vulgar insults against Senator Ossoff, Trump attached derisive nicknames to him, and the official White House rapid-response account attacked a CNN reporter by invoking her children.

**Thursday**

Thursday was the busiest day of the week and, in some ways, its clearest illustration of institutions pulling in opposite directions. Courts blocked two separate executive actions: a federal judge halted the transfer of 17 million commercial driver's license records to federal custody, and another blocked the Department of Homeland Security and FEMA from imposing DEI-related conditions on disaster-relief grants to Salem, Oregon, finding likely violations of separation of powers.

At the same time, the Justice Department defended its prosecution of former FBI Director James Comey, arguing in court filings that his seashell Instagram post could reasonably be read as a coded threat against the president — a prosecution his lawyers continue to call vindictive. Documents obtained through a Freedom of Information Act request showed FCC chair Brendan Carr working closely with the White House and right-wing media figures on license matters even as he oversaw renewal decisions affecting ABC. And reporting deepened on aide Natalie Harp, revealing she had worked at the White House for more than a year without a required security clearance while serving as a key filter of information reaching the president — a lapse that drew bipartisan concern from national security officials, regardless of the personal dynamics also being reported. Trump, meanwhile, gave the press a tour of extensive White House renovations that included materials donated by a campaign contributor, and separately staged a high-speed car race that required modifications to Washington's streets.

**Friday**

The week closed with a Supreme Court intervention that crystallized its earlier posture. Chief Justice Roberts stayed a lower court's order that had blocked construction of Trump's $400 million White House ballroom pending congressional approval, allowing the project to resume while the Court considers the underlying case — even though the appellate court had found the project likely required congressional sign-off.

The Pentagon, separately, fired the publisher, editor, and a reporter of Stars and Stripes, the military's own news outlet, for insubordination following its coverage of conditions aboard the USS Abraham Lincoln and staff defense of editorial independence. The Department of Homeland Security dramatically retreated from its earlier claims about non-citizen voting, admitting after meeting with Nevada officials that it had manually confirmed only 185 non-citizens on the state's voter rolls — down from an earlier claim of nearly 16,000, itself a fraction of a national figure of roughly 278,000 the agency had floated. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent formally ended beneficial-ownership disclosure requirements under the 2021 Corporate Transparency Act, a law originally passed over presidential veto to combat money laundering. And federal budget cuts that reduced food-safety inspection capacity by more than 40 percent were linked to the largest cyclospora outbreak in U.S. history, as gas prices approached record highs and school lunch costs rose 11 percent.

**Assessment**

Read end to end, the week does not describe a single institution failing uniformly. It describes several institutions choosing differently under similar pressure. The Fourth Circuit limited enforcement at houses of worship; district courts blocked a data transfer and grant conditions built on shaky legal footing; the Supreme Court declined to revisit a jury's finding in the Carroll case. But the Attorney General publicly declined to promise independence, the FCC's chair coordinated with the White House against a network he regulates, and the Supreme Court's own stay let a presidential construction project proceed over an appellate court's constitutional concerns.

Alongside these institutional questions ran a due-process crisis in immigration courts, a foreign policy driven by threats and impulse rather than settled process, and a financial picture in which oversight tools were dismantled even as self-dealing surfaced in plain view. None of this occurred by accident; each instance involved a choice by a specific office or official. The record of who chose to check power, and who chose not to, is now part of the public account of how this period was governed.
```
