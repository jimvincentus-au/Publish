# The Week the Government Was Taken Apart From the Inside

*A frozen federal budget, a private takeover of the Treasury, purges at the FBI and DOJ, and courts scrambling to hold the line*

![Header image](week03-digest-featured.jpg)

```markdown
The first week of February 2025 began with an extraordinary concentration of executive action and ended with courts, unions, and career officials straining to hold the line against it. In the span of seven days, the federal government experienced a spending freeze and its reversal, the gutting of independent oversight bodies, the effective dismantling of a federal agency, a private takeover of Treasury payment systems, three separate judicial rulings against the same executive order, and a new set of sanctions aimed at an international court. The throughline was consistent: rapid assertions of executive power, followed by institutional and legal pushback that only partially caught up by week's end.

The weekend opened with an attempt to freeze trillions of dollars in federal grants and loans, a move that disrupted Medicaid, childcare, and other programs before a federal judge blocked it and the administration rescinded its own memo amid public confusion. It was the first sign of a pattern that would recur throughout the week — bold executive action, swift real-world disruption, and an eventual, often grudging, retreat under legal pressure. That same weekend, eighteen inspectors general were fired without the notice required by law, board members at the NLRB and EEOC were dismissed before their terms ended, and a new order stripped civil service job protections while pressuring roughly two million federal employees toward resignation. The effect was to remove, in a single motion, many of the internal watchdogs designed to check executive misconduct.

USAID became an early casualty of the broader consolidation. The administration moved to fold the independent, congressionally established agency into the State Department, placing security officials who resisted DOGE's access on leave, locking staff out of headquarters, and having Secretary of State Marco Rubio declare himself acting administrator. The action foreshadowed a theme that would define the week: agencies and systems built with statutory independence being absorbed into direct executive control, often over the objections of the career staff meant to administer them.

The weekend also saw Trump issue pardons to roughly 1,600 people connected to the January 6 attack on the Capitol, nearly the entire universe of those convicted or charged, while a separate executive order attempted to reinterpret the 14th Amendment's guarantee of birthright citizenship. Both actions drew immediate legal and political opposition, and the citizenship order in particular would become one of the week's central storylines, as three separate federal judges — appointed by presidents of both parties — moved to block it over the following days. Simultaneously, new tariffs of 25 percent on Canada and Mexico and 10 percent on China were declared under emergency powers, prompting swift retaliatory measures from all three countries before a partial one-month delay was negotiated in exchange for border security concessions.

Rounding out the weekend, the Pentagon began rotating established outlets like the New York Times and NPR out of its press pool in favor of more favorable coverage, the White House denied Reuters access to a cabinet meeting, and a HuffPost reporter was removed from the press pool altogether. Executive orders also targeted transgender athletes and federal use of gender pronouns, while decentralized protest movements such as 50501 and Tesla Takedown began organizing nationally, and Democratic state attorneys general launched what would become a coordinated litigation campaign eventually producing more than 170 court rulings against administration actions.

By Monday, the tariffs on Mexico and Canada were formally paused until March 4, with the administration reserving the right to reimpose them at will — a resolution that left the underlying leverage over trade policy intact even as the immediate crisis eased. Far more consequential was the deepening of Elon Musk's DOGE team's access to Treasury payment systems, which manage roughly $6 trillion annually. Career officials had resisted, but DOGE-affiliated staff obtained access anyway, prompting federal employee unions to sue over what they characterized as violations of federal privacy law. The same day brought a new executive order directing the Treasury and Commerce Departments to draft a plan for a U.S. sovereign wealth fund within 90 days, with suggestions it could facilitate a TikTok purchase — a novel financial vehicle assembled without congressional authorization. Higher education associations sued over DEI-related funding threats, and two groups of FBI agents sued the Justice Department to block the collection of information on agents who had worked January 6 investigations, an early sign of the retaliatory pattern that would harden as the week went on. North Carolina, meanwhile, enacted criminal penalties for mask-wearing and road-blocking at protests, and Secretary of State Rubio announced that El Salvador had offered to imprison deportees of any nationality, including U.S. citizens, for a fee.

Tuesday brought the judiciary's first major counterpunch. A federal judge reaffirmed the block on the spending freeze as unconstitutional, while judges in Maryland and Washington state separately halted enforcement of the birthright citizenship order — the first two of what would become three consecutive rulings against it. A federal judge also issued a temporary restraining order stopping the transfer of transgender women from federal women's prisons to men's facilities, preserving hormone therapy access for sixteen incarcerated individuals and finding the underlying policy discriminatory. Even as courts pushed back, DOGE's control over Treasury systems deepened further, enabling the cancellation of the free IRS Direct File tax-filing program — a concrete illustration of how much authority the team had assumed without formal accountability. The CDC and other health agencies were separately ordered to purge public resources on contraception, gender-affirming care, and HIV. The Senate advanced Robert F. Kennedy Jr.'s nomination to lead Health and Human Services along party lines and confirmed Chris Wright, a climate skeptic, as energy secretary. In foreign policy, the administration withdrew the United States from the UN Human Rights Council, halted funding to the Palestinian refugee agency UNRWA, and issued a memorandum relaunching a maximum-pressure campaign against Iran, directing federal agencies to drive Iranian oil exports to zero and pursue Iranian-linked networks domestically.

Wednesday combined a formal executive order banning transgender athletes from women's sports — directing federal agencies to enforce this reading of Title IX and threatening funding cuts to noncompliant schools — with a second nationwide injunction against the birthright citizenship order, this one from Judge Deborah Boardman. Senate Democrats staged an all-night filibuster against Russell Vought's nomination to lead the Office of Management and Budget, citing fears that a key architect of Project 2025 would use the position to target Medicare, Medicaid, and Social Security; his confirmation proceeded along party lines two days later regardless. The day's most unexpected development came during a joint press conference with Israeli Prime Minister Netanyahu, when Trump announced that the United States would "take over" Gaza, administering and redeveloping the territory — a proposal that drew bipartisan criticism and visible unease even among his own advisors. Elsewhere, West Point disbanded twelve student cultural and identity organizations, including the Society of Women Engineers and an LGBTQ+ support group, following DEI-related orders. The Justice Department ended civil rights investigations and police reform agreements tied to the killings of Breonna Taylor and George Floyd in Louisville and Minneapolis. And the Senate confirmed Pam Bondi as attorney general largely along party lines, positioning her to lead a Justice Department already reshaped by personnel purges — a role she would move on immediately.

Thursday opened with an executive order sanctioning officials of the International Criminal Court, blocking their assets and barring their entry into the United States over the court's investigation into alleged war crimes by U.S. and Israeli personnel. Bondi wasted little time asserting her new authority, eliminating the Justice Department's foreign bribery unit and its Kleptocracy Initiative and scaling back enforcement of the Foreign Agents Registration Act, redirecting resources toward drug and smuggling cases instead. The Justice Department separately sued Chicago and Illinois over their sanctuary immigration laws, escalating a federal-state conflict over enforcement authority. In North Carolina, Judge Griffin sought to discard roughly 60,000 ballots in an effort to overturn a certified state Supreme Court race, prompting public campaigns to defend the result. Protests over Musk's Treasury access and broader concerns about a private takeover of government functions intensified nationwide, accompanied by reports that ICE had altered timestamps on years-old press releases to make them appear as fresh enforcement actions, and by Trump's public call for CBS to lose its broadcast license over an edited "60 Minutes" interview with Kamala Harris. Amid this, a federal judge issued an order restricting Treasury access to two DOGE-affiliated staff on a read-only basis pending further litigation — a narrower but still significant judicial check.

That check hardened by Friday, when a federal judge issued an emergency order halting DOGE's access to Treasury systems altogether and ordering the destruction of any downloaded records, prompting Musk and Vice President Vance to publicly suggest the administration might simply ignore the ruling. It was the most forceful judicial intervention of the week, and it tested directly whether the executive branch would comply with a court order it disagreed with. The same day, Judge John Coughenour, a Reagan appointee, issued the third ruling against the birthright citizenship order, calling it "clearly unconstitutional" and criticizing the administration's disregard for the rule of law — a rebuke made more striking by the ideological range of the three judges who had now reached the same conclusion. The administration also suspended aid to South Africa over its land expropriation law while directing the State Department to prioritize resettlement of Afrikaner refugees, escalating diplomatic tension through a reshaping of refugee policy along ethnic lines. After a weeklong standoff, the FBI complied with a Justice Department demand to submit details on 5,000 to 6,000 agents connected to January 6 investigations, a disclosure that, despite excluding names, raised concerns about retaliation against career law enforcement personnel. The week closed with further censorship measures: a Department of Defense memo ordering military schools to purge books and lessons tied to gender and race, affecting 67,000 military-family students; the Education Department's decision to stop investigating school book bans; OSHA's order to destroy workplace safety publications flagged for unrelated keyword matches during a DEI purge; and the firing of the Archivist of the United States, whose office had played a role in retrieving classified documents from Mar-a-Lago.

Taken as a whole, the week illustrated a government being reshaped from the inside at a pace that outstripped the ordinary machinery of oversight. Independent watchdogs were removed before they could object, a private team gained hands-on access to the nation's payment infrastructure before the courts could catch up, and a constitutional guarantee was challenged by executive order before the ink on the order had dried. But the week also showed where resistance held: judges blocked the funding freeze and struck down the citizenship order three times over; unions and attorneys general filed suit after suit; and by Friday, a court order had forced at least a temporary halt to DOGE's Treasury access, even as the administration's response suggested compliance was not guaranteed. Whether that judicial and civic pushback could keep pace with the speed of executive action remained, at week's end, an open question — one the weeks to come would begin to answer.
```
