# This Week in Democracy: Vaccines Rolled Back, Maps Rejected, and a Fight Over Who Controls the Facts

*A week of immigration crackdowns, a rare bipartisan pushback on gerrymandering, and an executive order aimed at silencing state AI rules*

![Header image](week47-digest-featured.jpg)

```markdown
This week in December brought two competing currents into sharp relief: an administration testing how far executive power could reach into immigration, media, health policy, and the machinery of elections, and a scattering of institutions—courts, a Republican-led state Senate, grand juries—pushing back with real effect. Neither current dominated entirely. By week's end, the country had a clearer picture of where the executive branch's ambitions ran up against resistance, and where they did not.

The weekend set the tone. After a shooting involving an Afghan national, the administration moved quickly to pause asylum decisions for Afghans and freeze immigration applications from 19 other countries, with U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services suspending Afghan-related requests almost immediately. The speed of the response, applied by nationality rather than individual circumstance, raised early due-process questions that would recur throughout the week. At the same time, the CDC's Advisory Committee on Immunization Practices—reshaped by appointees aligned with Health Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr.—voted to end the decades-old universal recommendation that newborns receive the hepatitis B vaccine, shifting the decision to parental discretion. President Trump also pardoned Democratic Representative Henry Cuellar and his wife on bribery charges connected to Azerbaijan and a Mexican bank, then publicly chided Cuellar for not switching parties—an unusually candid suggestion that loyalty, not just the merits of the case, factored into the decision. Elsewhere, Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth faced fresh scrutiny after a Pentagon inspector general found he had shared sensitive strike details over the unclassified Signal app, adding to bipartisan resignation calls he continued to resist with Trump's backing. The New York Times, meanwhile, sued the Pentagon over new rules requiring journalists to promise not to seek unauthorized material, arguing the policy had already pushed out established outlets in favor of friendlier ones. And the administration rescinded protections that had shielded non-citizen military veterans from deportation, while the VA began compiling an internal report on its non-citizen workforce—raising the possibility that data on people who had served in uniform could be funneled into enforcement efforts.

By Monday, the questions about executive authority sharpened considerably. Hegseth asserted that the president could order military strikes without congressional authorization, a claim that took on added weight when the Pentagon opened an investigation into Senator Mark Kelly and five other Democratic lawmakers who had urged troops to refuse illegal orders. Trump went further, suggesting the senators' conduct could amount to "seditious behavior, punishable by death." Taken together, the two moves—claiming unilateral war powers while threatening lawmakers who invoked a long-standing military obligation to refuse unlawful commands—tested the separation of powers on two fronts at once. Elsewhere Monday, Alina Habba resigned as the top federal prosecutor in New Jersey after courts ruled her interim appointment invalid without Senate confirmation, though the Justice Department signaled she might return if the ruling were reversed—a reminder that constitutional confirmation requirements still carried weight even amid efforts to work around them. The EPA, under Administrator Lee Zeldin, loosened toxic chemical regulations and moved to unwind PFAS drinking water limits. Consumer boycotts gained visibility, with a 220-group coalition claiming to have redirected some $109 billion toward small businesses, while Home Depot and Target faced separate pressure campaigns tied to ICE arrests and diversity policy rollbacks. The White House launched a campaign soliciting public complaints about media bias, even as CNN and CNBC began weaving prediction-market data into their coverage, blurring the line between reporting and speculation. Trump also cleared the way for Nvidia to sell advanced AI chips to China in exchange for a quarter of the proceeds flowing to the U.S. government, a deal that drew bipartisan alarm over national security. And House Speaker Mike Johnson quietly stripped a previously bipartisan-approved IVF coverage provision for military families from the annual defense bill.

Tuesday brought the week's most consequential single day for institutional structure. The Supreme Court heard arguments testing a New Deal-era precedent that shields independent agency heads from at-will removal, in a case stemming from Trump's firing of Federal Trade Commission member Rebecca Slaughter—even as Trump proceeded to fire her outright while the justices were still deliberating. A civil rights coalition alleged beatings, sexual abuse, and unlawful deportations at the Fort Bliss detention facility, which held more than 2,700 people; the Department of Homeland Security denied the claims. More than 200 former Justice Department civil rights employees issued a public letter accusing political leadership of purging career staff and redirecting the division's mission toward priorities like voter-fraud investigations, away from its traditional civil rights enforcement work. In the corporate world, Jared Kushner's Affinity Partners helped arrange $24 billion in Middle Eastern sovereign wealth funding for Paramount's hostile bid to acquire Warner Bros. Discovery, with Trump publicly favoring that bid over a competing offer from Netflix—an arrangement that intertwined presidential family business interests, foreign capital, and a major media company all at once. The administration also accused Federal Reserve Governor Lisa Cook of mortgage fraud, even as records surfaced showing Trump had made similar claims about his own properties decades earlier without consequence. And the redistricting fights that would come to define the week's most surprising turn continued to build: organizers in Missouri submitted more than 300,000 signatures to force a referendum against a gerrymandered map, even as the Supreme Court allowed Texas's contested map to proceed for the 2026 elections.

Wednesday delivered the week's clearest counterweight to executive pressure. Despite direct threats from Trump, Vice President Vance, and Don Trump Jr. to withhold federal funding, Indiana's Republican-controlled state Senate voted 31-19 to reject a new congressional map drawn to favor Republicans. Tim Chapman, co-founder of the Heritage Foundation, went so far as to call the coercion unconstitutional. It was a rare instance of a legislative body within the president's own party absorbing direct pressure and declining to yield—a genuine surprise in a week otherwise dominated by expanding executive assertion. The same day, a federal judge ruled that the administration's federalized deployment of National Guard troops in Los Angeles had been unlawful, rejecting the argument that immigration protests amounted to rebellion, and ordered the troops returned to California's governor. Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick unveiled a proposal to sell U.S. citizenship for $1 million through a so-called "Trump Gold Card," drawing criticism for treating legal belonging as a purchasable commodity. In the Senate, neither Democratic nor Republican healthcare proposals reached the 60 votes needed to pass, leaving roughly 22 million Americans facing potential premium increases of up to 114 percent, or outright loss of coverage, as Affordable Care Act subsidies neared expiration. Smaller but telling: the administration announced plans to replace the winning photographs on national park annual passes with an image of Trump himself, prompting a lawsuit from the Center for Biological Diversity, while Trump toured the country insisting the economy deserved an "A-plus-plus-plus-plus-plus" grade even as only 31 percent of adults approved of his economic management.

Thursday's dominant story was a sweeping executive order aimed squarely at states' authority over a rapidly growing technology. Trump signed an order establishing a federal AI Litigation Task Force to challenge state AI laws and tie federal funding to compliance with a national standard, following the collapse of similar efforts in Congress and heavy lobbying from OpenAI and the venture firm Andreessen Horowitz. The move would, if it holds up in court, concentrate authority over AI regulation in the executive branch at the direct expense of state legislatures. The same day, the House passed a $900 billion defense bill that pushed back against several of the administration's recent moves—mandating disclosure on Caribbean boat strikes, withholding part of Hegseth's travel budget until strike footage is released, and reinforcing European troop commitments contrary to the administration's stated foreign policy direction. Courts, too, kept checking executive and prosecutorial reach: a federal judge ordered Kilmar Ábrego García released from ICE custody, a grand jury declined for a second time to indict New York Attorney General Letitia James on mortgage fraud charges, and prosecutors dropped a related bribery case involving a former Fox executive. Meanwhile, FDA officials under Kennedy's influence signaled forthcoming changes to childhood vaccine policy based on unconfirmed claims of deaths linked to COVID vaccination, drawing sharp criticism from former FDA leaders over the lack of supporting evidence. Senator Ruben Gallego gave the Justice Department a one-week deadline to release files related to Jeffrey Epstein, and a report from the Network Contagion Research Institute found that extremist figure Nick Fuentes's online reach had been artificially amplified through coordinated, partly foreign-sourced manipulation.

Friday closed the week with the Indiana story still unresolved in its aftermath. Rather than accepting the Senate's rejection, the administration and its allies, including Vance, kept pressuring Indiana legislators, and Governor Mike Braun vowed to challenge those who had opposed the map—an escalation that showed a willingness to keep applying coercion even after a clear legislative defeat. A federal judge issued a new restraining order shielding Ábrego García from re-detention, just hours before he was due to appear at an ICE office, extending judicial protection in a case that had already drawn repeated court intervention. ICE detentions expanded further: agents detained Afghan asylum seekers who were complying with court-ordered proceedings, and arrested a Muslim photojournalist, Ya'akub Vijandre, over his social media posts after revoking his DACA status. Churches around the country responded with nativity displays—including a handcuffed baby Jesus—as a form of public protest against enforcement practices. The National Trust for Historic Preservation sued Trump over the demolition of the White House's East Wing to make way for a $300 million ballroom, alleging the project bypassed required federal review and congressional approval. House Democrats released nearly 100,000 photographs from the Epstein estate featuring Trump, Bill Clinton, and Steve Bannon, and separately pressed for disclosure of the classified portion of a report by former special counsel Jack Smith. Representative Brian Fitzpatrick launched a discharge petition to force a House vote on extending ACA subsidies after Speaker Johnson declined to schedule one, a maneuver that will require Democratic votes to succeed. And Trump began a multi-stop tour aimed at defending his economic record against declining approval numbers, continuing to blame his predecessor for price increases.

Looked at as a whole, the week showed an administration probing the limits of executive authority on nearly every front—immigration, war powers, agency independence, technology regulation, and the shape of congressional maps themselves—while a handful of institutions demonstrated that those limits still exist in practice, if unevenly. Courts blocked a domestic military deployment and twice protected the same detained man from re-arrest. A grand jury twice declined to indict a political target. And in Indiana, a Republican legislature under direct pressure from its own party's leadership chose institutional resistance over compliance, even as that same administration made clear it intended to keep pushing. Set against expanding immigration enforcement, weakened vaccine guidance, a stalled healthcare system, and growing questions about who controls both the airwaves and the algorithms shaping public information, the week left an unfinished argument about where authority in American governance actually resides—an argument that courts, legislatures, and voters will keep having well beyond this single week's news cycle.
```
