# This Week in Democracy: Tariffs, Judges, and a Widening Regulatory Purge

*A week of executive trade power, contested court appointments, funding pressure on universities, and mounting scrutiny of the Epstein-Maxwell files*

![Header image](week28-digest-featured.jpg)

```markdown
This week ran from July 26 to August 1, and it accumulated pressure on nearly every institutional front at once: trade policy conducted almost entirely by presidential decree, a judicial confirmation pushed through over documented objections, university funding used as a bargaining chip, immigration enforcement widening its reach into legally resident populations, and the Epstein-Maxwell case producing a steady stream of subpoenas, transfers, and shifting explanations. No single event defined the week. Instead, the pattern was cumulative — a series of moves that, taken together, tested how much authority could be concentrated in the executive branch before courts, Congress, or the public pushed back.

The weekend set the tone. Staff at the Department of Government Efficiency began using an AI tool to identify federal regulations for elimination, with a stated goal of cutting half of the roughly 200,000 rules on the books by January. The tool had already been deployed at HUD and the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau, and its rollout signaled an approach to deregulation that relied on automation rather than the normal notice-and-comment process. Alongside it, Trump signed a rescissions package clawing back previously appropriated funds — the first such action in decades — while budget director Russell Vought floated the idea of "pocket rescissions," letting approved funds simply expire without being spent. Both moves raised direct questions about compliance with the Impoundment Control Act and would resurface later in the week.

The weekend also brought a striking political demand: Trump called for the prosecution of Kamala Harris, Beyoncé, Oprah Winfrey, and Al Sharpton over unsubstantiated claims of paid campaign endorsements, targeting four prominent Black public figures without a clear legal basis. He also urged the revocation of NBC's and ABC's broadcast licenses, falsely describing them as arms of the Democratic Party. Elsewhere, a Florida traffic stop escalated into the detention of a U.S. citizen who was recorded making threatening remarks by officers and ultimately convicted of obstruction for filming the encounter, and a New Jersey defendant filed the first legal challenge to Alina Habba's authority as acting U.S. attorney — a dispute that would grow through the week. The New York Times also reported that renovation costs for the Qatar-gifted Air Force One had been folded into a nearly billion-dollar transfer nominally earmarked for nuclear missile modernization, obscuring the expense from public view.

By Monday, the week's most consequential threads began to interlock. Vice President Vance argued that American citizenship should be rooted in ancestry and cultural heritage rather than adherence to founding ideals, suggesting that descendants of Civil War-era soldiers held a stronger claim to the country than other citizens — a framing DHS reinforced hours later by posting the 1872 painting "American Progress" with nationalist captioning, alongside a separate case in which an artist's painting was altered and used without permission under an ideological title. The Justice Department opened an investigation into Duke University over alleged race-based practices in law journal editor selection, a probe that foreshadowed the funding freeze that would land two days later. The Office of Personnel Management issued guidance permitting federal employees to promote their religious views in the workplace, shifting long-standing norms around church-state separation in government offices. Attorney General Bondi filed a misconduct complaint against Judge James Boasberg over his public comments about the administration, a move widely read as retaliation for his earlier rulings against it. The dispute over Alina Habba's legitimacy as acting U.S. attorney escalated further, forcing the cancellation of federal criminal proceedings in New Jersey while the underlying legal question remained unresolved. A federal judge separately blocked a Medicaid cutoff for Planned Parenthood clinics nationwide, preserving reimbursements while litigation over the funding provision continued. North Carolina's Republican legislature moved to override eleven vetoes touching DEI, immigration, guns, and the environment, while a hearing on a voter-access bill was rescheduled in a way that limited public attendance. And the Epstein-Maxwell story took its first major turn of the week: Ghislaine Maxwell filed an emergency appeal to the Supreme Court arguing that Jeffrey Epstein's earlier plea deal should have shielded her from prosecution, even as officials met with her directly and pardon speculation continued to circulate.

Tuesday deepened several of these threads while opening new ones. The administration terminated the CHNV parole program and raised its immigration arrest target to 3,000 per day, moves that together threatened to strip legal status from more than a million people and strain industries dependent on immigrant labor. EPA Administrator Lee Zeldin announced plans to revoke the 2009 endangerment finding that established greenhouse gases as a public health threat — the legal foundation for most federal climate regulation under the Clean Air Act. In the Senate, Emil Bove was confirmed to a lifetime federal appeals court seat in a narrow party-line vote, despite whistleblower allegations that he had urged defiance of court orders and misled Congress; more than eighty judges had registered concerns about his fitness for the bench. Congressional security spending also expanded sharply in the wake of political violence: the House doubled personal security limits for members, Minnesota agreed to fund enhanced home security for a lawmaker following an assassination, and several states moved to strip lawmakers' home addresses from public websites. The Epstein-Maxwell thread advanced again as the House Oversight Committee subpoenaed Maxwell for deposition testimony but rejected her request for immunity, while DOJ officials disclosed that only two witnesses had testified before the original grand jury. Separately, the DOJ requested detailed personal data — including Social Security and driver's license numbers — on noncitizens listed on California voter rolls, and Texas sued a New York county clerk over her refusal to enforce a fine against a doctor accused of mailing abortion medication across state lines. Enforcement stories that day also included the arrest of a South Korean scientist with a valid green card, held at San Francisco International Airport and denied attorney access, alongside a 20-year permanent resident facing deportation over an old, previously disclosed arrest.

Wednesday brought the funding pressure on Duke to a head: the administration froze $108 million in research funding, with HHS and the Education Department demanding a new oversight committee and threatening further legal action. That freeze would prove to be the opening chapter of a story that widened dramatically by week's end. The same day, Trump announced a $200 million White House ballroom, funded by himself and private donors, with the projected cost having already doubled from an initial estimate of $100 million. On trade, Trump signed an order imposing a 40 percent additional tariff on Brazilian imports, explicitly citing Brazil's prosecution of former president Jair Bolsonaro as a national security concern — a rationale that blurred the line between economic policy and retaliation over a foreign government's domestic judicial proceedings. Tulsi Gabbard called publicly for the prosecution of Barack Obama and other former officials over what she described as a "treasonous conspiracy" tied to 2016 Russia intelligence findings, drawing sharp pushback from former officials who called the framing baseless. Congressional oversight of immigration enforcement also hit a wall: Representative Greg Stanton was denied entry to an Arizona ICE facility during an attempted oversight visit, part of a broader lawsuit by a dozen House Democrats challenging new advance-notice requirements for congressional visits to detention sites. The DOJ, meanwhile, pushed to unseal grand jury transcripts from the Epstein and Maxwell indictments, continuing to reveal how limited the original grand jury proceedings had been. The Bove confirmation reached its formal conclusion with a 50-49 Senate vote, closing a two-stage process that had unfolded over consecutive days despite sustained opposition from judges and whistleblowers. And in a separate but related maneuver, the DOJ named interim U.S. attorneys in Nevada and Los Angeles as "acting" just before their terms expired, a procedural step that prevented district judges from installing replacements and extended the pattern first seen with Alina Habba's appointment in New Jersey.

Thursday's dominant story was economic. Following a July jobs report showing only 73,000 new jobs and steep downward revisions to prior months, Trump fired Bureau of Labor Statistics Commissioner Erika McEntarfer, citing a need for "accurate" numbers — a justification widely interpreted as political retaliation against unfavorable data rather than a response to any demonstrated inaccuracy. The same day, Columbia University agreed to pay $221 million and abandon race-conscious hiring practices in exchange for the restoration of $1.3 billion in federal funding that had been withheld over antisemitism allegations, a settlement that stood in direct continuity with the funding freeze imposed on Duke just one day earlier — two related but distinct instances of federal leverage producing different outcomes at different institutions. Trade policy escalated further as Trump raised tariffs on Canadian goods from 25 to 35 percent, citing both fentanyl cooperation and Canada's stance on Palestine, while a separate order modified reciprocal tariff rates across additional trading partners. Trump also publicly criticized Federal Reserve Chair Jerome Powell over interest rates while signing a 50 percent tariff on copper imports, extending pressure simultaneously onto monetary policy and trade costs. And reporting emerged that the administration had illegally impounded funds Congress had already appropriated — across foreign aid, veterans' benefits, and other programs — a direct continuation of the rescissions fight that had opened the week.

Friday closed the week with several major developments landing at once. The administration announced sweeping changes to the Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program, shifting administrative costs onto states, expanding work requirements, and restricting eligibility for non-citizens — changes analysts warned would deepen poverty and hunger, and which stood apart from the earlier Medicaid litigation as a distinct track of low-income assistance under pressure. The tariff campaign that had run through the week culminated in a base reciprocal rate of 10 to 15 percent alongside specific negotiated arrangements with Japan, South Korea, the European Union, Vietnam, Indonesia, Pakistan, and the Philippines, with additional rates still threatened against India and Mexico — a sprawling reordering of trade relationships conducted almost entirely through executive orders, with a federal appeals court still weighing whether the underlying emergency authority was lawful at all. In North Carolina, the legislature followed through on its earlier plans, overriding eight vetoes including a bill easing restrictions on Duke Energy and an immigration enforcement measure, while introducing a new bill targeting transgender recognition. Sixteen states sued the administration over an executive order that described gender-affirming care for transgender youth as "mutilation" and threatened hospital funding, a suit filed only after several care centers had already shut down under the pressure. Conditions at the Everglades detention facility known as "Alligator Alcatraz" drew renewed scrutiny as detainees reported contaminated food and water, arriving the same day that Ghislaine Maxwell was quietly transferred to a lower-security prison in Texas — a juxtaposition that drew sharp criticism from victims' families without needing further comment. The week ended with dozens of pro-Palestinian protesters, including two elected officials, arrested outside Senator Chuck Schumer's New York office while demanding an end to bombing in Gaza and expanded humanitarian aid.

Taken as a whole, the week illustrated how quickly institutional guardrails can be tested when several forms of authority are exercised in parallel. Trade policy was conducted almost entirely by presidential proclamation, judicial appointments moved forward despite documented professional objections, university funding became a lever for policy change, and immigration enforcement reached further into legally resident and citizen populations alike. Courts, state attorneys general, and advocacy groups responded at nearly every turn — blocking a Medicaid cutoff, suing over healthcare restrictions, challenging prosecutorial appointments, and pressing for transparency in a long-unresolved criminal case — but the sheer volume of simultaneous action tested the capacity of those checks to keep pace. Whether measured in tariffs, funding freezes, or personnel removals, the week's throughline was the same: institutions built to slow or divide power were repeatedly met with actions designed to concentrate it, and the resolution of that tension — in the courts, in Congress, and in public opinion — remained unfinished as the week closed.
```
