This Week in Democracy, Week 66
War funding, immigration escalation, pressure on oversight, and new fights over records, media, and voting rules
The week opened with two stories that would shape nearly everything that followed: a widening war in Iran and a widening enforcement state at home. The administration asked Congress for $200 billion for the war even as reports raised questions about suspiciously timed market bets connected to the conflict. By the end of the weekend, officials were also conceding that the Strait of Hormuz was not open, that gas prices could remain high, and that the Strategic Petroleum Reserve was being used to manage the domestic fallout. What might have been framed as a foreign-policy emergency quickly became a test of congressional control, economic accountability, and public trust.
That same weekend, Democrats began organizing a response not only to the war but to the broader question of corruption and self-dealing. House Democrats introduced an anti-self-dealing bill and announced a task force focused on ethics and presidential misuse of public funds. These were not yet decisive actions, but they set the week’s terms: oversight would not be limited to policy disagreement. It would also turn on whether public office was being used for private advantage, and whether Congress still had the means to investigate that question.
Immigration policy, meanwhile, showed a harder edge before the workweek even began. ICE disclosed that it had deported 174 people who were renewing DACA protections in 2025, a stark sign of how little practical security deferred action now provided. In North Carolina, election officials advanced a measure allowing voter citizenship challenges and broader sharing of voter data with federal authorities. The two developments came from different policy areas, but they pointed in the same direction: less stability for immigrants and more aggressive use of administrative tools in the name of enforcement and election integrity.
The weekend also offered an early picture of how scrutiny itself would be managed. Trump held an Oval Office event with Joe Rogan and barred press questions, limiting the most basic form of accountability in a presidential setting. At the same time, Kash Patel moved from threatening legal action against *The Atlantic* to filing a $250 million defamation suit over reporting on his conduct. In the courts, the signals were mixed. An appeals court allowed White House ballroom construction to continue while litigation proceeded, but a federal judge blocked the Nexstar-Tegna merger, and another vacated an HHS threat to cut funding over gender-affirming care for minors. Even before Monday, it was clear that the judiciary would remain a central but uneven arena: sometimes slowing executive action, sometimes allowing it to continue.
By Monday, the war was generating both formal oversight and visible dissent. Senate Democrats prepared subpoenas tied to possible financial conflicts around the Iran war, and Representative Jamie Raskin opened an inquiry into Jared Kushner’s diplomatic role and his Saudi-linked business ties. Outside Congress, veterans and military family members were arrested while protesting the war inside the Cannon House Office Building after lawmakers failed to stop funding for the conflict. The same policy was now producing strain in several places at once: inside Congress, in the streets, and in the overlap between foreign policy and private financial interest.
Monday also showed how broadly executive power was being used. Trump granted clemency to January 6 participants, using the pardon power to erase consequences tied to an attack on democratic institutions. He also signed memorandums invoking defense-style authorities to expand domestic fossil-fuel production, alongside the psychedelic-treatment order that had surfaced over the weekend. Different in subject, these actions were similar in method: a presidency willing to use its powers across criminal accountability, industrial policy, and symbolic politics, often with little sign of restraint from the other branches.
A parallel fight over transparency widened the same day. Pam Bondi skipped a congressional subpoena tied to the Epstein investigation, while a separate lawsuit pressed the Justice Department over delayed FOIA processing. DOJ was already under criticism for missing deadlines under the Epstein Files Transparency Act. The significance of these disputes was larger than any one file set. Oversight depends on subpoenas being honored and records laws being followed. Monday suggested that both were becoming negotiable.
Immigration enforcement also broadened in more intimate ways. ICE detained the wife of a U.S. Army sergeant during an immigration appointment despite prior protection, while community groups responded with immigrants’ rights trainings and new lawsuits challenged immigration rules. At the administrative level, the Labor Department moved to rewrite or repeal more than 60 workplace rules affecting home-care workers, disabled workers, and mine safety. That was a reminder that the week’s story was not only about headline confrontations. It was also about quieter administrative changes that can shift risk, bargaining power, and legal protection without Congress passing a new law.
Tuesday was one of the week’s densest days, and immigration was at the center of it. The administration fired six more immigration judges, including judges who had blocked deportation orders in politically sensitive cases. Senate Republicans prepared a major funding package for ICE and Border Patrol, and the administration moved ahead with border-wall construction through Big Bend National Park despite local opposition and transparency concerns. The pattern was no longer just arrests or individual removals. It was the construction of a larger enforcement system through staffing, money, and physical infrastructure.
The same day, fights over surveillance and voter data entered a new legal phase. Congress passed a 10-day extension of Section 702 after failing to agree on a longer renewal, keeping warrantless surveillance authority alive while reform disputes continued. Common Cause sued DOJ over demands for unredacted statewide voter rolls, and American Oversight sued multiple agencies over withheld Palantir-related records. These cases linked two recurring concerns of the week: the state’s appetite for personal data and the growing reliance on litigation to force disclosure about how that power was being used.
Tuesday also brought more evidence that the administration was shrinking institutions meant to regulate markets and protect the public. It ended the CFPB headquarters lease early and sought to cut the agency to roughly a third of its former size. At the same time, Congress was still trying to extend Temporary Protected Status for Haitians, underscoring a widening gap between legislative efforts to preserve protections and executive efforts to narrow them. Fresh reporting also sharpened conflict-of-interest concerns around Iran policy by emphasizing that Kushner remained central to diplomacy while maintaining major Saudi-linked financial relationships. And in the information sphere, Meta removed AI-generated pro-MAGA influencer accounts, a TikTok network spread AI-generated Iranian military propaganda, and Trump dismissed a negative poll as rigged. The public was being asked to navigate war, surveillance, and elections in an environment where both synthetic content and political attacks on credibility were becoming routine.
On Wednesday, election administration and representation moved to the foreground. Virginia voters approved new congressional maps after days of buildup, a decision that could reshape the state’s House delegation. At the same time, the Republican National Committee sued Fairfax County over voter-identification procedures, and North Carolina Republicans moved to share voter data with the Trump administration. These were not ordinary campaign-season disputes. They were fights over the rules and data systems that structure who votes, how votes are validated, and how representation is drawn.
Wednesday also marked a sharper escalation in pressure on the press. The FBI’s investigation of *New York Times* reporter Elizabeth Williamson became public, with reports that agents searched databases and interviewed sources after she wrote about Kash Patel’s use of agency resources. That was a different order of threat from Patel’s private defamation suit against *The Atlantic*, which also remained active as House Democrats opened an inquiry into his conduct. One story involved a powerful official using litigation against a publication. The other involved federal investigative tools being turned toward a reporter. Together they showed how scrutiny of one official had produced both private and state-backed forms of pressure on journalism.
Elsewhere on Wednesday, censorship fights spread through schools, libraries, and state law. An Iowa court upheld restrictions affecting LGBTQ-related books and topics in schools, a new report found book bans had reached a record high in 2025, and the House Oversight Committee targeted LGBTQ content in a North Carolina school district. In Florida, Ron DeSantis signed laws blocking local DEI initiatives and local climate-related policies. These measures worked through different institutions—courts, legislatures, congressional committees—but converged on a common question: what identities, histories, and policy goals can remain visible in public institutions. The same day also brought more examples of money and access shaping outcomes, including a White House move toward a $500 million rescue package for Spirit Airlines, a Pentagon robotics contract tied to Eric Trump’s business interests, and a lawsuit by Justin Sun against the Trump family crypto venture World Liberty Financial.
Thursday was the week’s clearest escalation point on immigration. Senate Republicans advanced a $140 billion immigration funding plan. Reporting showed the administration had deployed large numbers of federal officers into Democratic-led cities and reassigned Homeland Security Investigations agents to deportation work. Most strikingly, the Justice Department was reportedly preparing denaturalization cases against hundreds of people. That moved the crackdown beyond border control, detention, and deportation into the legal security of citizenship itself. The week’s earlier signs—DACA insecurity, judge firings, new funding, city deployments—now looked like parts of a larger project.
Congress, meanwhile, showed both its willingness to investigate and its inability to reclaim authority over war. Democrats’ bill to end the Iran war was blocked by Republicans, leaving the conflict on executive terms. At the same time, the House Oversight Committee kept pressing Bondi over the Epstein files. The contrast mattered. Congress could still generate hearings, letters, and inquiries. But when it came to constraining war-making, it failed to reassert itself.
Thursday also turned administrative cuts into a governance story. A Senate Democratic report said layoffs and management decisions at the Social Security Administration had worsened wait times and service for seniors and disabled people. That gave concrete form to a pattern visible earlier in the week at the CFPB and Labor Department: reducing agencies is not only an ideological project. It changes whether people can actually receive benefits, get answers, or enforce rights. Civil society groups were under pressure as well. The DOJ’s indictment of the Southern Poverty Law Center continued to expand as a major story, while protest groups sued the National Park Service over threats to revoke a demonstration permit on the National Mall. Yet courts still offered some counterweight: public coverage reinforced a federal judge’s earlier ruling against HHS pressure on gender-affirming care. New reporting also tied a $2 million donation from the Yocha Dehe Wintun Nation to a live Interior Department fight over casino competition, another example of campaign money, agency discretion, and economic stakes converging.
By Friday, the federal government’s punitive turn was unmistakable. The Justice Department announced that federal executions could again use firing squads, electrocution, and gas asphyxiation as part of a broader effort to strengthen the federal death penalty. Coming after a week that had already included clemency for January 6 participants and repeated resistance to oversight, the announcement underscored a pattern in which executive power was being used both to absolve allies and to intensify coercion elsewhere.
Immigration law tightened further before the week closed. The Board of Immigration Appeals ruled that DACA status alone does not require termination of removal proceedings, potentially making it easier for the government to pursue deportation cases against DACA recipients. After the weekend’s disclosure that DACA renewers had been deported, and after Thursday’s reports of denaturalization planning, the ruling fit a clear trajectory: legal categories that once offered some practical security were being narrowed through adjudication as well as enforcement.
Friday also made transparency and recordkeeping one of the week’s clearest accountability fronts. Freedom of the Press Foundation and CREW sued over an Office of Legal Counsel opinion declaring the Presidential Records Act unconstitutional, arguing that White House recordkeeping practices now threatened the destruction of presidential records. Other litigation challenged detention expansion in Arizona, EPA delays on soot standards, and federal pressure on New York’s DMV, while a judge ordered release of records in another case. The point was not that every lawsuit would succeed. It was that watchdogs, advocacy groups, and courts were increasingly being asked to preserve the public record itself.
The week’s press and information fights also remained active to the end. Patel’s lawsuit against *The Atlantic* continued, while reporting clarified that the FBI had opened and then dropped an investigation into Elizabeth Williamson over her reporting on Patel. Even without charges, the combination of a major defamation suit and federal investigative scrutiny can chill reporting. And in a different but related sphere, the CDC blocked publication of a report showing COVID-19 vaccine effectiveness in reducing hospitalizations and emergency-room visits among healthy adults. That added scientific suppression to a week already marked by records disputes, AI propaganda, attacks on polling, and pressure on journalists.
Taken together, the week showed how several kinds of power reinforced one another. War policy generated domestic conflict-of-interest questions and economic strain. Immigration enforcement expanded from case-by-case actions into budgets, personnel, city deployments, and even citizenship status. Congress investigated but often failed to constrain. Courts sometimes checked executive action and sometimes allowed it to proceed. Watchdogs and civil society groups increasingly turned to litigation to preserve records, force disclosure, and protect protest and reporting. By Friday, the larger picture was not a single crisis but a governing pattern: more coercive capacity, weaker practical protections, and a growing struggle over whether the public can still see clearly enough to hold power to account.
