The Ballroom, the Shutdown, and the Nuclear Option
A week in which food aid, congressional oversight, press freedom, and constitutional term limits were all tested at once
```markdown
This was a week in which two Halloweens bookended a month of accumulating strain — one on the front end, with tear gas at a Chicago parade, and one on the back end, with immigration sweeps in three cities — while in between, food aid, historic preservation, congressional oversight, and constitutional term limits were all tested within the same seven days. The government shutdown, entering its fourth week, ran underneath everything else as a kind of connective tissue, joined by a demolished White House wing, a purge of ICE leadership, a wave of prosecutions against the president's critics, and open musings about a third term. Courts intervened repeatedly, sometimes successfully and sometimes not. Congress, and particularly the House, was often simply absent.
The weekend set the tone. The administration demolished the East Wing of the White House without approval from the National Capital Planning Commission, clearing ground for a $300 million ballroom funded by corporations and tech firms with business before the government — a reversal of an earlier promise not to tear down existing structures. At nearly the same moment, the Agriculture Department confirmed it would not tap a $6 billion reserve to keep food assistance flowing, despite earlier guidance saying that money could be used for exactly this kind of emergency, and it barred states from covering the gap with their own funds. Forty-two million Americans depend on that program. Then, on Sunday, ICE and Border Patrol agents deployed tear gas and pepper spray at a Halloween parade in Chicago's Old Irving Park neighborhood, tackling and arresting several people, including U.S. citizens, in front of children in costume — despite the governor's request to pause enforcement. The same weekend brought the detention of British journalist Sami Hamdi at a San Francisco airport, his visa revoked without notice and his political speech relabeled as terrorism sympathy, with no charges filed. Separately, reporting surfaced that a 60 Minutes producer had resigned earlier in the year under pressure from Paramount leadership to soften Gaza and Trump coverage ahead of a merger requiring federal approval — an early sign of a theme that would recur through the week. Trump, meanwhile, was reported joking privately that he was "the speaker and the president," prompting Senator Chris Murphy to warn publicly of "King-like powers" during the shutdown.
By Monday, the shutdown's food-aid standoff had hardened. Speaker Mike Johnson declared a $5 billion emergency fund "not legally available" for SNAP benefits, even as the House marked its 84th day in recess out of the last 96. The Justice Department separately argued in court that a funding lapse had voided lawmakers' legal right to inspect ICE detention facilities, effectively exempting the agency from congressional oversight during the shutdown. On a different front, more than 100 former Justice Department officials filed a brief describing the prosecution of former FBI Director James Comey as "an act of personal retribution by the President." Aboard Air Force One, Trump entertained the idea of running for vice president as a way around the 22nd Amendment — calling the maneuver "too cute" while also saying he "would love" a third term, language that continued to circulate alongside "Trump 2028" merchandise. The day's darker undercurrent came from Tucker Carlson's two-hour interview with white nationalist Nick Fuentes, in which antisemitic and pro-segregation statements went unchallenged, drawing condemnation from Ben Shapiro, Mitch McConnell, and Ted Cruz alike. Tennessee's Secretary of State ordered 181 public libraries to remove LGBTQ-themed children's books, citing state and federal directives, and at least one county closed its libraries for a week rather than comply piecemeal. Separately, Trump filed federal tort claims seeking $230 million from his own Justice Department over the Mar-a-Lago search and the Russia investigation — a claim that would be decided by his own appointees.
Tuesday concentrated more institutional retaliation into a single day than perhaps any other this year. The Justice Department indicted Comey, charged New York Attorney General Letitia James with mortgage fraud, brought an assault charge against Representative LaMonica McIver, and opened investigations into Senator Adam Schiff and the Democratic fundraising platform ActBlue. The same day, the administration reassigned at least a dozen ICE field office directors, replacing many with Border Patrol officials under a new "Commander-at-Large," Greg Bovino, who now reported directly to the Homeland Security secretary — a restructuring driven by an internal demand for 3,000 daily arrests. Trump then fired all members of both the National Capital Planning Commission and the Commission of Fine Arts, the two bodies with authority to review his ballroom project, eliminating any independent check just as reporting revealed that corporations including Amazon, Google, Microsoft, Meta, and Lockheed Martin — all of which had pledged in 2021 to withhold support from officials who challenged the 2020 election — had donated to the ballroom and to Trump's inaugural fund. Courts pushed back on several fronts that same day: judges blocked planned mass shutdown layoffs as likely "arbitrary, capricious" political retribution, disqualified a Trump-appointed U.S. attorney, and the Ninth Circuit agreed to rehear the Portland National Guard deployment case en banc. In Chicago, federal judge Sara Ellis confronted CBP's Bovino directly over the Halloween tear-gassing, ordering him to appear in court daily and undergo body-camera training after finding apparent violations of an earlier restraining order. Meanwhile, in the Senate, Democrats blocked a Republican spending bill for the thirteenth time, and Majority Leader Thune blocked a bipartisan effort by Senator Josh Hawley to fund SNAP directly.
Wednesday offered one of the week's few instances of visible bipartisan pushback in Congress: five Republicans joined Democrats in a 52-48 Senate vote to terminate Trump's 50% tariffs on Brazil, part of a growing pattern of resolutions against unilateral executive trade authority, even though the measure was expected to stall in the House. On the shutdown front, a coalition of 24 Democratic-led states sued the USDA over its refusal to release SNAP reserves, and reporting revealed the department had quietly deleted its own September guidance stating those funds were legally available. Trump, meanwhile, called for Jack Smith to be "investigated and put in prison" and named several other former officials for potential investigation, extending the week's pattern of using public rhetoric against perceived adversaries. A different story deepened: days after Trump pardoned Binance founder Changpeng Zhao, the exchange enabled trading of Trump-linked cryptocurrencies, and Trump personally held more than 15 percent of the token supply of one of them — a financial entanglement that would receive fuller scrutiny before the week was out. Separately, the administration briefed only Republican lawmakers on military strikes in the Eastern Pacific, prompting Senator Mark Warner to call the exclusion "indefensible and dangerous," while two DOJ prosecutors were placed on leave for describing the January 6 attack as a "mob of rioters." Independent analysts estimated the shutdown was costing the economy $7 to $14 billion, even as separate reporting showed ACA marketplace premiums were set to rise 26 percent in 2026.
Thursday's most striking development came from South Korea, where Trump ordered the Department of War to resume nuclear weapons testing "on an equal basis" with Russia and China, based on disputed claims about their programs, just minutes before a trade meeting with Xi Jinping — a reversal of a 33-year moratorium that a nuclear proliferation expert called factually unfounded. The Trump-Xi meeting itself produced a one-year framework on tariffs, rare earths, and soybean purchases, though reporting noted the deal largely restored pre-escalation conditions and that the promised soybean volumes were lower than what had been purchased under the previous administration. Domestically, the Seventh Circuit paused Judge Ellis's order requiring Bovino to report daily on use-of-force incidents, temporarily removing one of the only real-time judicial checks on enforcement conduct in Chicago, even as the Pentagon separately ordered every state to stand up rapid-response National Guard units for civil unrest by January. Lawmakers found themselves blocked from entering ICE field offices in several cities despite mandated oversight visits, while Pentagon officials working on Latin America operations were required to sign additional non-disclosure agreements. The Binance story gained further specificity: reporting showed a lawyer had simultaneously represented both the pardon effort and Trump's crypto venture, and that Binance's market capitalization had surged from $127 million to over $2 billion in the same period. Trump, responding to Senate Minority Leader Chuck Schumer's criticism of the China trip as "a total dud," posted that the remark was "almost treasonous." Senate Democrats, led by Adam Schiff, sent a letter demanding disclosure of the ballroom's donor list, naming Apple, Amazon, and Lockheed Martin among contractors with pending federal business.
Friday closed the week with its most consequential judicial intervention. Federal judges in Rhode Island and Massachusetts ruled that the administration's SNAP suspension was likely unlawful and ordered the USDA to use its contingency funds — directly contradicting Speaker Johnson's claim, made only hours earlier, that there was "no way" to fund the program. The ruling arrived just as benefits lapsed nationwide, forcing the executive branch to comply with congressional appropriations after weeks of insisting it had no legal path to act. At the same time, the enforcement surge that had opened the week returned on its final day: Homeland Security Secretary Kristi Noem refused Illinois Governor J.B. Pritzker's request to pause immigration operations over the Halloween holiday, and agents in costume masks operated in Los Angeles while National Guard troops and federal officers arrested five minors in Washington, D.C. Evanston's mayor reported that agents had assaulted residents. The Seventh Circuit formally issued its ruling curtailing the daily oversight of Bovino, reasoning that the arrangement improperly cast the district court as "an inquisitor" into executive personnel matters. The ACLU sued over conditions at the Broadview ICE facility, alleging detainees were denied private attorney calls, blocked from access by lawmakers and journalists, and denied food and medical care. Court testimony detailed how White House aide Stephen Miller had personally directed at least a dozen visa revocations targeting pro-Palestinian academics, part of a broader campaign soliciting public denunciations for further action — a direct echo of the Hamdi detention from the weekend before. Trump, for his part, urged Senate Republicans in all-capital letters to invoke the "nuclear option" and abolish the filibuster to end the shutdown, while Johnson announced the House would skip yet another full week of business, and continued to refuse to seat Representative-elect Adelita Grijalva, whose swearing-in was the final signature needed for a discharge petition on the Epstein files. Trump also repeated his third-term musings, with ally Steve Bannon claiming "many different alternatives" existed to bypass the 22nd Amendment. Smaller items rounded out the day: the FCC permanently raised rate caps on phone and video calls for incarcerated people, benefiting private prison-telecom firms tied to major Trump donors, and the Washington Post detailed how DHS had used misleadingly edited enforcement footage to promote its record, even as Trump told 60 Minutes he knew "nothing" about the crypto executive he had pardoned days earlier.
Taken together, the week showed an executive branch willing to test almost every available boundary at once — over public money, over enforcement conduct, over prosecutorial independence, over war powers, and over the constitutional limits on its own tenure — while the legislature best positioned to check it was, for much of the week, either absent, excluded, or unable to act. The judiciary intervened unevenly: forcing food aid to flow when the other branches would not, but also stepping back from oversight of an enforcement official whose agents had documented histories of using force against families and children. What emerged was less a single crisis than a pattern across many small ones — a demolished wing of a historic building, a defunded safety-net program, a purged enforcement bureaucracy, a pardoned executive whose fortunes rose with a president's family business, and a president publicly weighing whether the rules that were meant to end his time in office might not apply to him. Each piece, examined alone, might be explained away. Assembled together, across a single week, they describe an institution testing how far its guardrails can bend before something gives.
```
