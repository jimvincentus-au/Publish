# This Week in Democracy: A Holiday Week of Stalled Transparency and Unchecked Enforcement

*As Washington emptied out for the holidays, courts pushed back on executive overreach even as the Justice Department slow-walked the Epstein files and immigration enforcement expanded*

![Header image](week49-digest-featured.jpg)

```markdown
The week of December 20 to 26 unfolded as most of Washington quieted down for the holidays, but the pause did little to slow the collision between an executive branch testing its limits and courts and lawmakers trying to hold a line. Two threads ran through the week almost independently of each other: a Justice Department transparency process that kept unraveling by the day, and a series of court rulings that repeatedly checked the administration's use of force and funding against states and immigrants. Neither story resolved. Both simply hardened.

The weekend opened with the Justice Department's release of a heavily redacted batch of Epstein-related files, arriving after the December 19 deadline set by the Epstein Files Transparency Act had already passed. Almost immediately, the release generated its own controversy: files disappeared from the DOJ website without explanation, including a photograph of Trump with Epstein and Ghislaine Maxwell that was later restored. Lawmakers from both parties criticized the release as incomplete, with some raising the possibility of legal action or impeachment proceedings against DOJ leadership — an inauspicious start for a legally mandated disclosure that was supposed to close the book on a long-running controversy.

The same weekend brought a cluster of developments on separate tracks. The administration canceled $679 million in offshore wind funding, ended a $7 billion low-income solar program, and blocked California's plan to ban new gas-powered car sales by 2030 — using federal authority to override state climate policy and redirect the country's energy trajectory without new legislation. Congress, meanwhile, recessed for the holidays after four House Republicans joined Democrats to force a vote on extending ACA premium tax credits; Speaker Mike Johnson sent members home shortly after, effectively shelving both that healthcare push and further Epstein-related oversight. The Kennedy Center's Trump-appointed board voted to rename the institution the "Trump-Kennedy Center," a vote Rep. Joyce Beatty said she was muted during, setting up a lawsuit she would file later in the week. A mass shooting at Brown University left two dead and nine injured, and Trump drew bipartisan criticism for suggesting filmmaker Rob Reiner's death was connected to his political opposition, while a separate economic speech drew fact-checking scrutiny for its inaccuracies.

By Monday, the Epstein file story had progressed rather than settled. Reports surfaced that sixteen files had vanished from the DOJ site without explanation, that redactions had blacked out entire documents, and that unrelated material — including a misattributed photo of Bill Clinton — had been inserted into the release. Congressional Democrats formally condemned the department's non-compliance. On a separate track, CBS News editor-in-chief Bari Weiss pulled a fully vetted 60 Minutes segment examining the deportation of Venezuelan migrants to El Salvador's CECOT prison, a decision correspondent Sharyn Alfonsi and others characterized as political; the segment eventually aired through a Canadian broadcaster. This was its own story about editorial independence, distinct from the Epstein saga, even as both touched on the administration's relationship with disclosure and scrutiny. Also on Monday, the case of Vilma Palacios, a 22-year-old nursing school graduate held by ICE for six months despite no criminal record and a pending asylum claim, offered a quiet but telling illustration of how enforcement was playing out at the individual level.

Tuesday was the week's busiest day and the moment its two central threads came into sharpest relief. The administration unveiled a $170 billion immigration enforcement expansion, with plans for new detention facilities, expanded workplace raids, and increased deportation processing beginning in January; ICE separately reported that detention numbers had reached a record high of more than 68,400 people, many without criminal records. Business, faith, and civil rights groups objected. The same day, the Supreme Court delivered its first major check of the week, ruling 6-3 to block the administration's attempt to deploy federalized National Guard troops to Chicago for immigration enforcement, finding the government had not shown that regular forces were insufficient for the task. Lower courts added to the pattern: one judge blocked ICE from re-detaining Kilmar Abrego Garcia through the holiday, and Judge James Boasberg ordered the administration to either facilitate the return of 137 Venezuelan men deported to El Salvador's prison system or allow them to challenge their removal in U.S. courts.

Tuesday carried further weight beyond the two headline threads. The Department of Veterans Affairs ended abortion and abortion counseling services for veterans, including in cases of rape or incest, reversing Biden-era policy and allowing staff to opt out on moral or religious grounds. Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy threatened to withhold federal highway funding from states issuing driver's licenses to non-citizens. Vice President J.D. Vance told the AmericaFest conference that the United States is a "Christian nation," a claim critics said sat uneasily with the First Amendment's establishment clause. Trump, in a Truth Social post, called the New York Times a "serious threat to the National Security of our Nation." The Justice Department sued Washington, DC over its AR-15 ban, while Senate Democratic leader Chuck Schumer announced plans for a resolution to authorize Senate legal action compelling full release of the Epstein files. Reporting also emerged that the FBI, under Director Kash Patel, had deprioritized investigations into far-right extremist groups, including the neo-Nazi group known as the Base. And organizers announced a "Free America Walkout" planned for January 20, signaling that civic pushback was already being organized for the new year.

Wednesday brought the week's judicial thread to a formal conclusion on one front: the Supreme Court denied the administration's request for a stay in Trump v. Illinois, finalizing the block on National Guard deployment, with Justices Alito, Thomas, and Gorsuch dissenting. Elsewhere, the Freedom of the Press Foundation reported 170 assaults on journalists in 2025, nearly matching the combined total of the previous three years, with most occurring at protests against immigration policy. The CBS story reached something like its own resolution as well: the network formally postponed the deportation segment, citing a need for more reporting, even as it continued to circulate online and through its Canadian broadcast. On the Epstein front, the Justice Department disputed a fake letter purportedly from Epstein to Larry Nassar referencing Trump, while releasing a third batch of files that noted Trump's flights on Epstein's plane alongside a disclaimer about unverified claims — a release that managed to be both more detailed and more confusing than what came before.

Christmas Eve added its own texture to the week's developments. A federal judge blocked the administration's attempt to cut more than $230 million in homeland security funding to states including Connecticut, New York, and Massachusetts over noncompliance with immigration enforcement demands, calling the move an abuse of federal grant administration. Rep. Beatty formally filed suit over the Kennedy Center renaming. The White House assumed direct control of the Justice Department's social media accounts, producing unprofessional exchanges with reporters and raising questions about the department's independence from political direction. And on the holiday itself, the official White House account posted a meme urging migrants to "self-deport before Christmas," amplified by ICE, while separate reporting revealed plans to detain up to 80,000 immigrants in warehouse-style facilities that ICE's acting director compared to an "Amazon Prime" system for people. The Epstein disclosure process, meanwhile, took its most significant turn yet: the Justice Department acknowledged the existence of roughly 1.2 million additional Epstein-related documents that had never been reviewed, saying processing would take weeks — a stunning addendum to a release that had already missed its legal deadline. The Interior Department, separately, suspended five offshore wind projects citing national security concerns tied to the "Department of War," an agency that has not existed since 1947, after a federal judge had already ruled a similar suspension "arbitrary and capricious."

Christmas Day and the day after closed the week without resolving either of its central tensions. Trump amplified ICE's own social media posts celebrating Christmas Day deportations, called for the prosecution of former President Obama without evidence, and promoted a video pushing the unfounded claim that COVID-19 had been used to steal the 2020 election. The House passed a bill restoring collective bargaining rights for more than a million federal employees, reversing earlier executive orders that had stripped those rights, with the AFL-CIO planning a Senate push in January. The Justice Department formally confirmed the 1.2 million-document Epstein backlog first disclosed the day before. A federal judge ruled that the DOJ had unlawfully seized attorney-client communications during the failed prosecution of James Comey and ordered their return; the department missed its own deadline to comply and asked for more time, citing staffing shortages. And despite the week's repeated judicial rebukes, the administration continued dismantling environmental regulations and climate information systems, touted large-scale federal job cuts, and kept National Guard troops deployed to cities for immigration enforcement.

Taken as a whole, the week showed an executive branch moving on largely undeterred by the specific losses it absorbed in court. The Supreme Court's two rulings against National Guard deployment, the funding-cut injunction, the Comey ruling, and the holiday-timed block on ICE re-detention all reinforced that judicial review remains an active constraint on executive power — courts intervened, and in each instance the administration's action was paused or reversed. Yet none of these rulings altered the underlying direction of policy: enforcement expanded, detention numbers grew, and rhetoric toward migrants sharpened even as legal challenges mounted around it. The Epstein files saga, for its part, illustrated a different kind of institutional strain — not a single decisive confrontation but a slow accumulation of missed deadlines, vanished documents, and belated admissions that left Congress's own transparency mandate unresolved as the year wound down. Between a legislature gone home for the holidays, a press facing both official hostility and a documented rise in physical danger, and a judiciary issuing rulings that the executive branch absorbed without changing course, the week left open the same question it began with: whether the country's institutional checks are holding, or merely recording each infringement after the fact.
```
